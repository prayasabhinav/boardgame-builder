abstract | The abstract theme gives the game no story or setting, just shapes, colours and moves, so the rules are the whole experience.
historical | The historical theme sets the game in a real time and place from the past, so the moves can stand for real events.
fantasy | The fantasy theme brings dragons, magic or quests, so the same moves can feel like an adventure.
science-fiction | The science-fiction theme sets the game in space or the future, so the same moves can feel like exploring or inventing.
everyday-life | The everyday-life theme uses places players already know, like a market or a school, so the rules are easier to picture.
absurd | The absurd theme makes the setting silly on purpose, so players can laugh at their own moves.
educational | The educational theme ties the game to something to learn, so playing also teaches a fact or a skill.
real-world-issue | The real-world-issue theme connects the game to a problem people face today, so the moves can start a conversation.
poetic | The poetic theme makes the game short and a little strange, so the moves ask players to notice something rather than to win it.
ritual-or-sacred | The ritual-or-sacred theme turns the board into a path towards something holy, so each move can feel like a step in a practice.
mortality-and-loss | The mortality-and-loss theme ties the game to time running out or someone being lost, so the moves can carry weight beyond the table.
war-and-conquest | The war-and-conquest theme sets the game among armies and borders, so the moves can stand for taking and holding ground.
fun-competition | The game is aimed at competition (agon). The pleasure of testing your ability against a rival under rules built to make the contest fair, so that whoever wins has clearly shown superiority in one quality such as speed, memory or ingenuity. (Roger Caillois; Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi; Macklin and Sharp)
fun-chance | The game is aimed at chance (alea). The pleasure of leaving the result to luck: waiting, hoping and risking a stake, with no skill able to change the outcome. In the milder sense Macklin and Sharp give, the pleasure of planning around unpredictable events. (Roger Caillois; Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi; Macklin and Sharp)
fun-make-believe | The game is aimed at make believe (mimicry). The pleasure of accepting a pretended world, or of behaving as someone other than yourself, so that you feel more than your ordinary self through fantasy, disguise or pretence. (Roger Caillois; Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi; Raph Koster)
fun-vertigo | The game is aimed at vertigo (ilinx). The pleasure of deliberately unsettling your balance or perception, a short voluptuous panic in an otherwise clear mind, through spinning, falling, speed or other dizziness. (Roger Caillois; Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi; McKenzie Wark)
fun-exuberance | The game is aimed at exuberance (paidia). The unplanned pleasure of free, noisy, improvised play with no purpose but itself, such as a kitten with wool or a baby with a rattle, tending to run to excess. Boyd names the same ground as spontaneity (acting freely as a whole person) plus exhilaration (a lift that is more than mere excitement). (Roger Caillois; Neva Boyd)
fun-challenge | The game is aimed at challenge (ludus). The pleasure of working against a difficulty that has been set up for its own sake, an obstacle and not an opponent, and of reaching a satisfactory solution for no other reward. (Roger Caillois; Elias; Raph Koster)
fun-skill-mastery | The game is aimed at skill mastery. The satisfaction of developing and using skill, whether quick bodily skill (timing, precision) or mental skill (planning, analysis), and of playing well in itself. (Macklin and Sharp; Elias)
fun-learning | The game is aimed at learning. The pleasure of absorbing a pattern, understanding something new or seeing yourself improve, which the brain rewards. Koster restricts the word fun to this; Burgun and Elias describe the same pleasure as mastery or self-improvement over many plays. (Raph Koster; Keith Burgun; Elias)
fun-flow | The game is aimed at flow. Complete absorption in an activity in which the challenge meets your skill, done for its own sake, with self-consciousness and sense of time dropping away. (Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi; Raph Koster; Jane McGonigal)
fun-hard-fun | The game is aimed at hard fun. Enjoyable hard work: choosing a demanding task so that the stress you feel is energising and optimistic, not fearful, and leaves you stronger than you started. (Jane McGonigal; Raph Koster)
fun-easy-fun | The game is aimed at easy fun. Relaxing, low-effort enjoyment such as passive entertainment. McGonigal argues it leaves people feeling lower than hard fun does; Csikszentmihalyi's 'pleasure' is the same ground, contentment from needs being met, which restores order but does not make you grow. (Jane McGonigal; Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi; Raph Koster)
fun-triumph | The game is aimed at triumph (fiero). The surge of triumph after overcoming a real difficulty, often shown with arms thrown up. The harder the obstacle, the stronger it is. Elias lists the plain pleasure of winning as a reward. (Jane McGonigal; Raph Koster; Elias)
fun-hope-of-success | The game is aimed at hope of success. The feeling of confidence that you can succeed and are improving, and the charged moment of hope just before success when you try hardest. (Jane McGonigal)
fun-vicarious-pride | The game is aimed at vicarious pride (naches). The bursting pride of seeing someone you taught or helped succeed, for example coaching a friend through a game you have mastered. Koster adds kvell, the feeling of bragging about someone you mentor. (Jane McGonigal; Raph Koster)
fun-happy-embarrassment | The game is aimed at happy embarrassment. Playful teasing and trash-talking that stings a little and, because it is plainly not meant to hurt, confirms trust and warms the relationship. (Jane McGonigal)
fun-gloating | The game is aimed at gloating (schadenfreude). The pleasure of seeing a rival fail, in effect a put-down. (Raph Koster)
fun-cooperation | The game is aimed at cooperation. The pleasure of working with others towards a shared goal, in step with them and doing what none of you could alone, like a pass that leads to a goal. (Macklin and Sharp)
fun-sociability | The game is aimed at sociability. The pleasure of the company of other players: talk, give and take, belonging and the bonds that form around a game, before and after as well as during it. (Elias; Jane McGonigal; Germaine and Wake; Neva Boyd)
fun-playing-well-together | The game is aimed at playing well together. Satisfaction in how a group plays, judged by the manner of playing together, not by who wins; choosing and changing games to suit what all are willing to play, and competing as a loving challenge to each other. (Bernie DeKoven)
fun-negotiation | The game is aimed at negotiation. The pleasure of social give-and-take in a game: persuading, forming and breaking alliances and bargaining. (Elias)
fun-status | The game is aimed at status. The satisfaction of standing, ranking or being seen as skilled within a group, including records of who won and by how much. (Raph Koster; Elias)
fun-delight | The game is aimed at delight. The brief pleasure of recognising a pattern and being surprised by it, such as the end of a mystery when everything falls into place. Elias uses aesthetic enjoyment for the look of the game, its graphics and well-made pieces. (Raph Koster; Elias; Johan Huizinga)
fun-elegant-action | The game is aimed at elegant action. The pleasure of acting, deciding or solving well: the elegance of a clean solution or a perfectly dodged obstacle, and the fit between your abilities and the task. (C. Thi Nguyen)
fun-touch | The game is aimed at touch. The pleasure of handling the components: the weight of chess pieces, the feel of shuffling cards, rolling dice, punching out cardboard, a well-carved game piece. (Germaine and Wake; Elias; Raph Koster)
fun-excitement | The game is aimed at excitement. The bodily charge of fast, physical or risky play, such as an adrenaline rush, or the physical reactions of mastering a bodily problem. (Elias; Raph Koster)
fun-escape | The game is aimed at escape. The relief of stepping out of everyday life and its cares for a time. (Elias; Germaine and Wake)
fun-discovery | The game is aimed at discovery. The pleasure of exploring a world or a space of possibilities, finding out how it works and what is in it, and of the sense of discovering something new. (Macklin and Sharp; Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi; Jane McGonigal; Raph Koster)
fun-epic-meaning | The game is aimed at epic meaning. The satisfaction of belonging to, and contributing to, something larger and longer-lasting than yourself, felt with curiosity, awe and wonder at large scales. (Jane McGonigal)
fun-story | The game is aimed at story. The pleasure of a story unfolding through your actions, whether you take on a character in a loose rule-set or move through a designed narrative. (Macklin and Sharp)
fun-performance | The game is aimed at performance. The pleasure of acting, improvising and putting on a show, often as much fun to watch as to play, such as charades or a physical dance of bodies. (Macklin and Sharp)
fun-expression | The game is aimed at expression. The pleasure of expressing a feeling or idea through a game, as the maker or as the player. (Macklin and Sharp; Neva Boyd; Raph Koster)
fun-simulation | The game is aimed at simulation. The pleasure of playing inside a model of a real system, for example as mayor or tycoon, and seeing its workings and the point of view it takes. (Macklin and Sharp)
fun-tension | The game is aimed at tension. The strain of an undecided outcome: wanting something to succeed or resolve and striving to decide it, strongest in contests and gambling. (Johan Huizinga)
fun-rapture | The game is aimed at rapture. Complete absorption and devotion in play that passes into elation and abolishes the sense of 'only' playing; frivolity and ecstasy are the poles between which play moves. (Johan Huizinga)
fun-whimsy | The game is aimed at whimsy. The pleasure of silly actions, unexpected results and absurdity, a sense of euphoria that has to be felt to be understood; for DeKoven also a shared joke, savoured between players. (Macklin and Sharp; Bernie DeKoven)
fun-fun-failure | The game is aimed at fun failure. The enjoyment of failing in an entertaining way, when failure shows your agency, makes you laugh and keeps hope and learning going. (Jane McGonigal)
fun-blissful-productivity | The game is aimed at blissful productivity. The pleasure of being immersed in clear, demanding work that gives quick, visible results. (Jane McGonigal)
fun-value-clarity | The game is aimed at value clarity. The satisfaction of goals that are clear, comparable and rankable, offering relief from the muddle of real-life values. (C. Thi Nguyen)
fun-striving | The game is aimed at striving. Taking up a goal only for the sake of the struggle to reach it, so that the activity is the point and the winning is a device; the reverse of ordinary practical life. (C. Thi Nguyen)
fun-mirth | The game is aimed at mirth. Plain gladness in play, tending to break out in laughter. (Neva Boyd; Johan Huizinga)
fun-subversion | The game is aimed at subversion. Playing against the expected script: acting out the forbidden or unexpected, redressing or reskinning, or rewriting the rules, in order to question conventions. (Mary Flanagan)
fun-creative-playing | The game is aimed at creative playing. The excitement of play that comes from the precarious meeting of inner reality and the real, shared world, Winnicott's account of children's playing. (D. W. Winnicott)
