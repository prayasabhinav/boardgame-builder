## Proposed block: trump suit
- Slot: core-mechanic
- Id: trump-suit
- Why no block covers it: Trick-taking says the highest card wins, and it misses the rule that one suit is named to beat every other suit for that hand.
- Games in this book that use it: Division Loo, with a trump turned up from the deck (pp. 79–81); Ecarte, where the turned-up card sets trumps (pp. 83–84); Euchre, where players order up or name the trump suit and the two Jacks of that colour become the highest cards (pp. 92–94); Boston, where the bidder names trumps (pp. 54–55).
- ? text: One suit is named for the hand, and every card in it beats every card in the other suits. A low trump can win a trick against a high card of another suit.

## Proposed block: calling a contract
- Slot: core-mechanic
- Id: calling-a-contract
- Why no block covers it: Bidding is offering money or things to win an item, and it misses a call that promises how much you will win, so that failing costs you.
- Games in this book that use it: Boston, where each bid names a number of tricks and pays more the higher it is (pp. 54–56); Calabrasella, where one player says "I play" and takes on the other two (pp. 56–58); Euchre, where ordering up or naming trumps commits the side to three tricks or a two-point loss (pp. 92–94); Ecarte, where playing without asking for cards risks two points to the other player (pp. 83–84); Division Loo, where a player who plays and wins nothing pays into the next pool (pp. 79–81).
- ? text: Before the play starts, you say what you are going to do, such as win three tricks. If you manage it you score; if you fail the other side scores or you pay.

## Proposed block: swapping cards for new ones
- Slot: core-mechanic
- Id: swapping-cards
- Why no block covers it: Hand management is choosing which card to play, and it misses the step of trading cards away for new ones from the table or a stock before play.
- Games in this book that use it: Commerce, swapping with cards laid face up in the middle (pp. 64–67); Calabrasella, swapping with the stock and asking for a Three (pp. 56–57); Ecarte, where the first player asks to swap and the dealer can accept or refuse (pp. 83–84); Euchre, where the dealer swaps a card for the turned-up trump (p. 93).
- ? text: You may put cards back and take new ones from the table or a pile, to make your hand stronger before it is played. Sometimes another player has to agree.
