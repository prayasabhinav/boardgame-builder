## Proposed block: must capture when you can
- Slot: core-mechanic
- Id: forced-capture
- Why no block covers it: jumping over pieces (proposed) is about the hop itself; this is the rule that a player with a capture available has to take it, sometimes the biggest one.
- Games in this book that use it, with pages: Anglo-American, International, Russian, Pool, Spanish, Italian and Turkish Checkers, each with a different rule about which capture is compulsory (pp. 170-174); Emergo, where the longest capture must be made and entering is next (p. 178); Hexdame (p. 184); Giveaway Chess (p. 190).
- ? text: If you can capture, you have to. Some games make you take the capture that wins the most pieces. Your opponent can use this to force your move.

## Proposed block: play to lose
- Slot: victory-condition
- Id: play-to-lose
- Why no block covers it: one loser (nearest) ends the game with a single loser; it misses games where the aim is turned upside down, so the player who runs out of pieces or takes the last object is the winner or the loser by an opposite rule.
- Games in this book that use it, with pages: Misère Nim, where taking the last object loses (p. 131); Misère Sprouts, where the last player able to move loses (p. 134); Giveaway Checkers, where the first player to lose all pieces wins (p. 176); Giveaway Chess (pp. 190-191). The book says most Nim-like games can be played in a misère form (pp. 131-133).
- ? text: The aim is turned upside down. In some games the player who gives everything away wins; in others the player who makes the last move loses.

## Proposed block: doubling the stakes
- Slot: core-mechanic
- Id: doubling-the-stakes
- Why no block covers it: bidding (nearest) is an offer for a prize, and betting blocks are made at the start; this is an offer made mid-game to double what the game is worth, which the other player accepts or gives up the game.
- Games in this book that use it, with pages: Backgammon with the doubling cube, where either player may offer to double the stakes before a roll, and may redouble later (pp. 160-161); Acey Deucey, where a throw of 1-2 doubles the stakes (p. 167). The book says the cube suits other race games of chance and skill, such as Parcheesi or Sorry! (p. 160).
- ? text: Part-way through the game you may offer to double what it is worth. The other player either accepts and plays on, or gives up the game and loses the points already at stake.
