## Proposed block: help the player behind
- Slot: core-mechanic
- Id: help-the-player-behind
- Why no block covers it: change the rules (nearest) lets players alter rules, and handicap (proposed from another book) is fixed before play; neither covers a rule that works during the game to bring a trailing player back.
- Games in this book that use it, with pages: Mario Kart, where missiles fire forward and one homes in on first place (p. 109); Chutes & Ladders, where chutes and ladders move players about (p. 106); Warcraft III, where a tax on large armies slows the leader (p. 109); racing games that speed up cars that are behind (p. 109). The book treats these as a general kind of rule, called catch-up features (pp. 106–120).
- ? text: A rule that works while you play and lifts the player who is behind, or slows the player who is ahead, so the game stays close for longer.

## Proposed block: counter cycle
- Slot: core-mechanic
- Id: counter-cycle
- Why no block covers it: call and response (nearest) answers a move with a move, but does not cover three or more choices that each beat one and lose to another, so that no choice is always right.
- Games in this book that use it, with pages: rock-paper-scissors used to settle fights in some live-action role-play games (p. 103); melee, ranged and flying units in strategy games (pp. 103–104); early, middle and late units in card and strategy games (pp. 103–104); rush beats economy beats defence (p. 104); colour A beats B beats C beats A in trading card games (p. 134). The book treats this as a general kind of rule, called a rock-paper-scissors subgame (pp. 103–104, 134).
- ? text: Each choice beats one other choice and loses to a third, like rock, paper and scissors. No choice is always best, so every choice stays worth playing.

## Proposed block: split your forces
- Slot: core-mechanic
- Id: split-your-forces
- Why no block covers it: area control (nearest) is about holding places on a map, but does not cover dividing one fixed amount between several places, in secret, and comparing.
- Games in this book that use it, with pages: the two-player ten-point game with three buckets, won by taking two of three (p. 104); battle in Diplomacy, where forces are spread over areas (p. 104); building a character or starship by sharing out points (p. 104). The book treats this as a general kind of rule, called the resource commitment game (p. 104).
- ? text: You have a fixed amount to share out between several places or jobs. Putting a lot in one means less in another, and the other player is sharing out theirs too.

- Id: staged-game
- Why no block covers it: take turns (nearest) says who acts when, but does not cover a game split into stages so different that each is almost its own game.
- Games in this book that use it, with pages: bridge, with bidding and then play (p. 103); Magic drafting, where you pick your deck and then play with it (p. 103); Skitgubbe, with a trick-taking stage and then a stage of emptying your hand (p. 103).
