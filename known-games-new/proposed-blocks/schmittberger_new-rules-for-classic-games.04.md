## Proposed block: one-use powers
- Slot: core-mechanic
- Id: one-use-powers
- Why no block covers it: Spend to use (proposed) charges a price each time, and it misses a special power each piece holds that can be used once and then is gone.
- Games in this book that use it, with pages: Missile Chess, where a checker under a piece lets it capture without moving, once (p. 201); Teleportation Chess, where a piece may jump to any empty square once (p. 201); Exotic Chess, a family of chess games with one-time powers shown by checkers (p. 202). Re-throw options, a limited number of times a player may throw the dice again, work the same way (p. 229).
- ? text: Each piece or player starts with a special power that works once. When you use it, you take its marker away, so choosing when to use it is the game.

## Proposed block: promotion
- Slot: core-mechanic
- Id: promotion
- Why no block covers it: Grow stronger over time (proposed) is about a character gaining power across a long game, and it misses a piece that changes into a stronger one only on reaching a marked zone of the board.
- Games in this book that use it, with pages: Chu Shogi, where nearly every piece can turn over into a stronger one inside the far four ranks (pp. 208-209, 215); Grand Chess, where a pawn may only become a piece its side has already lost (p. 206); Wildebeest Chess, where a pawn may become only a queen or a wildebeest (p. 207); Capablanca's Chess (p. 204). The book says chess uses promotion to give players a reason to push forward (p. 230).
- ? text: When one of your pieces reaches the far end of the board, it can turn into a stronger piece. This gives you a reason to push forward, even when staying back feels safer.

## Proposed block: the other player has a say
- Slot: core-mechanic
- Id: other-player-has-a-say
- Why no block covers it: Call and response lets each player answer the other's move with a move of their own, and it misses a rule that lets the other player reject or choose the move you are about to make.
- Games in this book that use it, with pages: Refusal Chess, where the opponent may reject a move and the mover has to make a different one (p. 231); Compromise Chess, where the mover proposes two moves and the opponent picks one (p. 231). The book says both ideas fit almost any two-player strategy game.
- ? text: On your turn you offer a move, but the other player has a say. They may turn it down, or choose between two moves you offer.
