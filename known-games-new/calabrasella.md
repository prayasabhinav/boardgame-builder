# Calabrasella — one player against two

- From: Calabrasella, an old card game for three players
- Players: 3 players
- Time: 15–20 min
- Made by: Traditional
- Rules from: The Everything Games Book, pp. 56–60

Three players, and each hand is its own small game. One player decides to play alone. The other two become allies against that player. The lone player may swap cards before play starts.

## What it shows a designer

Most card games with three players have a problem: two can gang up. Here the game builds that into the rules, so the sides are unequal on purpose. The lone player gets extra help (swaps) to make up for being outnumbered.

## What you need

- a deck of 52 cards with all the Eights, Nines and Tens taken out (40 cards left)
- paper and a pen for scores

## How to play

1. **Deal.** Cut for the deal; the lowest card deals. Give each player 12 cards. Put the last 4 cards face down in the middle. This is the stock.
2. **Rank of cards.** From lowest to highest in play: Three, Two, Ace, King, Queen, Jack, Seven, Six, Five, Four. This is not the order of their worth in points.
3. **Declare.** Starting left of the dealer, each player says "I play" or "I pass". If all three pass, throw the hands in and the next player deals. The first to say "I play" is the lone player; the other two are allies.
4. **Ask for a Three.** The lone player may name a suit and ask for its Three. Whoever holds it hands it over and takes any one card from the lone player's hand. If it is in the stock, nobody gives anything. A player holding all four Threes may ask for a Two.
5. **Swap with the stock.** The lone player puts 1 to 4 cards face down, then turns the stock face up and takes the same number from it. The cards left over and the cards put down make a pile called the discard. It goes to whoever wins the last trick.
6. **Play tricks.** The player left of the dealer leads, even if that player is an ally. Each player plays one card. You must follow the suit led if you can. There are no trumps. The highest card of the suit led wins the trick, and the winner leads next. Twelve tricks are played.
7. **Count.** The lone player and the two allies each keep a face-down pile of their tricks. Count points in each pile: each Ace is worth 3; each Three, Two, King, Queen and Jack is worth 1; the last trick is worth 3 more. The discard pile goes with the winner of the last trick.
8. **Score.** Find the difference between the two sides. If the lone player has 20 and the allies 15, the lone player wins 5 from each ally. If the allies have more, the lone player pays 5 to each ally.

## After playing, talk about

- Which role was easier to play, the lone player or an ally, and why?
- The Ace is worth the most points but does not win the most tricks. How did that change what you kept back?
- Why might the last trick be worth so much?
