term: Cross and circle game | A race board made of a circle of marks with a cross of paths through it, like the Korean game Nyout. A piece landing on a cardinal mark can take a short cut along an arm of the cross. | p. 1
term: Dice sticks | Flat sticks, round on one side and flat on the other, thrown in place of dice. The number of flat sides facing up gives the score, as in Zohn Ahl and Nyout. | p. 4
term: Castle | A marked square where a piece cannot be captured. In Pachisi a castle holding one player's piece is open to his partner and closed to the enemy. | p. 8
term: Cowrie shells | Small sea shells with a slit mouth, thrown as dice. The number of mouths facing up gives the score, as in Pachisi and Thaayam. | p. 9
term: Partners | Two players on the same side who win or lose together, as when players sitting opposite each other in Pachisi share a result. | p. 10
term: Extra turn | A right to throw again, given for a high throw, a capture or landing on a marked point. It lets a player's luck run on within one turn. | p. 10
term: Exact throw | A throw that matches the number of spaces needed to bring a piece home or off the board. Anything higher is not allowed, so a piece may wait several turns. | p. 9
term: Capture by landing | Removing an enemy piece by moving a piece onto the space it stands on. The captured piece goes back to the start or off the board. | p. 10
term: Spiral race game | A race game whose track winds in a spiral towards a centre, such as the Hyena game or the Royal Game of Goose. | p. 12
term: Doubling up | Placing two pieces of one colour on one space. In some games this makes them a unit that is safe or can only be attacked by an equal or larger group. | p. 10
term: Safe square | A square on which a piece cannot be captured. Thaayam calls its safe squares havens, as a guest is safe in the host's house. | p. 19
term: Twin | In Thaayam, two pieces of one player placed together on a square. A twin moves at half the thrown distance and can only be hit by another twin. | p. 19
term: Entering a piece | Bringing a piece from off the board onto the track. Often it needs a particular throw, such as a 1 in Thaayam or a 5 in the Sumerian game. | p. 18
term: Bearing off | Taking a piece off the board once it has come to the end of the track. Backgammon allows it only when all of a player's pieces are in the home table. | p. 44
term: Forfeit | A payment of a counter or other token made when a player lands on a penalty space or cannot move. | p. 8
term: Pool | A common store of stakes or forfeits, which the winner takes at the end of the game. | p. 24
term: Penalty space | A space on the track that costs the player who lands on it a turn, a payment or a move back. Bell suggests that the marked squares of Patolli were of this kind. | p. 7
term: Snake and ladder line | A line drawn between two points of a track so that a piece landing on one end moves to the other, moving forward or back. Bell finds this in the Egyptian Palm Tree game. | p. 21
term: Long die | A four-sided die, shaped like a long stick, used in Egyptian games. | p. vii
term: Point | In backgammon and tabula, one of the twenty-four triangular spaces on which pieces rest. | p. 43
term: Blot | In backgammon, a single piece alone on a point. It can be hit by an enemy piece moving onto the point. | p. 45
term: Making a point | Putting two or more pieces on one point so that the opponent cannot land there. | p. 45
term: The bar | The strip between the inner and outer tables of a backgammon board, where hit pieces wait until they can re-enter. | p. 43
term: Inner table | The home quarter of a backgammon board, where a player gathers pieces before bearing them off. | p. 43
term: Gammon | A double win in backgammon, scored when the loser has borne off no piece. A backgammon is a triple win, when the loser also has a piece on the bar or in the winner's inner table. | p. 46
term: Doubling cube | The backgammon device by which a player offers to double the stake in mid-game. The opponent either accepts and plays on at the higher stake, or refuses and loses the game. | p. 46
term: Automatic doubling | A rule that a tie on the opening throw doubles the stake, or adds one to it. | p. 46
term: Fritillus | A wooden dice box or tower with an inside track, used in Roman times so that dice could not be palmed or switched. | p. 28
term: Cheating prevention by device | A piece of equipment that stops a player from altering a throw, such as the straw ring for Nyout dice or the Roman dice tower. | p. 2
term: Tables | The medieval name for the family of backgammon-like games. By the sixteenth century it meant any game played on a flat board. | p. 43
term: Blocked piece | A piece that cannot move because the points ahead hold enemy pairs. In Tabula such a piece was incitus. | p. 35
idea: Many games are the same board with changes | Bell finds that Nyout, Patolli and Pachisi are one idea altered. Take the circle away and you have Patolli, take the cross away and you have the Kiowa game Zohn Ahl, fold the circle into the cross and you have Pachisi. A designer can treat a board as a part that is kept while rules and pieces are changed. | p. 4
idea: A board tells you less than a rule book | Boards were reused for other games. Chess boards were used for draughts, and the fox and geese board for solitaire. Bell warns that because one people used a board for a game, we cannot know the next people did the same. A designer can see that board shape and rules are separate choices. | p. v
idea: Games that lose their rules still show their shape | Patolli's rules were lost when priests destroyed Aztec records, but its board, five beans and twelve stones survive. Bell reconstructs it by comparison with related games. When rules are missing, the parts and how they are scored point to likely rules. | p. 7
idea: One mechanism, many wrappers | Cross-and-circle games turn up in Korea, in Mayan stucco at Chichen Itza and in India. Bell uses this to argue for old links between peoples, or for the same idea arriving twice. A mechanism can be made again in different materials and meanings. | p. 3
idea: Games change as they cross borders | Pachisi became Ludo in England around 1896, with a cubic die in place of cowries. The game kept the race and lost the royal court. Here a game that travelled kept its core rule and gave up its local objects. | p. 12
idea: A theme can be added to a race | The Hyena game turns a race along a spiral into a family story: mothers walk to a well, a hyena is released, and the winner becomes the hyena. The track and dice are simple, and the story gives the moves meaning. | p. 12
idea: Stories on the track | The Royal Game of Goose has numbered spaces with named hazards: an inn, a well, a maze, a prison, death. Landing on one means paying, waiting or going back. The players' only choice is whether to play, so the track decides everything else. | p. 15
idea: A form that teaches | After Goose, makers produced spiral games to teach children history, geography, botany and astronomy. The same track became a carrier for lessons. | p. 16
idea: Luck is part of the structure | In Goose a throw of the two dice decides every move, and no choices are left to the players. In Pachisi and Thaayam, a player can split a throw between pieces, or refuse to move, and so gains some control over luck. | p. 10
idea: Safe spaces change how risk feels | Pachisi castles and Thaayam havens are squares where capture does not happen. The meaning attached to them differs: Thaayam explains them as the sanctity of a guest under a host's roof. A designer can treat a safe square as a rule and as a story. | p. 19
idea: Partnership changes tactics | In Pachisi, partners win or lose together, so a player sometimes holds back pieces to block enemies or to guard a partner who is behind. A partner who rushes home leaves the other facing two opposing turns for each of his. | p. 10
idea: Capture can be a tool of the race | In race games a hit piece loses ground and the hitter often gets another turn. Thaayam adds that hitting gives an extra turn, and a twin is protected from single pieces. The same capture rule can work against the pace of a race or add to it. | p. 19
idea: Exact finishing prolongs the end | Pachisi, Backgammon and Thaayam need an exact throw or a full home table before pieces leave. This keeps a player waiting after his pieces have covered the rest of the track. | p. 9
idea: The oldest known boards had no recorded rules | Boards from the royal tombs of Ur, about 3000 B.C., are the oldest in existence, yet no account of play survived. Bell offers numbered suggested rules, and marks them as suggestions. A designer can learn to separate what a source shows from what is added to make it playable. | p. 24
idea: Old games turn into new ones by small changes | Cribbage, invented in the seventeenth century, may have used the old board design from Thebes, a pegged board known over 3,500 years earlier. Bell's point is that a score board is a lasting object. | p. 22
idea: A new game can take over an old name | The Roman game of twelve lines was replaced by a thirty-six space game from Egypt or Greece, which took the old name. Bell compares this to Sphairistike becoming Lawn Tennis and then simply Tennis. The name stays and the game changes. | p. 30
idea: A rule can hurt the player who has to follow it | In Zeno's game of Tabula, the Emperor had to use his whole throw if he could, and that forced him to break three pairs and leave eight single pieces. A rule that makes a player use every number can ruin an otherwise sound position. | p. 34
idea: Dice with no choice are sometimes the whole game | In Chasing the Girls only throws of 6, 1 and doublets count; all other throws score nothing. A designer can make most throws do nothing and let a few carry the game. | p. 37
idea: A losing player can change role | In Chasing the Girls, a player with one piece left becomes a corner-rattler, moving only between corner points and capturing only on corners. The change of rules for the last piece keeps a trailing player in the game. | p. 38
idea: Drinking and gambling shaped rules | The seventeenth-century Sixe-Ace variant made a player drink on a throw of 2, and the last players on the board paid for the drinks. Stakes, pools and forfeits are parts of many rule sets. | p. 40
idea: A name can cover many versions | By the sixteenth century Tables meant any game on a flat surface. Backgammon came back in the seventeenth century as a new variant and was known as gammon in Scotland, tric-trac in France and puff in Germany. | p. 43
idea: Games rise and fall in favour | Bell notes that chess was the popular game by 1475, backgammon revived in the seventeenth century, was a craze among London writers before 1939, and by 1960 was in eclipse outside the Middle East. A game's rules stay while its audience moves. | p. 43
idea: Technical words can put people off | Bell blames part of backgammon's decline on its many terms, and so reduces them to a minimum in his account. A game with a long vocabulary asks more of a new player. | p. 43
term: Short leap | A capture made by jumping over an enemy piece on the next space and landing on the space just beyond it. | p. vii
term: Long leap | A jump over an enemy piece to land beyond it, where empty spaces may lie on either side of the captured piece. | p. vii
term: En prise | Said of a piece that can be captured by the opponent on the next move. | p. vii
term: Huff | Taking away a piece from a player who did not make a capture he was able to make. In Alquerque a piece that could capture and did not was huffed. | p. 48
term: Compulsory capture | A rule that a player has to take a piece when one is on offer. Fighting Serpents and Alquerque both use it. | p. 50
term: Multiple capture | Capturing more than one piece in one move by a chain of jumps, with a change of direction allowed. | p. 48
term: Custodian capture | Capturing an enemy piece by trapping it between two of your own pieces. The Zuni Stone Warriors game uses it. | p. 49
term: Orthogonal move | A move along a rank or file, in a straight line along the squares. | p. vii
term: Diagonal move | A move across the corners of squares, along a diagonal of the board. | p. vii
term: Replacement capture | A capture made by moving onto the square held by an enemy piece and taking it off the board. | p. vii
term: Promotion | A change of a piece into a piece with wider moves, usually on reaching the far side of the board. In Shatranj a pawn became a prime minister. | p. 59
term: Privileged pawn | In Shaturanga, a pawn belonging to a player who had only a ship and the pawn left. It could be promoted to any piece he chose. | p. 56
term: Throne | In Shaturanga, a marked square where a rajah starts. A rajah who lands on an enemy throne wins a stake. | p. 54
term: Ally | A partner player on the same side. In four-handed Shaturanga a rajah who takes an ally's throne controls the ally's pieces as well. | p. 54
term: Concourse of shipping | In Shaturanga, three ships on adjacent squares joined by a fourth, which lets the player capture two enemy ships. | p. 55
term: Check | A threat to capture the king on the next move. | p. 60
term: Checkmate | A position in which the king is in check and cannot escape, so the game is lost. | p. 60
term: Stalemate | A position where a player has no legal move. In Shatranj a stalemated king could exchange places with another piece, and otherwise the player lost. | p. 60
term: Bare king | In Shatranj, the position in which a player has lost all pieces except the king. Capturing all the rest of the opponent's pieces won the game. | p. 60
term: Perpetual check | A repeated sequence of checks that cannot be stopped; Shatranj counted it as a draw. | p. 60
term: Battle array | In Shatranj, the opening positions reached when each player made ten or twelve moves at once to set up his forces. Bell gives the name Ta'biyat. | p. 60
term: Long die | A four-sided die marked 2, 3, 4 and 5, used in Shaturanga to decide which piece moves. | p. 53
idea: One game tradition is many games in sequence | Bell traces Alquerque from temple roof slabs in Kurna, about 1400 B.C., to the Arabic Quirkat of A.D. 976 and the Spanish Alquerque of the thirteenth century. The Spaniards carried it to New Mexico, where the Zuni made a quadruple board into a new game, the Stone Warriors. A game keeps its board and gets new rules at each stop. | p. 47
idea: Rules are often missing from old sources | The Alfonso manuscript gives Alquerque's moves but Bell says they are not enough to play a game. He adds numbered suggestions: no backward moves, no returning to a point, and a scoring rule for the end. He marks the added rules as his own. | p. 48
idea: Unfinished boards show what the maker was cutting | One Alquerque board in the roof of the Kurna temple was left half cut after a mistake with a diagonal line. This shows that diagonal lines were part of what makers had to draw. | p. 47
idea: Compulsory capture keeps the board moving | In Fighting Serpents the opening piece is captured by a short leap at once, and taking is compulsory. This opens the crowded board and makes each early move forced. | p. 50
idea: Replacing a lost piece with a special one | In Stone Warriors the first piece a player loses is replaced by a Priest of the Bow, which moves in more directions. Loss gives the player a piece with wider moves. | p. 49
idea: Shaturanga was a battle of four armies | Shaturanga used four armies of infantry, cavalry, elephants and boatmen, one for each player, with allies sitting opposite. It was played on the Ashtapada board, which had earlier been used for a race game. A board for a race was turned into a board for war. | p. 51
idea: Dice chose which piece could move | In Shaturanga a long die marked 2, 3, 4, 5 told the player which piece to move: ship, horse, elephant, rajah or pawn. If the piece could not move, the turn was lost. A game of war was joined to a throw of chance. | p. 53
idea: Chance and skill together make a stake game | Shaturanga paid stakes for seizing a throne, capturing a rajah, and building an empire by capturing both enemy rajahs. These payments gave the allies more to play for than one win. | p. 54
idea: An old account had to be filled in | Bell prints his additions to the Shaturanga rules in italics where the ancient account was missing a rule, such as the stake pool and the turn order. This marks which rules are from the source and which are added. | p. 54
idea: A ban on gambling reshaped a game | Hindu law punished anyone who played at dice or at Shaturanga for stakes. Players kept the game and dropped the die. Bell suggests that removing luck led to the other changes. | p. 56
idea: Four players became two | After the die was dropped, the allied forces were merged into one army, and the game for four players became one for two. Bell says this is why chess has two of several pieces. The doubled pieces in modern chess are left from the old allied armies. | p. 56
idea: Pieces change power when a game changes | When Shaturanga became Shatranj the allied kings became prime ministers whose move was halved, and the ship and elephant swapped moves. The names stayed the same while the powers changed. | p. 56
idea: A game travels with people who have a reason to move | Bell lists three routes by which Shatranj reached Europe: through Spain with the Moors, through the Byzantine court by marriage and gift, and through the Crusades. Each route was a meeting of courts, soldiers or doctors. | p. 62
idea: A game can arrive with a story | Firdausi's poem tells how an Indian ambassador brought a board to the Persian court of Chosroes I and challenged the court to solve its mysteries. A wise man solved it in a day and a night. The tale explains how chess came to Persia. | p. 57
idea: A game carries its time | Bell notes that the European medieval form of chess in Caxton's book of 1474 was little different from the Persian Shatranj of the Crusades. Rules held for four centuries. | p. 60
idea: Many ways to win make a draw likelier | Shatranj had three ways to win: checkmate, taking all other pieces, and trapping a stalemated king. Even so, drawn games were common, since some pieces had narrow powers and a larger force could not always beat a smaller one placed on favourable squares. | p. 60
idea: Players change a slow opening | Shatranj's opening was slow, since several pieces moved only a little. Players agreed that the first player could make ten moves at once to set up, and then the second player did the same. This is a house rule that became a custom. | p. 60

term: Trap square | In the Jungle game, a square beside a den. An enemy animal on it loses its power and can be captured by any defender. | p. 70
term: Den | The goal square of each player in the Jungle game. Entering the enemy's den wins. | p. 70

term: Leopard | In Cows and Leopards, one of the two pieces that kill cows by jumping over them. | p. 81

idea: War games can be race boards under another rule | Bell notes that the Ashtapada board was a race board before Shaturanga, that the Tab board of nine by four resembles the ten by three race board of ancient Egypt, and that the chequered chess board served draughts. He treats these as cases of a race board adapted for war. | p. 87
idea: A new game can be built from two old ones | Draughts was made in the south of France about A.D. 1100 by joining the chess board with the Alquerque moves. Pieces moved as the queen then did, one square on the diagonal, and captured as in Alquerque. The new game took parts from each. | p. 71
idea: Compulsion of capture is a choice a rule makes | Early draughts had no compulsion, a survival of chess. When France added compulsion about 1535, the old game was called Plaisant and the new one jeu forcé. English draughts descends from the forced game. Whether capture is required changes how a game plays. | p. 71
idea: Penalties for missing a capture are a design | English draughts lets the opponent return the move, let it stand and demand the capture next turn, or huff the offending piece. Each is a different cost for the same mistake. | p. 72
idea: Reversing the goal gives a new game | In the Losing Game, played with draughts pieces, each player tries to force the opponent to capture his pieces, and the first to lose all of them wins. The board and moves stay and the aim turns round. | p. 72
idea: Rules vary for the same pieces | Italian draughts forbids a man to take a king and forces the larger capture. Turkish draughts has men that move forwards and sideways on an unchequered board. Polish draughts allows men to capture backwards. All use the same basic idea of the jump. | p. 73
idea: A game that took off in a city | Polish or Continental draughts was first played in Paris cafes in 1727, after earlier French versions died out within fifty years. Bell writes that, as now played, it ranks among the major board games. A game grows when a meeting place gives it a steady group of players. | p. 75
idea: Unequal forces change what each side wants | In the tafl games, a small force with a special piece tries to break out, and a larger force tries to hem it in. In Fox and Geese one fox faces thirteen geese. Each side has a different goal and different powers. | p. 75
idea: A weighted board needs balance | In Fox and Geese, Bell writes that if the geese are played correctly the fox loses. Later forms raised the geese to seventeen and took away their backward move. A designer can change numbers and moves to adjust a one-sided game. | p. 76
idea: The edge of the board is a goal | In Tablut and Saxon Hnefatafl, one side wins if the king reaches the board's edge, and the other wins by capturing him. The board shape and the king's goal together set what each side does. | p. 78
idea: A call-out makes a threat public | In Tablut a player who opens one route to the edge says Raichi, and two routes, Tuichi. The second call means the king cannot be stopped, like checkmate. A spoken rule keeps both players aware of the threat. | p. 78

idea: Games hide in other uses of the same object | The Gokstad ship burial held a board with Nine Men's Morris on one side and probably hnefatafl on the other. Triple-purpose boxes in Europe from the fourteenth century carried chess, Nine Men's Morris and backgammon. A single board can carry several games. | p. 93
idea: A game need not stay with its first meaning | A Nine Men's Morris design on the steps at Mihintale in Ceylon was used as a charm against evil. The same figure was a board in other places. | p. 93
idea: Fixing the board while the pieces change | A fourteenth-century Italian codex draws Morris pieces as moons, stars, shields, crosses, squares and rounds. Bell reports that all seemed to have the same value and movement, so the shapes may only record the order of play. | p. 94
idea: A game with a forced win needs a balancer | In Three Men's Morris the first player can force a win by correct play, so Bell suggests dice may have decided the advantage of first entry. idea: Two phases make placing matter | In Seega, Dara and Ludus Latrunculorum, players first put stones on the board, then move and capture them. Bell says skilful placing in the first phase may decide the second. The opening phase builds the position that the second phase tests. | p. 82
idea: Draws arise from safe walls | In Seega a player can build a barrier behind which only his own pieces move, and those cannot be attacked. Bell names the frequency of draws as a flaw of the game, so the rules add a points win for the larger number of pieces. | p. 84
idea: Movement and capture can be paired in different ways | High Jump uses the Seega board and moves, but captures by orthogonal jump and does not require capture. The only change from Seega is the capture rule. | p. 84
idea: An old game can survive at the edge of an empire | Bell suggests that Seega and High Jump survive from Roman Ludus Latrunculorum among Egyptian farmers and Somali herders, each keeping one form of its capture. A game may be lost in a centre and kept at the margin. | p. 86
idea: A poem as a rules source | The poem Laus Pisonis describes pieces of glass, blocking, trapping between two pieces, and supported lines. idea: A leader piece can unlock a stalled game | Bell suggests that Latrunculorum had a leader that could jump an enemy and break a blocked position, which would prevent games ending with many pieces left. He notes that no source supports or contradicts this. | p. 85
idea: Unclear rules end in blockade | Bell's suggested Latrunculorum rules end the game if no capture is made in thirty moves. A rule is added to meet a situation the old accounts leave open. | p. 87
idea: Dice can drive a war game | Tablan and Puluc use throwing sticks or maize ears to decide how far pieces run in a fight along a track. The war game combines a race with capture, and chance decides the pace. | p. 87
idea: Capture can carry a prisoner | In Puluc a captured piece is put under the capturing piece and carried backwards along the track. If the stack reaches the end the prisoner is slain, and a stack that is hit changes owners. A captured piece stays on the board and travels. | p. 90
idea: Pieces that are stuck still count | In Tablan a piece that lands on the enemy back row cannot move or be captured, and the winner holds most enemy squares. The pieces become scorers where they stop. | p. 88

idea: Games can be gathered in one box | An antique compendium from western India is painted for Tablan on the top, holds a Pallanguli board inside, has Nine Men's Morris underneath and Cows and Leopards on the lid. Several games share one object. | p. 172
idea: A writer of rules can fix a standard | Edmond Hoyle began teaching whist in 1741 with manuscript notes, then printed them when pirated copies appeared. The Short Treatise gave the laws of the game, a few rules for beginners and calculations of odds. Over three hundred editions followed, and According to Hoyle came to mean the accepted way of playing. | p. 185
idea: A group of players can study a game and write down principles | About 1736 gentlemen at the Crown Coffee House in Bedford Row studied whisk, a tavern game, until it became whist, and set out four rules: play from your longest suit, study your partner's hand as much as your own, do not force your partner unnecessarily, and attend to the score. The game changed class by being studied. | p. 185
idea: A chess master wrote down openings and end games | As-Suli, a champion at the court of Baghdad before A.D. 946, wrote a Book of Chess with ten standard openings, common middle-game problems and a collection of end plays with comments. Bell calls him the first to try to discover the science of the game. | p. 178
idea: Foreign games were collected from visitors | Thomas Hyde, interpreter in oriental languages to the English court under three kings, met envoys from eastern countries and gathered their games without leaving England. His two books on chess and on backgammon-type games came from these conversations. | p. 182
