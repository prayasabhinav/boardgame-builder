term: Sportsmanship | A way of playing that accepts both the written rules and a code of conduct about how to behave, such as winning and losing gracefully. Oriard takes the word from the historian John Dizikes. | p. 16
term: Gamesmanship | A way of playing that accepts the written rules but recognises no code beyond them, so finding a loophole counts as skill. | p. 16
term: Fair play | The shared idea that opponents meet on equal terms and win without cheating. Oriard shows that people in the same culture meant different things by it. | p. 13
term: Loophole | A gap in the wording of a rule that lets a player gain an advantage without breaking the rule. American football coaches in the 1890s looked for them and then new rules were written to close them. | p. 15
term: Scientific football | A name used from the 1880s for football played as a contest of planned tactics and technical inventions rather than plain strength. | p. 13
term: Game butcher | Roosevelt's name for a hunter who killed animals only for hides or numbers, against the hunters' code of killing for meat or for sport. | p. 21
term: Agon | Roger Caillois's word for games of competition, where skill decides the winner. | p. xii
term: Alea | Roger Caillois's word for games of chance, where luck decides the winner. | p. xii
term: Umpire | The person on the field who applies the rules and sends offenders off. Roosevelt pictured government as an umpire, and Oriard asks who gets to be umpire. | p. 18
term: Muscular Christianity | A mid-nineteenth-century belief, brought from England, that team sport builds both the body and moral character. | p. 11
term: Square deal | Roosevelt's phrase for fair dealing with everyone. Oriard notes it points to the poker table, where tricks are part of play, rather than to the gentleman's playing field. | p. 26
term: Work and play | Oriard defines work as action for an outside goal and play as action for its own sake. Work is about the result, play about the process. | p. xii
idea: A game sits between work and play | Oriard sees work and play as the two ends of a line, with games between them. A game is play with effort and rules in it, or work done in a playful way. His example is the Virginian in Wister's novel, who runs a cattle business as a game and so stays a boy at heart while working. | p. xiii
idea: The word game once meant gambling | In colonial America game mostly meant gambling, which was the least accepted pastime. Over the nineteenth century it came to mean a playful contest by agreed rules. Oriard also notes that game could mean a scheme or a trick, as in playing games with someone. | p. xii
idea: Two attitudes to the same rulebook | Sportsmanship follows the rules and a code of manners. Gamesmanship follows only the rules and treats loopholes as clever play. The coach Stagg said British players keep the spirit of the rules and Americans the letter, and that he would still rather lose than win by a trick. | p. 15
idea: Loopholes make rulebooks longer | American football has far more rules than soccer or rugby. Oriard says this is because rule makers kept adding rules to stop players evading the old ones. Each new clever tactic produced a new rule. | p. 15
idea: Referees and long rules show distrust | The economist Veblen read the need for umpires and thick rulebooks as proof that players try to cheat each other. Stagg read the same rules as the setting for fair play. Oriard accepts both readings as true of American sport. | p. 16
idea: A violent game brings in a governing body | College football in the 1890s caused so many injuries that newspapers asked for it to be banned. In 1905 President Roosevelt called the colleges together and the National Collegiate Athletic Association was formed to regulate the rules. | p. 20
idea: Calling a conflict a game can make force look fair | Roosevelt described frontier fights between settlers and Indians as one-to-one contests with equal chances, such as a race to reload a gun. Oriard says this turned conquest into a fair match and hid the size of the forces behind it. | p. 23
idea: A rule is fair only if everyone accepts who made it | The Virginian hangs his friend Steve and says Steve knew the rules and played the game. Oriard asks who made the rules, who gains from them and who acts as umpire. When a rule is imposed on someone who has not accepted it, it works as force. | p. 26
idea: Which game you name shows what you value | Calling something a gentleman's sport suggests fixed values and a stable order. Calling it poker suggests a fluid world where an underdog can win. Oriard shows that the choice of game for a metaphor carries a view of how society works. | p. 16
idea: Some games are won by leaving the game | In the novel the courtship of Molly and the Virginian is a contest in which winning is possible only when both stop playing it. Oriard calls it an anti-game. | p. 6
idea: Poker as skill with a dealt hand | The Virginian says a person with the right nature wins poker with whatever hand is dealt, even a lame horse or an empty gun. Games of cards give a picture of life where luck sets the hand and skill decides how it is played. | p. 3
idea: Players may be shut out of a game | In Wharton's novel Lily Bart cannot play the game of business and speculation. She is expected to play another game, finding a husband, with money at stake in both. Oriard uses this to show that who may play which game can be fixed by rank or sex. | p. 9
idea: Games have losers who started behind | Writers who disliked the game metaphor pointed out that some players have little chance from the start because of sex, race or class. A game with an equal start and a fair field is a claim that has to be checked. | p. 10
idea: Word contests are games too | In The Virginian cowboys compete in wit and in tall tales, a liars' contest, where the winner gains respect and nobody is hurt. The prize is the loyalty of the other men. | p. 4
idea: Competition needs a limit on severity | Roosevelt wrote that some competition is needed, but if it becomes too severe the whole group suffers. He named fair play as the means of controlling it. | p. 24
term: Rough-and-tumble | A frontier fight with almost no limits, in which biting and gouging were common. Visitors took it as a sign of how little restraint the frontier had. | p. 32
term: Shooting at a mark | A frontier contest in which each shooter fires at a target and the most accurate aim wins. It made a game out of a skill needed for hunting and survival. | p. 40
term: Corn husking | A community work event run as a contest, where neighbours race or compete at a chore. Barn raisings and stump clearings were treated the same way. | p. 40
term: Trappers' rendezvous | The yearly gathering of fur trappers, with wrestling, fights and heavy gambling. Writers used it to show how far play went outside towns. | p. 40
term: Undue advantage | A gain from a method that removes the other side's chance, such as firing on an unarmed opponent. In Cooper's novels the hero refuses it even in war. | p. 47
term: Fair play system | A real settlement in western Pennsylvania from 1769 to 1784 where elected fair play men settled disputes by what the community agreed was fair. | p. 46
term: Local champion | The person in a community who wins at every kind of contest, such as Billy Kirby in Cooper's The Pioneers. The type returns in later Westerns. | p. 43
idea: Games grow out of work and danger | Frontier people made contests from what they needed to survive: shooting, racing, wrestling, and community jobs like barn raising. Oriard says these games gave status and turned risky skills into ritual. | p. 40
idea: Rules by custom against rules by statute | In Cooper's turkey shoot a flint misfires and the men argue whether the shooter gets another shot. They agree he is entitled to one, but the sheriff rules it a miss. Oriard says fair play works through custom and agreement, law through written statutes and their interpretation. | p. 44
idea: Three limits a hunter sets for himself | Natty Bumppo's code has three parts: do not waste, kill cleanly, take no undue advantage. The sheriff, by contrast, measures success by the number of birds and fish he brings down. Oriard contrasts measuring by quantity with measuring by quality. | p. 44
idea: Judging the means or the ends | Natty shoots a deer out of season and a panther that threatens a girl with the same care, and sees no difference. The law punishes the first and rewards the second. In Oriard's summary, the law judges ends and the hunter's code judges means. | p. 45
idea: A fair system needs shared agreement on what fair is | The real fair play settlements worked because their members agreed on what was fair. Oriard says the town in The Pioneers had no such agreement across its classes, so a fair play system could not have held there. | p. 46
idea: Sportsmanship needs an outside authority, gamesmanship does not | Oriard says sportsmanship looks to timeless values known through tradition. Gamesmanship looks to no authority outside the game, so its rules justify themselves and onlookers are needed to confirm who won. | p. 37
idea: A contest of equals against a contest of mismatched players | Frontier games assumed equal players, and winners were said to earn their win rather than inherit it. In nineteenth-century love games a man and woman were called partners, but the woman risked more and had more to lose. | p. 38
idea: A game with a hidden contest | Love games said the two players were partners and that marriage was a win for both. Oriard says the word game gave away that the situation was a contest with losers. | p. 38
idea: Refusing an advantage as part of the game | In The Deerslayer Natty will not fire at an enemy who has no loaded gun. He puts down his rifle and offers a fair fight, though the enemy tries to trick him. Oriard shows the hero defined by the advantages he will not use. | p. 47
idea: A contest framed to look equal | Cooper's Indian war scenes show single fights between one white man and one Indian, shaped like sporting contests. Oriard notes that the end of the same novel is a massacre by soldiers and that numbers and force decide it. | p. 48
idea: Play was allowed in its place | Puritans did not ban all play. They objected to play on the Sabbath, play that led to gambling, and play that was immoderate or untimely. Oriard says the question was the time and kind of play, not play itself. | p. 32
idea: Seasonal work gives seasonal play | Southern planters worked hard at planting and harvest and then rested, so visitors saw them as lazy. New England worked at a steady pace. Oriard shows that the rhythm of work shapes when and how much people play. | p. 34
term: Sport (a gambler) | In Western novels the word for a gambler who was also a dandy and a gunman. It was a different meaning from sport as athletic contest. | p. 67
term: Horse swap | A trade of horses done as a public contest of wit, with each side hiding the faults of its animal. | p. 52
term: Shooting match | A contest in which men shoot at a target for a prize, such as a turkey. In Kirkland's writing a version with a live turkey tied to a post is called cruel. | p. 60
term: Rendezvous | The yearly gathering of fur trappers in the mountains, a holiday of drinking, storytelling, brawling and gambling after the hunting season. | p. 57
idea: Cheating can move a game from luck to skill | In the humorists' tales a player who cheats is playing a game where the deal counts for less and the players' wit counts for more. The most typical tales end with the trickster being tricked, as when a gambler gets a deck with all four jacks removed. | p. 53
idea: Outcheating the cheater | A common story had a clever player take advantage of others and then be beaten by someone cleverer. In Longstreet's horse swap the clever trader ends with a horse that is blind and deaf. Oriard shows readers enjoying the game of wits more than any single winner. | p. 52
idea: When everything is a contest | In Southwestern humour horse races, weddings, dances, camp meetings and steamboat trips all become contests. Oriard says this showed a society where rank had to be won by wit and chance rather than inherited. | p. 51
idea: A fair fight can be a joke and a boast | A hunter in one tale fights a panther with bare hands, offers odds on himself and announces his own fight, then says he won with no gouging. Oriard reads the no gouging as a joke that still marks a real rule. | p. 52
idea: Winning a dare can cost the winner | Sut Lovingood wins a dozen marbles by jumping into a house wall as far as he can, and his head is damaged. Oriard uses the story to ask what a win amounts to when the contest has no larger point. | p. 54
idea: Ritual fights give status to both sides | In the tall tales fights follow rules and both winner and loser gain standing, so the game settles disputes in place of law. When the game turns spiteful, as in Sut Lovingood's bets on other people's misery, it stops working. | p. 54
idea: Holiday ends the rivalry of the season | After the hunt the trappers meet and drink, sing, tell lies and wrestle. Their feuds are dropped and fights end in reconciliation. Oriard notes that Irving shows hunting rivalry as sharpening wit and the holiday as healing it. | p. 57
idea: A pause for festival does not cost work | Kirkland complains that her neighbours have no day set aside for gaiety at the end of the harvest. She argues that people would work as much after an interval of gaiety. | p. 59
idea: Play and sport differ in a contest | Women writers of the West separated play, such as dances, picnics and gardens, from sport, which meant hunting, violence and winning. Oriard notes that men's writing was also unsure about this and that the line was not absolute. | p. 60
idea: The same word can mean a fair contest or a scheme | In dime novels game could mean a shooting match under fair play or the plot of a villain. Murder, hunting buffalo and robbery were all called games. Oriard reads this as a sign that readers felt both attitudes toward the same activity. | p. 65
idea: Game words spread from the card table to everyday speech | Dime novels were full of poker idioms such as playing your cards and playing trumps, and heroes named Poker Jack or Ace High. Oriard thinks cheap fiction carried these words from gambling saloons up to respectable speakers, until Roosevelt used them. | p. 67
idea: Fairness may be owed only to an equal | Pat Garrett wrote that he owed a fair fight only to a man of high reputation and equal social standing. A man like the Kid could be shot with every advantage. Oriard shows that fair play depended on who the opponent was. | p. 68
idea: Losing gracefully as a rule of the game | A boys' magazine hero tells his team to play honestly and to admit defeat like a gentleman. They lose to opponents who tamper with cartridges and saddles, and keep their manners. Oriard reads this as a moral win. | p. 64
idea: Law itself can be played as a game | Baldwin described lawyers' contests as struggles for standing with bets placed all round. In these stories a game replaces justice as the way disputes are settled. | p. 49
term: House edge | The percentage by which the dealer or bank is ahead of the players over many rounds. A gambler in Harte's story accepts fate because he knows the usual percentage favours the dealer. | p. 72
term: Fresh deal | A new hand of cards dealt to everyone, so the earlier luck no longer counts. In a story a miners' camp names a baby Luck to give him a fresh deal at life. | p. 72
term: Debt of honour | A bet that has to be paid at once and without argument, because refusing would ruin a gentleman's name. A Southern gentleman said a gambling debt was one, and a debt to a tradesman was not. | p. 84
term: Pot-hunter | A hunter who is only after the kill. The Southern gentleman's code separated him from the true sportsman, for whom the chase mattered more. | p. 84
term: Law (head start) | In fox hunting, the advantage given to the quarry before the chase begins. A fox hunter said the quarry be given considerable law, so the contest would be fair. | p. 85
term: Code duello | The written rules of the duel, which gave the fight a formal order. John Hyde Wilson wrote them down in 1838, and many duels ended without blood. | p. 85
idea: How you play the hand you are dealt | An epitaph in Lewis's Wolfville stories says life is not about which cards you hold but about how you play a low-ranked hand. The characters talk of life in poker terms. Oriard shows the idea that skill and honesty matter more than the deal. | p. 80
idea: You cannot bluff the game itself | A Wolfville gambler says life is stud poker where Destiny holds a hidden ace. You cannot hold six cards, deal twice or bluff Destiny. You can only play the five cards that belong to you, and play honestly. | p. 80
idea: A gambler who follows a code | In Bret Harte's stories the professional gambler is a gamesman by trade and a sportsman by instinct. He accepts fate, pays what he owes and plays fair. Oriard says readers could take him as hero or as a fatalist who gave up. | p. 72
idea: Mixed audiences read the same game two ways | Oriard shows genteel magazines presenting the frontier gambler as a noble sportsman and cheap novels presenting him as a trickster. The same figure and the same game carried different values for different readers. | p. 71
idea: Cheating is disgrace, losing is not | In Southern race week, schools, shops and courts closed, and winning by cheating would have shamed a gentleman more than losing. Bets were paid without argument. Oriard says the contest was a public display of honour rather than a fierce rivalry. | p. 84
idea: Give the pursued side a start | Southern hunters offered the fox a head start, called law, and avoided night hunting. Oriard says the code tried to offset the inequality of human advantage over animals by placing limits on the more powerful side. | p. 85
idea: Rules and ritual make a violent act tolerable | Southern society officially disliked dueling but accepted it because it had rules, seconds, formal steps and often ended without blood. Oriard says its sport-like form let public ritual take the place of private revenge. | p. 85
idea: A contest played to save face | In Swallow Barn two neighbours each refuse to win a lawsuit over land neither wants, because honour requires it. A lawyer arranges a settlement in which one takes the land and both keep their honour. Oriard describes the suit as turning into a fox chase. | p. 87
idea: The one who sets the terms can win | Mike Brown challenges the devil to a duel and expects fair play, but lets the devil choose the ground. The devil picks a swamp where Mike sticks fast, and calls himself no gentleman. The story shows a loophole in the terms of a match. | p. 88
idea: Style is part of the contest | Ned Hazard fights a bully with a cheerful face as if in mere sport, refuses to strike a man who is down and offers a fair field. Oriard says the manner of winning shows class as much as the result. | p. 89
idea: Playing to win against playing for play | Oriard's reading of Southern sport is that the planters defined themselves as a leisure class, so they had to be seen to play, yet also to work. They needed to look both industrious and leisurely. | p. 83
term: Fight-or-frolic | Oriard's name for a story pattern in which the same people fight in earnest and then play, so that violence and merriment sit side by side. A scout in a Simms novel turns playful before a duel, like a boy in a playground. | p. 102
idea: A contest has to be hard enough to count | Whitehead's hunter complains that a hunt is like shooting deer in a park when it becomes too easy. Oriard shows the Southern code treating the quarry's skill and chance as part of what made the hunt worth doing. | p. 91
idea: Absorption in the chase | Elliott asks his readers whether, in the middle of a fox chase, they can still say their name, or whether they have a wife or child. A person who can answer such questions is only half a sportsman. Oriard quotes this as a picture of play that takes over the whole attention. | p. 90
idea: Equality in play can stop at the edge of the game | Tucker says friends who raced and wrestled as boys learned not to claim privilege in a fight with those treated as equals in play. Oriard notes that the sense of equal terms in games was then used to argue that an unequal system was fair. | p. 92
idea: Letting the quarry win | When a wounded deer escapes into the water, Cornelia begs the men not to shoot because he has won his life, and they lower their guns. Oriard presents this as women writers' answer to the hunt, in which the chased animal counts as a winner. | p. 96
idea: Play without stakes | A Southern woman's novel notes that at the family parlour games nothing was staked. Women writers kept the spirit of play and rejected gambling, racing and dueling. Oriard says this separated play from the competitive contest. | p. 97
idea: Gambling and showy hosting can ruin a household | In Dupuy's novel a planter loses his estate first through lavish hospitality and then through speculation. Oriard says hosts competed to out-entertain each other in a way as ruinous as heavy betting. | p. 94
idea: The prize shows what a community values | Marcus Warland comes home with a blue ribbon for an essay, where readers expected a fox's tail. He gives it to his foster mother. Oriard uses the scene to show that a trophy names what the winner cares about and who he plays for. | p. 98
idea: Players may stake different things | Simms's hero stakes the fate of his region and country in a war game, while the turncoat plays for his own gain. Oriard says the first plays the open game and the second the sly one, and links the two to class. | p. 103
idea: A fair fight can favour the other side | In Simms's novels a patriot offers a captured British sergeant a fair fight with broadswords, a weapon that gives the sergeant the advantage. Fair play is shown by what you hand the opponent. | p. 100
idea: Playful manner before a fight | Before a duel with a traitor Jack Bannister drops his stern tone and acts like a boy in a playground. Oriard says Simms's heroes fight in a sporting manner, and in his novels this redeems battle from murder. The hero still says war is a duty and not a sport. | p. 102
idea: Fair play sorts people into groups | In Simms's romances the heroes gamble at cards and dice for pleasure, and the villains gamble viciously and cheat. Oriard says the author sorted the same sporting practices into those he admired and those he rejected. | p. 102
term: Ring tournament | A mounted contest in which knights wearing their ladies' colours compete for rings, used by Southern writers to link gentlemen's sport to medieval chivalry. | p. 109
idea: A prize that is given away | In postwar Southern fiction young men race one another for a fox's tail that each will give to a lady. The contest is among rivals and the trophy goes to a third person. Oriard reads the scene as a gentle form of manly rivalry. | p. 109
idea: Prize money changes why people play | A hero in one novel races for the name of his family's horses and objects to purses and side bets, which would turn a race into a money matter. Oriard shows two ways of seeing a race: sport for its own sake, or a contest for stakes. | p. 110
idea: Keeping score is not enough to make a game | In Huckleberry Finn the feuding families acknowledge rules and keep score of who has shot whom. Oriard says this shows violence dressed as sport, where rules and scores are present but the harm is not play. | p. 112
idea: Laughter in the face of defeat | Confederate writers show Jeb Stuart's soldiers dancing before a battle and singing as they ride to it, and say their mirth is evidence of game. Oriard calls this doomed playfulness, in which the way of facing a loss is itself praised. | p. 114
idea: One player treats it as war, the other as sport | In The Bostonians Olive describes her contest with Basil as a fight to the death, while he enjoys it as sport and answers in a joking tone. Oriard says Basil sees a game because he thinks women have no real power. | p. 118
idea: Contests are not always equal | Olive warns Verena against a contest that is not equal. Oriard uses this to say that contests between men and women then could not simply be fair, however the language of sport described them. | p. 119
idea: When the opponent is also the prize | In the love game described by Oriard, the person a man plays against is the one he wants to win, and she is supposed to win by losing. Both sides use the language of a contest but want different outcomes. | p. 119
idea: Treating a person as a card | Pardon in The Bostonians thinks of Verena as a high card to be played in a tour of paid lectures, as her father did. Oriard picks out how game words turn a person into a piece in someone else's hand. | p. 118
idea: A game with unevenly matched players is no longer sport | Godey's Lady's Book printed pictures of the sports of love, and also warned against men who sport with women's affections. Oriard says the sports of love become unsporting when the players are unevenly matched. | p. 125
idea: The early rounds are light and the stakes rise | In The Coquette, Eliza's flirtations seem an innocent pastime at first. When the stakes grow serious she loses everything. Oriard says the person who agreed to play is caught by her own wishes as much as by the other player. | p. 126
idea: The role fixes the available moves | In courtship a woman of the period was expected to be alluring yet restrained, so she could only act by hints and evasion. Oriard says these became the moves of the game and made inequality part of its ritual. | p. 126
idea: Not playing may be safer than playing | Virtuous women in cautionary novels did not play the game of love at all, because losing meant shame or death. Oriard shows the choice to sit out as itself a rule set by society. | p. 125
idea: Game words that warn | In Sedgwick's novel the words of the lottery and the whist table are used for a suitor who wagers on marrying and says the game is his. Oriard says the author used game language to mark danger for those who view marriage as a gambler does. | p. 128
idea: An early board game on courtship | A board game of 1858, The Game of Coquette and Her Suitors, has players as a parson, doctor, sailor, farmer, artist, merchant, soldier or mechanic. The first to reach the centre of the board wins the Coquette. Oriard notes most board games of the time were meant to teach, so this one is unusual. | p. 127
term: Croquet | A lawn game in which players hit balls through wickets and may knock an opponent's ball away. In Stowe's novel the characters use it to describe how a person manages friends and enemies. | p. 131
term: Draw | A result in which neither player wins. A journalist quoted by Oriard calls flirtation a game played on top of the table between evenly matched players that ends in a draw. | p. 139
term: Dead game sport | A phrase from dime novels for a reckless adventurer ready to risk everything on one card and not troubled by the result. In Wharton's novel it is used as praise for Lily Bart. | p. 142

idea: The winner gives the prize away | Eva wins the croquet match and offers the prize to Sophie, the runner-up. Oriard reads this as giving up the competition once it is won. | p. 132
idea: The same stake weighs differently on different players | Lily Bart plays bridge for money she cannot afford to lose, while the rich players treat the money as markers of winning and losing. In the same way a debt to Gus Trenor is a game to him and survival to her. Oriard says stakes are not the same for all who sit down. | p. 141

idea: The rules of the game invoked as a debt | Gus Trenor tells Lily it is not playing fair to take his help and give nothing, and says she is dodging the rules of the game. Oriard shows a claim of fair play used to demand a favour that no rule had stated. | p. 140
idea: A contest whose visible purpose differs from its real one | Wharton remembers archery club meetings where women wore veils that hindered their aim, because the complexion mattered more than winning. Oriard reads the sport as a display for the marriage market rather than a test of skill. | p. 142
idea: Winning and losing can both do harm | Lily Bart is ruined as a loser in the marriage game, and Undine Spragg wins it and loses her character. Oriard compares the two to show that the cost of playing can fall on the winner too. | p. 142
idea: A player cannot be content with a draw | Edna Pontellier will not play the flirtation game that ends in a draw between equals. She has too much at stake, and her desires are not met by a tie. Oriard shows the result of a game depends on what each side wants from it. | p. 139
idea: Wishing for risk where no risk is allowed | Edna bets on horses at the racetrack and swims far out, because her home life gives her no place for risk. Oriard reads the gambling and swimming as her wish for agon, a test of strength against the world. | p. 139

idea: A rule that makes players say what they do not feel | In a story Dolly tells her brother, as the rules dictate, that she does not like the gymnast, though she does. Oriard says the game requires a denial, and the confusion ends only when she stops playing and states her wish. | p. 133
idea: A person as the stake of a game | In James's novels a woman's fate is decided by a contest among others, with moves and countermoves over her. She is a stake or plaything and not a contestant. Oriard says these games are about power, though they present themselves as something gentler. | p. 145
term: Sport of the gods | A phrase for the view that humans are playthings of a power that decides everything. Oriard also lists the forms sport of fate and sport of chance. | p. 152
term: Game of life | The picture of life as a game in which a person freely competes for a prize that can be spiritual, such as salvation, or worldly, such as wealth. | p. 152
term: Lot | A method of choosing by chance, such as drawing marked stones or dice, used in the Bible to divide land or choose a replacement apostle. Believers read the result as God's choice, not as luck. | p. 154
term: Lusory lots | Increase Mather's name for gambling with dice and cards. He forbade it as blasphemy because the lot belonged to God. | p. 154
term: Wheel of Fortune | The image of a goddess turning a wheel that lifts some people up and drops others, and who tells them not to complain when she plays. | p. 153
term: Calling | In Puritan teaching, the work a person is called to do, either the general calling to serve God or a particular job that makes the person useful to neighbours. | p. 164
term: Covenant | In Puritan teaching, an agreement with obligations on both sides. Oriard notes that God was understood to bind himself to a covenant like a business contract, and compares it with the rules of a game. | p. 164
idea: Fate, will and chance as four kinds of game | Oriard sorts the metaphors by how much choice a player has. In the game of life everything is will. In the sport of the gods everything is fate. In the desperate game fate and will clash. In the sport of chance the will is subject to random events. | p. 152
idea: Dice were once read as an answer from God | In the Bible land was divided and an apostle was chosen by lot, because casting lots left the decision to God. For this reason strict Protestants forbade gambling: using dice for a pastime was playing with God's judgements. Oriard shows the same mechanism read as chance by some people and as divine will by others. | p. 154
idea: Fortune's game of lifting and dropping | In Boethius the goddess Fortune says she spins her wheel and enjoys lifting the low and lowering the high. She tells the philosopher he may climb only on the condition that he will not complain when her sport requires his fall. | p. 153

idea: Bargaining with the more powerful side | Increase Mather prayed with a promise of what he would do if God did what he asked, and pushed the arrangement toward a contest. Oriard notes this wrestling for a blessing was common in Puritan writing. | p. 157
idea: Holding the card that would end the game | In The Golden Bowl Maggie holds one card, a spoken accusation, that would end the game her father is enjoying. She decides never to play it and steps out to the terrace so the others can keep their illusion. Oriard calls the renunciation her cleverest move. | p. 148
idea: The same game with a spiritual or a money prize | Washington Gladden wrote that life is a contest whose prize is becoming what we are meant to be. Zebediah Flint wrote that the game of life is getting on and prospering. Oriard says the two descriptions meet in the middle. Both keep the contest and name the prize differently. | p. 161
idea: Taking part counts as winning | Oriard argues that in the success writing the game placed winning in the playing itself. Wealth was never declared more important than the act of acquiring it. The value lay in the process and not in the result. | p. 163
idea: The word game changed from frivolity to serious effort | In 1707 the minister Benjamin Colman said God and sacred things were never to be called our game, since game suggested frivolity. By 1914 Gladden used game to mean competition and striving in its most serious form. Oriard shows that the same word can mean opposite things at different times. | p. 164
idea: A free player earns the prize | Gladden took it for granted that players of life's game freely choose to compete and earn what they win. Oriard notes that this is the reverse of the earlier belief that the result depends on the sovereign God, not on the player. | p. 164
term: Game of Goose | A race game first published in England in 1597, in which players move along a board by the roll of dice toward a goal. Oriard calls it the prototype for most English and American board games, with ancestors at least four thousand years old. | p. 167
term: Race game | A board game in which the aim is to be first to cross the board and reach the goal. The result depends on chance, as in the Game of Goose, where the dice decide. | p. 167
term: Moral improvement game | A nineteenth-century board game meant to teach young people proper Christian living, such as The Mansion of Happiness (1800) and Virtue Rewarded and Vice Punished (1818). Players are rewarded or sent back by their conduct. | p. 167
term: Morphology of conversion | The fixed sequence of stages, each with signs a believer could check, that Puritan teachers said a saved soul passed through. Oriard compares it to the squares on a board game. | p. 165
term: Score card | A sheet on which a player marks results to see progress. Franklin kept a little book with a column for each virtue and marked his faults every day. | p. 170
idea: Stages with a reward at the end make a board game | Perkins drew the steps by which a soul moves toward salvation or damnation, in 1592. Oriard points to the likeness with a race board game such as Goose, published five years later, which also depends on chance as Perkins's Christian depended on God's will. Later moral board games joined the two patterns. | p. 167
idea: A board game that teaches through advance and retreat | Nineteenth-century board games like The Game of Human Life and The Mansion of Happiness taught children Christian living by rewarding virtue and punishing vice. Alcott's Little Women draws on a Pilgrim's Progress board game from her childhood. Oriard says children's play mirrored what adults believed about a moral life. | p. 167
idea: A personal score card | Franklin took one virtue each week for thirteen weeks, four rounds a year, and marked every fault in a book. He also planned each hour of the day. Oriard compares this to a board game: a course, a score card and a progress toward a reward. | p. 170
idea: Teaching through the game children already play | Cotton Mather wrote that he watched what games his children played and reminded them of pious lessons suited to the play. Oriard sets this beside Franklin's own use of games for success rather than salvation. | p. 172
idea: Losers perform a task | Franklin and a friend agreed that whoever won each chess game could set a task, such as part of the Italian grammar to learn by heart, and the loser did it before the next meeting. As they played about equally, they taught each other the language. The stake is work and not money. | p. 172
idea: What chess teaches | In his essay on the morals of chess Franklin says it builds foresight, circumspection, caution and perseverance, and that life is a kind of chess with points to gain and opponents to face. He adds that a more skilled player can help the less skilled avoid mistakes to keep the match equal. | p. 173
idea: The opponent is your own tendency to err | Oriard notes that Franklin plays down rivalry. In chess and in life the real opponent is the player's own habit of making mistakes, an idea later success writers inherited. | p. 173
idea: A race in which everyone who finishes the course is crowned | Gladden wrote in 1868 that Greek races gave one prize for many runners, but in the races of life all who run are crowned, because people are matched against difficulties and not against each other. Oriard calls it a game without an opponent. | p. 178
idea: Everyone can win because the win is inward | Beecher said people are unequally equipped for the race of life, but since victory is inward, all can win by taking part in the struggle. Abbott said the pursuit of life is itself the prize. Oriard shows the win condition moved inside the player. | p. 177
idea: A career laid out as spaces on a board | Carnegie's lecture marks each turning point in turn: choice of a career, start in life, openings to success, second step upward, and so on, with the wrong path of speculation marked too. Oriard compares it to a game board with rewards at the end. | p. 182
idea: Each player sets the win condition | Later success writers said success meant whatever the individual wanted it to mean, and moved the contest from the social world into the mind. Oriard says this made winning possible for all who felt outside power, since the goal was whatever the player chose. | p. 183
idea: Two games called business | Oriard finds two games in business novels: speculation, treated as gambling and condemned, and productive enterprise, treated as an honourable sport. The same word described both a vice and a calling, depending on how the play was done. | p. 159
idea: A race can be run by foul means | William Matthews asks whether life is a scrub race in which you have to blind the man on your right and trip the one on your left to come out ahead. He answers that the true race is another one. The question separates a race with fair play from a race without limits. | p. 180
idea: Struggle changes from battle to sport | Oriard says liberal preachers moved from the language of war to the language of athletic contest between 1850 and 1914. Total war had made battle terrible, and sport carried an ethic of fair play. Calling a struggle a sport changed how the struggle was felt. | p. 174
term: Checkered Game of Life | A board game by Milton Bradley, published in 1860, that taught moral habits as players moved through spaces for virtue and vice. Parker Brothers published a similar game, The Game of Christian Endeavor, in 1896. | p. 185
term: Glad Game | The game Pollyanna teaches a whole town in a 1913 novel, in which the object is to find something to be glad about in every person and event. It replaces moral striving with feeling content. | p. 185
idea: A game assigned for a week | In Little Women, Mrs. March gives her daughters a week-long game based on Pilgrim's Progress, in which each girl is a pilgrim carrying her own burden and developing a habit. Oriard notes that moral board games of the time followed the same idea. | p. 185
idea: Games lie between play and striving | Oriard says all games fall on a line between pure play and pure striving. Marden's success game slid toward play: he wrote that the player loses sight of the hope of victory in the zest of the game and seeks the pleasure of the chase. | p. 185
idea: A game against your own faults | Oriard says the game of life moved the contest inward. Players competed against their own faults and not against other people. This differs from the Western and Southern sporting myths, where a rival had to be beaten. | p. 188
idea: Chance is denied to keep the game fair | Thayer and Marden said there is no such thing as luck in a world of law and order. Oriard explains that luck would have meant disorder and no equal opportunity. Whether a game includes chance says what a culture claims about fairness. | p. 190
idea: More tickets, higher odds | Hardwicke compared success to a lottery in which the person with the most tickets has the highest chance of the prize. He argued that wider knowledge and skill act as extra tickets. | p. 184
idea: The player who does not play fair is the villain | Frank Merriwell's stories rest on a simple contest between Frank and an opponent who does not play fair, such as a crooked umpire or an unscrupulous speculator. Oriard says the playing field becomes a picture for the whole world. | p. 193
idea: Exploiting an opponent who plays fair | In The American the Bellegardes agree to the rules and the stake, which is a marriage, and then withdraw permission when it no longer serves them. Newman stays a sportsman, and at the end a friend points out that they had counted on his fairness all along. | p. 196
idea: Handing your hand to a friend | Christopher Newman tells a friend that when he left business he made over his hand to someone else and could take up the cards again if he liked. Oriard uses the picture of a player who can leave a game and return to it. | p. 195
idea: Playing only if you can sweep the board | Holland's villain says he will sweep the board, fairly or foully, or not play. He plays for two stakes at once, present profit and future power, and ends the story whimpering that he is played out. Oriard shows a player whose need to win everything ends the game for him. | p. 198
idea: Swept along by the game's logic | Silas Lapham is warned that speculation is like betting on the turn of a card. Once in, he is carried along by the game and tries to buy out his competitors to hold the whole game in his hand. His escape comes by refusing his partner's games altogether. | p. 200
idea: A contest where the players hit each other | Phelps describes a strike as an unskilled, futile boxing match in which the strikers bash each other more than their real opponents. Oriard notes this anticipates a scene in Ralph Ellison's Invisible Man. | p. 196
