term: Play movement | A reform effort from the 1880s in which towns and reformers organised play for children and adults, for the sake of health, citizenship and less crime. | p. 393
term: Organised play | Play whose teams, leagues, times and aims are set by someone outside the players. The book says the organising brings in objectives beyond the activity itself. | p. 392
term: Instrumental play | Play that is valued because it serves something else, such as work done faster, health or a steadier town, and not for itself. | p. 393
term: Work/play dualism | The habit of splitting life into two separate halves, work for adults and play for children or for days off, with each half defined by the other. | p. 393
term: Make-believe | Play in which the players agree to treat things as something else, such as lath swords or a Sunday School outing as an army. It works only while everyone shares the pretence. | p. 403
term: Recreationist | A trained person who teaches others how to use free time, including skills of play. | p. 446
term: Enjoyment as duty | A mid-century attitude that enjoying yourself is expected of everyone, so that enjoyment becomes an obligation. Wolfenstein gave it a name in 1955 that the book quotes. | p. 446
term: Poker face | A face that shows nothing about the cards in the hand. A character who cannot keep one grins at a high hand. | p. 452
term: Game of life | The saying that life is played by rules like a game. The book shows it used by an adult to teach a boy, and rejected by the boy. | p. 452
term: Oasis of play | A short stretch of playing set inside a hard life, such as a swim, a fishing trip or a centre-field afternoon, remembered as free. | p. 453
term: Touch | A player's feel for the ball, shown in shooting that comes by instinct and cannot be switched on by will. | p. 471
term: Gunner | A basketball player who takes many shots and scores a lot, but does not play defence. | p. 471
term: Play power | The 1960s idea that playing, with pranks and costume, could change politics as well as protest could. | p. 459
term: Revolutionary carnival | A protest staged as a festival, with costume, music and jokes. The book gives it as Roszak's name for the Yippies' methods. | p. 460
term: Peak performance | A New Age idea that people do their most successful work when the work feels like play. | p. 469
term: Rule-governed play | Play in which the rules are fixed and shared, as children keep them, and the players take it seriously. The book sets it against spontaneous play. | p. 468
term: Level playing field | A contest in which the ground gives no side an advantage. The book says American belief that life is a game rests on a field that may or may not be level. | p. 478
idea: Organising play adds aims beyond the play | When teams, leagues, rankings, championships, statistics and records come in, players are working toward things outside the activity. The book says the prophets of college sport after the Civil War cared about character building and not play. | p. 392
idea: Play is promoted for its uses | Churches built gymnasiums to pull young people away from temptation, and employers backed working-class play so that work would go faster and crime would fall. Play was recognised, but only for what it did for something else. | p. 393
idea: A week with no work wears the players out | In Little Women the girls try a week of only play. After one day they are weary of it, and their mother says to keep regular hours for work and play. | p. 398
idea: A game can carry a lesson | The March sisters play a game of Pilgrim's Progress, which the book describes as moral striving in the form of play. The rules of the game are a story with a message. | p. 398
idea: Play defined as what you are not obliged to do | In the whitewashing scene Twain says work is whatever a body is obliged to do and play is whatever it is not obliged to do. By that line, Tom and Joe on Jackson's Island are no longer playing, since they stay on long after they are tired of it. | p. 401
idea: One player can bring others into his game | Tom Sawyer gets the adults of the town to go along with him in treating the world as a place for play. The children's game becomes a shared set of pretences. | p. 401
idea: A pretend game needs everyone to share it | Tom wants to tie Jim to a tree as a joke and plays pirates and robbers. Huck loses patience when he says the swords are lath and the elephants are a Sunday School outing. The people outside the pretence pay for it. | p. 403
idea: Play holds inside a boundary | On the raft Huck is free and has no one to answer to. On the shore the same play has no power over the feud, the con men or the killing. The raft works like a space marked off from the world. | p. 403
idea: The word game covers cruel contests as well as pastimes | In Huckleberry Finn the feud, the con men's schemes and the torturing of dogs in Bricksville are all called games. The book calls them versions of false play that refuse to see the world as it is. | p. 403
idea: Play does not sort right from wrong | Huck's lazy days with Jim and his lazy days with Pap are described in nearly the same words. The book says play has no moral conscience, and that the two sets of days differ only in what threatens them. | p. 404
idea: Hiding a fact that changes the game | Twain keeps back the news that Jim is already free while Tom stages an escape. The book says he cheated the reader, because the pretence of the final chapters rests on a fact the reader was not given. | p. 404
idea: Work and play can be mixed for a moment only | Hapgood writes of a spell in Italy when work was play and play was work. The mixture gives way when passion breaks both. | p. 414
idea: Cheating with nothing at stake | Lardner describes golf matches where rich members cheat whether or not anything is at stake. The cheating has become a habit of the game and is separate from any wish for the prize. | p. 417
idea: Fixing a contest ends belief in all contests | The gambler Wolfsheim fixes the World Series. The narrator treats this as toying with the faith of a whole nation in the result. | p. 425
idea: Social games run on scripts | Amory's courtships are games of lines and poses, with set phrases and rules nobody breaks. Rosalind ends one with a score and a rain call. | p. 419
idea: A game without a referee | Amory calls life a football game with every one offside and the referee got rid of, with everyone claiming the referee would have ruled for them. The picture shows what a referee does: he settles which claim counts. | p. 420
idea: Play can be set against the game | Salinger and Holmes use play for freedom and game for posing and pettiness. Holden says a game is a game only if you are on the side where the hot-shots are. | p. 453
idea: Playing without caring for the score | Seymour plays Ping-Pong hard and with joy, with no interest in the score. At soccer he runs down the field and waits so the goalie can get set, and his team finds this unendearing. | p. 452
idea: Aim less | Seymour tells Buddy to try not aiming so much at marbles, so that shooter and shot become one. The advice is to stop trying to win by control. | p. 452
idea: A feel that cannot be called up | Rabbit's touch on the court comes and goes, and one unforced golf swing sends the ball straight. The next swing, in A Month of Sundays, is just as unthinking and slices into the rough. | p. 471
idea: A pastime turns into work as it becomes routine | Rabbit plays golf weekly at a country club and says it feels more like work. His practice swing is smooth, but anxiety comes in when he faces the ball. | p. 472
idea: Control against touch | In sixties basketball novels, free-lancing players who live by touch meet coaches obsessed with patterns and control. The book reads this as the game's work and play pulling apart. | p. 456
idea: The same game can stand for different things over time | Twain saw baseball as a symbol of the rush and push of his century. From the 1930s writers made it a garden in a land of machines, the summer game. | p. 456
idea: Football novels set love of the game against management | The football novel of the sixties puts a hero who loves the game for its own sake against management that regiments his life on and off the field. | p. 456
idea: A calendar of games | In Couples the ten pairs live by a wheel of parties and games, with basketball and touch football at weekends and a Sunday evening with no game. Summer is the season of many games and autumn the season of duty. | p. 473
idea: Hard times raise the number of games | In the 1930s Monopoly, dance marathons, roller derbies, six-day bicycle races and softball all spread, and laws against gambling eased. The book reads some of these as efforts to keep up hope. | p. 443
idea: A festival of play ends | The Marble Faun stops at the Carnival, but Couples follows the couples back into the world after the carnival. When the parties stop, the same people turn to earnest matters. | p. 474
idea: A game with a ruler | Kesey invents a game called Power, in which the winner has thirty minutes when everything he says is law. Wolfe takes it as an allegory of the Pranksters' own hierarchy. | p. 464
idea: A group that rejects games makes its own | The Pranksters call the straight world a set of games, yet they divide into those on the bus and those off it, and an old personality game returns. | p. 464
idea: Play ordered by an organiser | The book says Kesey ends up like a recreation director of the 1910s, insisting on play for the benefit of young souls. Play that is ordered by an organiser is the thing the book calls antiplay. | p. 465
idea: A game closed to outsiders | At the Pranksters' graduation party the music is wired only to their own headphones. Outsiders look on puzzled or angry while the circle around Kesey closes. | p. 465
idea: Rules and community, set against spontaneity | Sennett wants play as children do it, with rules, seriousness and shared pleasure, in place of play as spontaneity. Lasch gets his model of such a game from contests of speech and body in Aristotle. | p. 468
idea: A prank can break up a serious meeting | Kesey brings the Day-Glo bus to a Berkeley rally and tells the students they are playing the Pentagon's game. Wolfe says it deadened the rally. | p. 458
idea: Play used as a method of protest | The Yippies exorcised the Pentagon, scattered money before the Stock Exchange, and put a pig up for president. The book says the politics of play freed culture more than it changed politics. | p. 459
idea: A style gets copied by sellers | Hippie entrepreneurs sold the signs of rebellion and mass advertisers took up the style. The book says the playfulness of the young was soon part of middle-class life. | p. 460
idea: Singing together, against playing alone | The scenes of blues in Hughes, McKay and Hurston show play shared by a crowd, unlike the lone self of earlier writers. The dance floor and the jook are crowded. | p. 439
idea: Play that carries grief | Ellison's blues keeps painful details alive and lifts them into something part tragic and part comic. The book calls this playful tragedy, and sets it against white writers who saw play without pain. | p. 436
idea: Sports words describe the rest of life | The book argues American writers used game and play to criticise, justify or celebrate ordinary life. Sport is a metaphor for life in the country. | p. 477
idea: Where tradition is thin, rules take its place | In the book's account, American society relied on laws and gamelike rules of behaviour. The book adds that a faith in competition and a level field made life look like a game. | p. 478
idea: Players turn to self-fulfilment | By the 1970s and 1980s free agency, endorsements and end-zone dances showed players treating sport as a place for themselves and not for the team. Courts sided with players over owners. | p. 479
idea: Courts may protect the game itself | The author suspects that earlier rulings which freed baseball owners from antitrust law came from a belief that the game is a public possession, ranking above any one player. | p. 479
idea: Leisure can keep people occupied | The Grand Inquisitor in Dostoyevsky would have people work and, in their free hours, live like children with songs and dances. The book reads this as a picture of a consumer culture. | p. 483
