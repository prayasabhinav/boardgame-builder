term: Gamespace | Wark's name for a world in which work, politics, school and leisure are all arranged as games, with ranks, scores and winners. It has no outside to step into. | p. 11
term: Agon | Competition between players who strive for rank and distinction, with a winner and a loser. Caillois uses it for one of his four kinds of play. | p. 14
term: Alea | Games of chance, where dice, cards or luck decide the result and skill does not. Caillois's word for the second kind of play. | p. 19
term: Ilinx | Play that seeks dizziness or a shaken sense of balance and order. Caillois names it as one of four kinds of play, alongside agon, alea and mimesis. | p. 94
term: Mimesis | Play of pretending, imitation and show. It is Caillois's name for the kind of play built on make-believe and spectacle. | p. 94
term: Level playing field | A phrase for a world where everyone starts equal and the rules are the same for all. Wark notes that the phrase hides who owns the teams and who writes the rules. | p. 16
term: High score | The ranked list of top results kept for a game. Wark says every game, inside and outside the cave, has its own such ranking. | p. 20
term: Score | The number a game gives back to a player for what they did. Wark says in gamespace the score is the main thing and needs fall away. | p. 25
term: Single-player game | A game played alone against the machine, not through a network of other people. Wark wonders if it will become a vanished form, like silent film. | p. 27
term: Strategy guide | A printed or online book that tells players how to raise their score or finish a game. Wark says his book is not one. | p. 27
term: Mod | A change a player makes to a game's own files or contents so it plays differently. Wark says a theorist who mods does it to play more closely inside the game. | p. 26
term: Allegory | A story or scene whose parts stand for something else, so that one thing can mean another. Wark says games work as allegories of the world around them. | p. 31
term: Allegorithm | Wark's coined word. It names the double relation of a player to a game's algorithm: the player learns the hidden rules by playing, and that learning stands for how people relate to the hidden rules of daily life. | p. 31
term: Algorithm | A finite list of instructions that turns a starting condition into a clear end condition. A recipe in The Sims and the requirements for a career are both examples. | p. 32
term: Algorithmic game | A game in which calculations or rules underneath decide how it answers what the player does. The term is Costikyan's. | p. 32
term: Career | In The Sims, a ladder of jobs, each with set requirements such as number of friends or points in creativity. When they are met the career ends and another begins. | p. 32
term: Remediation | Taking images, stories and characters from older media, such as films and novels, and reusing them inside a new medium such as a game. | p. 33
term: Meter | A bar graph on screen that shows how a character is doing, such as hunger or sleep. In The Sims the bars drift toward empty unless the player acts. | p. 35
term: Save | Storing the state of a game so the player can go back to it after a failure. Wark ties it to the way a game can undo time. | p. 35
term: Free will setting | An option in The Sims. When it is on, characters act on their own; when it is off, their actions follow the player's plan. | p. 36
term: Level | A stage in a game with a set threshold. Wark says that a higher level is only more of the same quantity as a lower one, whatever name it is given. | p. 37
term: Skin | The outward look of a character: colour, hair, clothes. In The Sims it has no effect on play, while the hidden numbers underneath do. | p. 38
term: Trifler | In Bernard Suits's words, a player who accepts the rules of a game but ignores its goal, for example someone who plays The Sims to arrange furniture and not to rise through careers. | p. 39
term: Cheat | In Suits's words, a player who aims at the goal of a game but ignores its rules. Wark sets this against the trifler. | p. 39
term: Crunch time | A stretch of long working hours in a game studio before a deadline. Wark describes it growing from eight hours a day to seven days a week. | p. 41
term: Grind level | A stage of a game that takes repeated effort and can be dull, which a player gets through by starting again after each defeat. | p. 47
term: Strategy game | A genre where the player manages resources over time and moves among many variables, such as Civilization III. | p. 59
term: Database | In Wark's account, the stored collection of numbers and facts that a strategy game draws on. Playing it means moving through the collection, with no story to follow. | p. 60
term: Dependent and independent variables | Quantities in a model, some of which change because of the others. A player of Civilization III finds out which is which by trial and error. | p. 60
term: Telesthesia | Wark's word for sensing and acting at a distance, through the telegraph, telephone, television and networks. | p. 50
term: Topic | In Wark's usage, a place that exists both on the ground and in language, such as a settlement known by name, before maps join places together. | p. 48
term: Topography | Space that is charted by maps and lines, with fixed shapes and boundaries between what is mapped and what is not. | p. 48
term: Topology | Space as a network of lines in which any point can be linked to any other and shapes change into one another without breaking. Wark says games are the form that fits it. | p. 52
term: Analog | Variation by more and less along a line, a continuous movement such as pushing a stick a little or a lot. | p. 73
term: Digital | Division of things by a line into either one side or the other, so that a ball is big enough or it is not. | p. 73
term: Analog stick | A control on a game pad that can be pushed by degrees in any direction. In Katamari Damacy it moves the ball, though the game turns the movement into yes and no decisions. | p. 69
term: Cut scene | A short film passage the game shows between periods of play, often as a reward for finishing a level. | p. 84
term: Utopia | An imagined ideal place described on a page, separated from the world by a barrier of distance or time. | p. 86
term: Heterotopia | A place set apart with its own rules, such as a stadium, gallery or court. Foucault's term, which Wark uses for the fields where games used to stay. | p. 91
term: Atopia | Wark's name for a place with no fixed location, where a game's values run through every space. In Vice City each part of the map carries a score. | p. 98
term: Mission | A set task in a game with a clear reward. In Vice City each mission pays a fixed sum of money. | p. 96
term: Game theory | The mathematical study of choices made by separate agents who calculate their own interest, as in the prisoner's dilemma. Wark contrasts it with gamer theory. | p. 100
term: Targeting | Picking out one thing among many as the object of an action. In Rez the player holds the trigger to select and lets go to fire. | p. 103
term: Synaesthesia | A blending of the senses. Rez joins colour, shape, beat, sound and controller vibration so that they pulse together. | p. 104
term: Level up | To move on to a higher level in a game after meeting its demands. | p. 104
term: Protocol | A set of rules saying which points in a network may open to which others. Wark treats the structure of a game's world as this kind of rule. | p. 111
term: Backstory | The background tale that explains why the player is acting, such as the story of Eden in Rez. Wark says the player may read it once or not at all. | p. 113
term: Boss | The large opponent at the end of a run of levels. Wark uses the big boss as a picture of time, always out ahead of the player. | p. 126
term: Stealth | A way of playing in which the aim is to avoid being targeted. In Deus Ex it is one of three ways through the game. | p. 143
term: Greimas square | A diagram of four terms: two that oppose each other and two that are each the other side of the first pair. Wark uses it to lay out the four endings of Deus Ex. | p. 141
term: Non-player character | A character in a game that the game controls and the player does not. | p. 145
term: Extopia | Wark's word for a state in which gamer and game cease to be separate terms and the limits of any place disappear. | p. 147
term: Hacker | Someone whose free, self-directed activity makes its own rules and its own measure of success. Wark places the hacker outside work and outside play. | p. 152
term: Game engine | The software that builds a game's world and fixes what the player can do in it. Wark says it makes worlds that seem made for the player to act upon. | p. 153
term: Resource allocation | Choosing how to spend limited supplies among uses. In SimEarth the player divides energy among agriculture, science, medicine and the arts. | p. 156
term: Win condition | The rule that says when a game is won and the algorithm ends. Wark says SimEarth has none that satisfies. | p. 164
term: Avatar | A figure through which a player acts in a game. Wark says the gamer is also an avatar of the game itself. | p. 166
idea: The game becomes the form of daily life | Wark argues that games have stopped being a pastime beside life and become the pattern life follows. Work is a rat race, politics is a horse race and the economy is a casino, and each ends in a win or a loss. | p. 15
idea: Games as allegories, not copies | Wark holds that games do not stand for the world the way a picture does. They work as allegories, carrying the abstract rules by which choices are made in the world. In The Sims a rise through jobs by points echoes how careers run outside the game. | p. 24
idea: Trifling to understand the form | A player can ignore winning and play with style to see how the game is built. Wark says this lets the player learn how the wider game-world falls short of the claims it makes about fair contests and level fields. | p. 25
idea: Learning a game is learning its algorithm | A player finds the rules by trial and error and gradually works out what each choice does. Wark quotes Galloway that to play is to play the code, and gives The Sims, where every object and person has a value that can be found out. | p. 32
idea: The game is more consistent than the world | Wark says everyday life seems an uneven version of the game, which runs on rules that always give the same result. A Sim looks crude next to a person, but the game's rules are easier to know than the rules of daily life. | p. 33
idea: Saving turns time into something reversible | In a game a mistake can be undone by going back to a saved state. Wark says this removes the one loose end of daily life, that time runs one way. In The Sims, a player who forgot to save has no earlier point to go back to. | p. 35
idea: Failure is measured and taught by the numbers | In The Sims the bar graphs show how needs fall when the player is busy elsewhere. A Sim who is not taken to the toilet in time wets himself, and the player learns through counted failure. | p. 35
idea: Characters and images carry a score | Players enjoy wrecking Sims because images are cheap, Wark says, but the algorithm still counts everything. Even a house without doors is scored as hunger rises. | p. 36
idea: A higher level is just more | Wark argues that names for levels, such as Scholar or Theorist, only label a larger number. In The Sims, climbing a career feels like a change of kind but is a change of amount. | p. 37
idea: The look is a skin, the numbers decide | In The Sims 2 the player chooses gender, colour, hair and clothes, but these do not alter play. Hidden values for charisma or creativity fix which careers a Sim can succeed in. | p. 38
idea: The trifler and the cheat | Suits describes the trifler as one who keeps the rules and drops the goal, and the cheat as one who keeps the goal and drops the rules. Wark sees players of The Sims who ignore the goal and tell stories or build houses as triflers. | p. 39
idea: Constraint is what a game offers | Wark says a game gives a consistent set of limits. A Sims player can try endless sofas and tables, yet the sequences built in by the designers are fixed, so the freedom is within those limits. | p. 39
idea: Work in the games trade is not a game | Wark points to EA Spouse's account of crunch time at a game studio. A Sim moves from job to job in measured steps, but a person in the real trade works twelve hours a day, six days a week, with nowhere else to go. | p. 41
idea: The meter always runs | A player writes to the makers of The Sims asking for no meter on items bought. Wark reads this as showing that the counting belongs to the whole game-world and cannot easily be removed. | p. 45
idea: A game is a contest over nothing | In a game nobody fights over minerals or labour contracts, and the rules treat every player the same way. Wark says that is the difference between a game and a world where the stakes are real. | p. 45
idea: A game reaches the world through its materials | Wark points out that a PlayStation contains coltan dug in the Congo. The game is closed off by its rules, but the machine it runs on depends on mining, wars and the destruction of animal habitats. | p. 42
idea: Starting over after defeat | Wark frames history as a run of levels, each opened only after the one before is cleared, where a defeated player starts again. Time in a game is counted in equal units, so a level can always be played once more. | p. 47
idea: Strategy games model resource allocation | In Civilization III everything is a quantity to be moved from one use to another. The player taxes, invests in science or culture, and sees that a small change in one variable can have a big effect elsewhere. | p. 59
idea: A database replaces a story | Wark says a strategy game turns descriptions into a database and a storyline into a way of moving through it. The Civilopedia is a reference book for the game's world, and the player picks combinations from it. | p. 60
idea: The player is a manager and a designer | In Civilization III the player works within limits, finds out by trial and error which variables depend on which, and starts over when a plan fails. Wark says this is the work of a game designer too. | p. 61
idea: The possible is real inside the game | Wark says that if the algorithm can produce an outcome from the database, it exists in the game. In Civilization III any mix of resources the rules allow can be tried, and the actual world is just one of the outcomes. | p. 63
idea: Every choice of side plays the same game | In Civilization III it does not matter whether the player picks Babylon, China or Zulu-land. Wark says the logic of the game is the same for all, so whoever wins plays by one set of rules. | p. 64
idea: Analog movement is turned into yes and no | In Katamari Damacy the player twists a stick by degrees, but the game turns this into a decision. A ball is big enough to become a star or it is not, and the stick movement only decides how quickly the player gets there. | p. 69
idea: Every game ends in a decision | Wark says that all games are digital in the sense that they come down to in or out, goal or no goal. Even a board game is a machine of human motion and objects that ends in a ruling by a referee. | p. 70
idea: A threshold needs to be marked | The task of Sisyphus is to roll a rock up a hill with no mark for the top, so it never ends. In Katamari Damacy a small icon shows the size the ball has to reach, so effort ends in a decision. | p. 70
idea: The digital allows saving | Because a game is digital, Wark says, a finished ball can be saved and comes back unchanged. What is saved does not decay, but if the system crashes it may be lost altogether. | p. 75
idea: Scale can change while the form stays | The Katamari ball starts with household objects and grows until it gathers whole towns and then stars. Wark says the same rule works at each scale, as a tiny electric switch can set off a missile. | p. 75
idea: Moves come before rules | Wark asks which came first, play or the game. He suggests rules grew out of moves: a transgression is marked only after someone has made the move. Now the game leads and play follows. | p. 78
idea: Play can go on, a game stops | The task of Sisyphus is a move that never crosses a line. A game, in Wark's reading, stops and starts again and again, because each run produces a score and then ends. | p. 79
idea: Games began in military research | Wark quotes Herz that nearly every part of an arcade or console traces back to a defence grant. The same technology that served command and control later became a form of entertainment. | p. 81
idea: The designer is also an artist | Takahashi, who made Katamari Damacy, is a sculptor who says he does not play games. Wark says the artist can work from inside the game, leaving something of the hand in the code. | p. 83
idea: Contest alone wears a culture out | Wark cites Huizinga that when competition dominates, decline follows in time. He suggests a style of play that moves away from the either-or of winning and losing. | pp. 83
idea: Games once stayed in set-apart places | Caillois placed games of competition and chance in special zones with their own rules, outside daily life. These fields, courts and arenas are the places Foucault called heterotopias. | p. 91
idea: Two aims for two kinds of play | Wark says aesthetic play aims to be free and calculated games aim to be fair. In each case the aim only holds inside the place built for it. | p. 91
idea: Rules chosen by the maker | The Oulipo writers set themselves arbitrary rules to see what kinds of play followed. Wark says this suits a time when the margins that held games have disappeared. | p. 94
idea: All four kinds of play at once | In Vice City chance, competition, dizziness and spectacle all appear together. A player takes chances, competes, drives through a world of drugs and noise and watches destruction, all inside one set of rules. | p. 95
idea: A game of crime in which cheating is not possible | Vice City is about breaking the law, but its player cannot break the game's rules. Even the cheat codes are part of the rules, so every move that looks wrong is a move into another game. | p. 96
idea: Fixed reward, fixed rule | Each mission in Vice City pays exactly one thousand dollars. Wark says this shows the game does not care about description, only about the relations between numbers. | p. 96
idea: Setting and story are decoration | In Vice City the criminal world, the art and the story are surface. The player uses them to find the steps of the algorithm, as a way of guessing the rules. | p. 97
idea: Game theory and gamer theory | Game theory starts with a calculating agent weighing odds, as a prisoner in a cell does. Wark says gamer theory asks how the player becomes an agent at all, and how a player decides when to act in the middle of a battle. | p. 100
idea: Targeting is done in two steps | In Rez the player holds the trigger to pick targets and releases it to fire, so aiming is separate from shooting. Wark says this puts the act of targeting ahead of the target. | p. 103
idea: Abstracting the enemy | The shapes in Rez are colourful and abstract, and the story about why one shoots is minimal. Wark says the game's interest lies in the act of targeting and not in who or what is hit. | p. 104
idea: Repeating the same time | A level of Rez can be replayed any number of times, and time inside it is measured in the same units each run. Wark says this makes game time repeatable and without the wear that time has outside. | p. 109
idea: Access follows protocol | In Rez every point may connect to every other, but only some links open at a time. Wark says play is the work of negotiating the rules that decide which points open. | p. 111
idea: The story is an alibi | The story in a game, Wark says, excuses the player for treating something as a target. In Rez the player saves a network, and in other games plays the villain, but the story is not the point of the play. | p. 113
idea: Games are not morality tales | Wark says the story of a game cannot be read as a moral. He proposes reading it as an allegory of how signs are held together in the game and not held together outside it. | p. 113
idea: Boredom is a lack of room for action | Wark describes boredom as being in a space where one cannot act, or where acts add up to nothing, like waiting in an airport. A game such as State of Emergency lets the player change the space, which displaces some of it. | p. 126
idea: Choosing your own necessities | In a game a player picks which circumstances to push against. Wark says this is a freedom the world does not give, since in a game the demands are chosen and the limits are set from the start. | p. 130
idea: A game's limit is given in advance | Boredom comes back, Wark says, whenever a player reaches the end of what a game offers. In games the limit is known from the start, which he counts as a feature of the form. | p. 130
idea: When there is nothing to trifle with | Wark finds State of Emergency has little room to play with style. Every object in it appears only as a way to win, so the game turns back to dullness. | p. 131
idea: Designers keep adding new games | Wark argues that the game industry answers boredom by producing new rules, new moves and new contests. Boredom with one game is moved onto the next one. | p. 134
idea: Three ways through a game | Wark notes that Deus Ex can be played by stealth, by violence or by negotiation. Each gets from beginning to end, but the player still works through it level by level. | p. 140
idea: Four endings as a puzzle | Deus Ex has four endings, each tied to a group seeking power. Wark arranges them on a Greimas square to show that the game's possible ends hang together as a set. | p. 141
idea: A game with several endings shows its rules | A story has one ending, but Deus Ex has four. Wark says this reveals a little more of the rules by which the game makes meaning. | p. 143
idea: Playing as an alternation | In Wark's account a player in play moves between being apart from a target and being joined to it. Deus Ex stealth mode reverses this, since the aim is to avoid becoming a target. | p. 143
idea: The maker of rules as a fourth character | Wark sets player against worker, then asks who is neither. The gamer is the non-player and the hacker is the non-worker, a person who makes both moves and rules. | p. 152
idea: Game engines make worlds to act in | Wark says a game engine presents a world as made for the player to act upon in a way set by the rules. A book or film is for looking at, while the engine's world gives the player actions. | p. 153
idea: The party ending | Deus Ex has a fifth ending where all the characters, whatever their side, party together. Differences stay but are no longer in opposition. Wark notes that it still sits inside the game. | p. 154
idea: A model that counts consequences | SimEarth lets players choose energy sources and uses, and it shows effects they did not plan. More farming raises the population and also the methane from rice fields and cattle, which warms the planet. | p. 157
idea: A game with no satisfying end | Wark says SimEarth lacks a clear win condition, since the usual result for most runs is that the people leave in spaceships. He reads this as showing the limit of gamespace, which leaves out the by-products of its choices. | p. 164
idea: Games change by variation and selection | Wark likens game forms to living forms: designers breed new versions from old ones and the market selects which survive. SimEarth left no sequel, while The Sims lived on and Spore offered more than one world. | p. 163
idea: The player is played by the game | In a God game the player seems to be in control, but Wark says the game plays the player. The gamer is an avatar of an abstract principle and answers to the game. | p. 166
idea: Games as a way to think about now | For Wark, games are the form in which the present can be felt and thought. To study them is to rethink cultural history after the digital game; it is more than adding a new medium to a list. | p. 172
