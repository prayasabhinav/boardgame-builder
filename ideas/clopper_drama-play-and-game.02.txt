term: Pageant wagon | A wheeled stage on which one guild's play was performed. In York, Coventry and Chester the wagons moved through the town and stopped at set places in turn. | p. 172
term: Processional staging | Playing a series of plays one after another at several stops in a town, so each audience waits at its own stop while the wagons come to it. The book sets it against plays given on a fixed stage. | p. 171
term: Fixed stage | A play given at one place with its stages built there, indoors or outdoors, instead of moving from stop to stop. Most plays in East Anglia and the south-east worked this way. | p. 171
term: Standing play | A play kept at one site and given over several days. Lincoln held a standing play of Tobit over two days in the summer in the 1560s. | p. 283
term: Guild | A trade body of a town, such as the Smiths or the Bakers. In the northern cities each guild owned, paid for and acted one play of the series. | p. 172
term: Register | The city's own copy of the guilds' play texts, kept so the clerk could check what was spoken. At York some guilds never registered their texts and others played different words. | p. 172
term: Regenall | The Chester name for the city's master set of play texts, apparently like York's register. Later copies show it was a body of texts from which a cycle could be put together each year. | p. 181
term: Auctor | A Latin word for the person who gathers and shapes a collection of texts, as against an author who writes one consistent work. The book asks whether the Wakefield Master was the auctor of the Towneley manuscript. | p. 175
term: Redactor | A later reviser who edits and adds to an existing text. The book asks whether the second Pilate rant in Towneley was written by one. | p. 177
term: Compilation | A volume made by gathering plays of separate origin and joining them. The N-Town manuscript is one: whole sequences existed on their own before the scribe joined them. | p. 187
term: Rubric | A heading or note written into a manuscript, often in a different ink. In Towneley, two plays carry a rubric naming Wakefield. | p. 174
term: Guild ascription | A note in a manuscript saying which guild played which pageant. Towneley has only five, added in the sixteenth century. | p. 174
term: Banns-crier | A person who went through nearby towns calling out the announcement of a coming play. The Castle of Perseverance text has gaps where the crier names the town of the performance and the towns visited. | p. 254
term: Place | The open ground in the middle of a fixed-stage play, around which the scaffolds stood. Crowds, battles and crucifixions were played there. | p. 188
term: Scaffold | A raised platform built for one group or place in a play, such as Herod, hell or heaven. New Romney paid for eight in its Passion. | p. 188
term: Stage plan | A drawing of where each scaffold and structure stands. The text of the Castle of Perseverance is followed by one, showing a castle in the centre with scaffolds around it. | p. 188
term: Rant | A loud boasting speech by a ruler or tyrant, usually at the start of a play. The Wakefield Master wrote several, for Pilate, Herod's messenger and others. | p. 177
term: Carnivalesque | Having the character of a festival in which rank is turned over, food is plentiful and laughter breaks into serious business. The book applies the word, from Bakhtin, to the shepherds' plays. | p. 177
term: Topsy-turveydom | A reversal in which the low overturn the high. In the Chester Shepherds play the apprentice wins the wrestling match against his masters. | p. 178
term: Grotesque realism | Bakhtin's name for festival humour that dwells on the body, food and death. The book finds it in the shepherds' feast and in Herod's slaughter scene. | p. 178
term: Anticarnival | A feast that keeps its food to itself. The master shepherds hide their food from the apprentice, which refuses the open sharing of a carnival banquet. | p. 178
term: Christmas Lord | A figure chosen to rule the Christmas season in jest, also called a King of Fools. The book asks whether Herod in the Wakefield play is built as one, and the torturers call Christ King Copin. | p. 178
term: Hot Cockles | A game named in the Coliphizacio, where the torturers say they will teach Christ a new play of it. The book takes their talk as the language of a Christmas game. | p. 178
term: Plough play | A folk play of the farming year whose players collect money. The book thinks the Mactacio Abel may be one that has been given a parody of the mass. | p. 280
term: Ludimagister | The Latin word for a school's master of games, a schoolmaster who writes and stages plays. The book wonders whether the Wakefield Master was one. | p. 179
term: Disguising | A court entertainment in which costumed figures arrive, perform a pageant and dance. Wolsey's household staged Schatew Vert as a banquet and pageant-disguising in 1522. | p. 272
term: Revels | The court entertainments of banquets, masks, plays and tournaments. Wolsey used them to flatter and manage the king and foreign ambassadors. | p. 271
term: Royal entry | A staged welcome with speeches and displays when a ruler visited a town. The expense was large, but the texts were seldom printed because the show was thought finished once seen. | p. 193
term: Morality play | A play whose characters stand for qualities, such as World, Flesh and Devil, and which follows a person's fall and return through penance. The book treats the label as a loose one covering plays that differ in length, staging and action. | p. 236
term: Psychomachia | A battle of virtues and vices for a person's soul. In the Castle of Perseverance the Virtues fight the Sins with roses as weapons until Humanum Genus gives way to Covetise. | p. 186
term: Vice | A comic evil character in a morality play. In Mankind the vices are called Nought, New Guise and Nowadays, and they lead Mankind into the frivolity of the season. | p. 192
term: Stock figure | A familiar type that playwrights and audiences both know, such as the tyrant or the disobedient servant. The same types speak in the same voices in biblical, saint and morality plays. | p. 236
term: Tormentor | The stock role of the people who beat and nail Christ, usually played as noisy and crude. New Romney named its tormentors Mischaunce, Untrust and Unhap. | p. 266
term: Personification | Giving a quality a person's body and voice on stage. Caro, who stands for the flesh, is huge, sits in a tower and wears a costume of flowers. | p. 252
term: Allegory | A way of speaking in which one thing is said and another is meant. Medieval writers described it as a distinction between words and meaning. | p. 250
term: Substance and accident | Substance is what all things of a kind share, such as chairness. Accidents are the particular marks that differ, such as colour or number of legs, and show the substance to us. | p. 251
term: Speech tag | A label naming the speaker in front of each speech. Readers keep seeing the abstract name, while a viewer sees a person acting. | p. 249
term: Stage direction | A note in a script telling what is to be done, such as which persons descend with beams of light. N-Town directs three beams passing from Holy Ghost to the Son to the Father. | p. 218
term: Play offered for acting | A printed play written for small groups, with few props and costumes and parts that one actor can double. Four to eight men are enough for the ones the book lists. | p. 190
term: Expositor | A figure who steps out of the action to explain what it means. In the Chester Abraham he closes each of three episodes with the meaning. | p. 184
term: Prolocutor | A speaker who opens and closes a play and talks to the audience. In Bale's Enterlude the Prolocutor tells the people where they stand between the old law and the new. | p. 228
term: Exemplum | A short story or action offered as a pattern for the audience. Mannyng turns Christ's prayer in the garden into an exemplum for all Christians. | p. 221
term: Typology | Reading a person or event as a forerunner of a later one, such as Isaac standing for Christ. The book says the plays use only the simplest sort. | p. 210
term: Literalism | Showing a story exactly as written so that gaps and puzzles in it show too. The book argues that playing a text on stage forced out questions that a commentary would have settled. | p. 213
term: Logic of iteration | Meeting a problem in a story by repeating the main pattern again instead of explaining it. Towneley repeats the plan of providence after Christ's prayer in the garden. | p. 214
term: Counterfeit | To act a sacred rite so closely that it could be taken for the real one. A 1576 order to Wakefield forbade plays in which the sacraments were counterfeited. | p. 224
term: Polemical drama | A play written to push one side of a religious or political dispute. John Bale wrote several for the king's side against the Catholic church. | p. 195
term: Political allegory | A story from one setting that stands for people and events in another. In Godly Queen Hester, Aman stands for Cardinal Wolsey and the Jews for English monks. | p. 199
term: Synchronic reading | Reading works side by side as if from the same moment, instead of in order of date. The book reads Bale's Baptism play beside the three older ones, which may have been played in the same year. | p. 226
term: Evolutionary thesis | The story that medieval drama developed in steps from church liturgy to cycles to moralities to Elizabethan theatre. The book calls it still present in histories, courses and textbooks. | p. 202
term: Antitheatrical tradition | A line of writing that attacks stage play. The book argues England had none against vernacular drama in the Middle Ages, which helped plays last into the sixteenth century. | p. 268
term: Ephemera | Things made for the moment and not kept. Printers treated plays and royal entries this way, so most texts were never printed. | p. 193
term: Inhibition | An official order stopping a performance. In 1572 the archbishop of York sent one to Chester, and the mayor later claimed it arrived too late. | p. 287
term: Licence | Written permission to stage a play. Elizabeth's proclamation of 1559 banned interludes unless notice was given beforehand and a licence issued. | p. 278
term: Corpus Christi | A church feast day on which the northern cities held processions and plays. York was asked in 1426 to move its play to the day after. | p. 204
term: Whitsun | The feast of Pentecost. Chester moved its play there from Corpus Christi and stretched it to three days between 1521 and 1531. | p. 270
term: Shrovetide | The days of festival just before Lent. A reference to February suggests Mankind may have been played then. | p. 192
term: Visus | A mechanical show of a scene. At Lincoln the cathedral's visus showed the Assumption and Coronation of the Virgin and was the main sight of the Saint Anne procession. | p. 302
term: Riding | A yearly show in which a figure such as Saint George, or the months of the year, was ridden through the town, done for the wealth of the church. | p. 304
term: Demonstratio | A Latin word used in records for a display, such as bear baits or ridings of Saint George, and not only for plays. A Shrewsbury entry shows why the book doubts it names a play. | p. 304
term: Mock battle | A pretend fight put on inside a procession. Bristol in 1461 and Norwich in 1471 staged one for Saint George. | p. 301
term: Auspices | The people or body that sponsor and put on a play. The book says assumptions about genre, mode and auspices stop us reading what the playwright did. | p. 237
term: Alternate version | Two texts of the same scene kept for one slot, of which only one is played in a given year. The Chester Shepherds play has two comic readings of the angels' song in different metres. | p. 181
idea: A script is not the performance | What survives on paper tells little about what was said on the day. At York the guilds were told to register their texts, but some never did and others played different words, and the clerk listening at the first stop could not rewrite them into one story. | p. 172
idea: Separate owners leave gaps and repeats | When each guild owns its own play and does not consult the others, the whole run repeats scenes and contradicts itself. York's Passion sequence shows this, and the book reads it as the guilds acting on their own. | p. 172
idea: A collection is not a set that was played | The Towneley manuscript holds five plays copied from York and others from nearby places, so it may never have been performed whole. The compiler may have gathered plays to look like the famous series at York and Coventry. | p. 174
idea: Owning a text is not playing it | A monk named Hyngham owned the Macro plays, but the book notes that collections of play scripts were also a reading genre in monasteries. John Lydgate wrote disguisings and mummings for the court and never a play text. | p. 186
idea: A long piece can be cut into parts played in turn | The N-Town Passion has two sequences meant for alternate years. The Digby Killing of the Children says last year's show was the Shepherds and Magi, this year's is the Purification and Slaughter, and next year's will be Christ and the Doctors. | p. 190
idea: The size of a place sets the size of its play | Only large cities with an organised set of guilds could pay for and man a long series of wagons. Smaller East Anglian towns gave single plays, often Old Testament ones, or cut a big project into smaller units. | p. 189
idea: Costs can be shared and paid back from takings | For the New Romney Passion, richer townspeople lent money for the scaffolds and others gave days of labour. Neighbouring towns also paid in, and the sponsors were repaid from the profit of four days of playing. | p. 189
idea: A play can travel as a text | The Castle of Perseverance needed at least twenty-two actors and a large stage, and its banns leave a blank for the town's name. The book suggests the text moved from town to town while local people played it, and printed plays offered for acting were small enough for one actor to take several parts. | p. 190
idea: Collecting money can be part of the play | Mankind is halted before the devil Titivillus enters so that the actors can collect from the audience. The quête is a mark of mummings and folk plays, and the book reads Mankind as closer to a house entertainment than to an inn show. | p. 191
idea: Clues in a script can be played with | Mankind mentions a hostler and the names of people, so scholars placed it in an inn in Norfolk. The book answers that a host might have written his guests' names into the script for a laugh, so the clues may describe an imagined inn. | p. 192
idea: A play can sit inside a feast and a season | Mankind has vices called Nought and Nowadays, a mock beheading and a rude Christmas carol, then ends with Mercy steering the audience back to Lent. The book reads it as an entertainment that moves from the year-end revel to the sobriety of the coming fast. | p. 192
idea: Print turns a play into a text | Printers worked in London while most plays were played elsewhere, and guilds that owned texts gained nothing by selling them. The book adds that a printed play needs no actors, and that the plays with the longest performance lives, such as the York Creed play, were never printed. | p. 203
idea: A title page can steer how a text is read | Everyman was printed as a Treatise to be read, not a text to be acted. The 1561 printers of Godly Queen Hester called it newly made, drawn from scripture and a lesson in duty, which the book says the text itself does not bear out. | p. 201
idea: The same text can mean different things in different years | The Chester Antichrist stayed nearly unchanged for seventy-five years. In 1500 it told of a figure to come, in Henry's last years the name Antichrist was aimed at pope and king alike, and under Elizabeth lines were added to the banns so an Expositor would name Catholics as the Antichrists. | p. 233
idea: A performance can say more than its script | The Chester Smiths played the Purification in 1572 with a gilded face for the child, a Simeon in church clothing, an altar under a Gothic arch and a repaired crown for the Virgin. Cutting lines from the text would not have removed those meanings. | p. 232
idea: A reader of the script cannot see the gestures | A censor could pass a text that quotes only the gospel words, but the book notes that performers might still act it so as to recall a Catholic rite. Only a censor in the audience would know if the performance strayed from the page. | p. 231
idea: One play can join several modes on purpose | The Digby Mary Magdalene moves between Bible history, allegory and romance and mixes them. The playwright shows Mary's life in sin as allegory with World, Flesh and Devil so that her fall reads as everyone's, then returns to history for her conversion. | p. 239
idea: Leaving a scene out can aim attention | The Magdalene playwright leaves out Mary at the Crucifixion and the Deposition, although the stage could hold them. The book says the Passion is left as a backdrop the audience imagines, which keeps the play on Mary. | p. 243
idea: A large play is a list of structures | The Castle of Perseverance needs a central castle and scaffolds for God and each enemy. The Magdalene adds a tavern, a garden, a rock, a boat, a devil's wagon with hell beneath and a cloud that lifts angels. | p. 188
idea: An abstract character is still a body on stage | Caro, the flesh, says in his first speech that he sits in a tower, wears flowers and decorates it with taffeta. The speech tells the stage builders what to make, so the quality appears as a large, costumed person with painted cloths. | p. 252
idea: A name for a quality can be real to the audience | Medieval thinkers held that a quality such as humanity is real and shows itself through particular people, in the way chairness shows in different chairs. On that view a character named Anger or Charity is not a thin idea but the real thing seen through its marks. | p. 251
idea: Shared stock figures make plays readable | Caesar, Herod and Pilate speak alike, and the boy servants in the Magdalene talk like the shepherds' servants in the cycles. Playwrights and audiences both knew how tyrants and apprentices behave, so a new play could use them at once. | p. 236
idea: Folk games can be built into a serious play | The Wakefield Master puts a rebellious servant, a plough-play opening, a parody of the Gloria and a shared feast into plays about Christ. The book reads these as chosen uses of festival play, not as a decline from the sacred. | p. 177
idea: Food shared with the audience changes the scene | The shepherds in the Wakefield plays first hide their food from their apprentice, then produce more than they could eat. At Chester the Bakers threw loaves out to the crowd, as favours are thrown at Mardi Gras. | p. 178
idea: Merging two lists damages both | In York's Christ and the Doctors the Two Precepts and the last eight commandments are joined into one list, and the Decalogue no longer holds its shape. Chester gives the seventh commandment twice, and Coventry swaps the ninth for a rule against oaths. | p. 212
idea: Acting out a story raises questions the story leaves unsaid | In the garden scene Christ asks that the cup pass, which invites the question why he does not know the plan. York has an angel explain, Chester stresses one will, N-Town shows a chalice and host, and Towneley repeats the plan of providence. | p. 219
idea: What a maker includes and omits shows whose interests it serves | The biblical plays leave out most of the church's required lists, such as the seven virtues and the seven sacraments, because they are stories, not teaching sheets. The book argues that what the lay writers brought in and left out defines lay religious life. | p. 208
idea: A representation can create a staging problem | The Chester Late Banns says God cannot be fitted to a man's face and that gilding the face disfigures the actor, so God is to come as a voice from a cloudy covering. Another note asks how the Chester Fall can make God vanish so Lucifer can take the throne. | p. 224
idea: Words can be kept while the act is changed | At Chester the Last Supper reports the gospel words of the bread and wine, and the Banns tell the Bakers to speak the same words Christ spoke, so the scene counts as a memorial and not a counterfeit. The book adds that gestures and props could still carry Catholic meaning. | p. 225
idea: A new play answers old ones played beside it | Bale's Enterlude of John the Baptist was written when the York, N-Town and Towneley Baptism plays could be played the same year. The book reads it as a reply to them, with Bale standing in front as presenter and explainer. | p. 226
idea: A play may stay inside the line between who may teach and who may urge | Only a bishop or someone he licenses may preach, while any layperson may urge a neighbour to penance. The book argues that lay producers kept their plays to moral urging and avoided technical doctrine, such as the Trinity. | p. 205
idea: A ban can fix a season | Proclamations from 1549 and 1559 forbid English interludes between May or August and All Saints, the months of church ales, Robin Hood days and summer games. The book reads this as marking an approved play season and shutting down the commoners' one. | p. 278
idea: One ban is given several reasons | London's rules against plays are justified by services on holy days, apprentices wasting their masters' goods, sedition and plague. The book suggests the fear of crowds spreading sickness covered a fear of speech spreading too. | p. 279
idea: Audience members who step into the play break it | Thomas More notes that everyone knows the sultan in a stage play is a cobbler, but a spectator who says so aloud may have his head broken by a tormentor. People of low rank at the foot of the scaffold look on, and those who step up and fail to play their parts disorder the play. | p. 281
idea: Old and new play run side by side | The last Chester cycle was played in 1575, a year before James Burbage built the Theatre in London, and Coventry kept annual plays until 1579. The book argues that the divide between medieval and Renaissance drama is imposed later. | p. 293
idea: Rising cost wears down a custom | In Beverley, Coventry, Lincoln and Norwich the ruling guilds made other guilds pay for plays and processions without taking part. The book says this tax antagonised people and shows the plays had become a burden before the church or crown acted. | p. 286
idea: A play can be taken as a picture of a real person | Cardinal Wolsey had John Roo arrested after a Gray's Inn play about a lord governor ruled by dissipation, which he took to be himself. Roo answered that it had been written twenty years earlier. | p. 273
idea: A staged debate can end without a winner | Wolsey's Love and Riches pageant of 1527 has speakers argue whether love or riches is the greater, knights defend each side at the bar, and an old man ends it by saying both are needed. | p. 272
idea: A stage villain can be shown as an actor | In Bale's Enterlude the Pharisee and Sadducee pretend to seek truth, and John says they play wicked parts. The true figure, like John, feigns nothing, since he acts in the present and leads the audience into the new age. | p. 229
idea: The same sign can be read two ways as a scene proceeds | In Wisdom, Anima enters in white cloth of gold under a black mantle, which first suggests a fallen soul. As the dialogue goes on, the mantle is read as original sin, and the audience learns she is pure and has not yet sinned. | p. 264
idea: A play need not say where or when it is | The opening of Wisdom is set in heaven before the soul is born, though the audience is not told so. They learn it only in the temptation scene, which the book says has caused scholars to argue over who staged the play. | p. 263
idea: A space where a play is given is negotiated | The book says lay producers of biblical plays took positions normally held by clergy, and that the clergy for a long time let the lay authority stand. Both sides gave and took over the ground where the plays were given. | p. 204
idea: The record shows what happened every year, not what was special | There is almost no record of performances of the Castle, Mankind or Wisdom, but ales, processions, boy bishops, hockings, king games and Robin Hood games are recorded yearly all over England. The book concludes that moral plays were occasional and rare. | p. 202
idea: Plays and printing run on different clocks | Most early printed plays appeared ten or twenty years after they were written and first played. Bale's plays of 1538 were printed in 1547 and 1548, after his exile, because they had to wait for a safe moment. | p. 195
idea: A text can be changed while the rules stay | Chester's Abraham play, probably written in the 1560s or 1570s, adds a bread and wine scene with Melchisedech and a ruling on circumcision, so the old story carries the new church's two sacraments. | p. 184
idea: One writer going through a whole set gives it one purpose | Most cycles use many verse forms and have no overall plan, but at Chester a late playwright added a single metre throughout, so the cycle shows a sharper aim, such as stress on the old law in the Old Testament plays. | p. 182
idea: A choice between two versions can be put to an official | In 1575 the Chester guild brought two versions of a play to the mayor for him to choose between. The same city's register let officials check what a guild proposed to play. | p. 182
