term: Characteristic | A general feature that describes what sort of game it is, such as how many players it takes, how long it lasts or how much luck it has. It is the kind of thing a friend asks before agreeing to play. | p. 3
term: Orthogame | A game for two or more players, with rules that rank or weigh the players, played for entertainment. Winners and losers, scores or finishing times all count as ranking. | p. 8
term: Systemic | Describes a characteristic that depends mainly on the game as a system, for example on its rules. | p. 8
term: Agential | Describes a characteristic that depends mainly on the people who play, so it can change without any change to the rules. | p. 8
term: Atom | The smallest complete unit of play that players feel is really playing the game, such as two possessions in football or one level of Donkey Kong. | p. 13
term: Game (unit of play) | The standard full round of play, usually from a set starting position to the point where a winner is decided. | p. 13
term: Session | One continuous period of play, such as an evening. | p. 13
term: Campaign | A series of games or sessions that are linked in some way, such as a weekly poker night, a sports season or an ongoing role-playing game. | p. 13
term: Match | A series of games agreed on as enough play to settle who the winner is, often a win of two games out of three. | p. 13
term: Zero-player game | Something that is watched like a game but where nobody influences the result, such as Conway's Life or a race between two raindrops on a window. | p. 22
term: Pure one-player game | A game where the player plays against the system and not against an imagined opponent. Crossword puzzles, Tetris and card solitaire are examples. | p. 22
term: One and a half player game | A game where one human plays and the computer plays simulated opponents, as when playing Starcraft against the AI. | p. 22
term: Two-sided team game | A game with two teams, each acting as one side that wins or loses together, such as football or bridge. It behaves much like a two-player game. | p. 23
term: One-sided team game | A game where one side of several players plays against the rules or an AI. It is usually called a cooperative game. | p. 23
term: Multiplayer game | In this book, a game with at least three sides. Two-player games and two-team games are not counted. | p. 21
term: Massively multiplayer game | A game where the number of people a player deals with is much smaller than the number playing, such as World of Warcraft or pari-mutuel betting. | p. 24
term: Sweet spot | The number of players that most often suits a game that allows a range of player counts. | p. 24
term: Replayability | How well a game holds up when played again. Single-player games tend to use up their content faster than multiplayer ones. | p. 26
term: Heuristic | A rule of thumb that helps a player play, such as develop your pieces in chess. Players talk about heuristics after a game more than about rules. | p. 29
term: Positional heuristic | A rule of thumb for judging the state of the game and telling who is ahead, such as counting piece values in chess. | p. 30
term: Directional heuristic | A rule of thumb that tells a player what strategy or move to follow, such as control the centre squares. | p. 30
term: Zero-level heuristic | A beginner's rule of thumb that gives a new player some idea of what to try, such as win a trick with the lowest card that will do it. | p. 35
term: Climbing the heuristics tree | Learning more refined rules of thumb for a game over time. For serious players this learning is much of the pleasure. | p. 32
term: Solved game | A game where the rules of thumb are so complete that a player can never miss the winning move, as in tic-tac-toe or Nim. | p. 33
term: Zugzwang | A position where every available move leaves the player lower placed than doing nothing would. The book uses it more widely for positions where most moves cost the player something and helpful ones are rare. | p. 33
term: Race | A multiplayer game built from several copies of a one-player game, one for each player, such as a footrace, golf or Scrabble. | p. 38
term: Brawl | A multiplayer game made by adding players to a two-player game that cannot be reduced to one player, such as Risk. Players hurt each other directly. | p. 38
term: Strict elimination | Being completely out of the game while the game goes on for others. | p. 38
term: Logical elimination | Having no chance of winning but still having to play, which sports call mathematical elimination. | p. 38
term: Effective elimination | Having a chance of winning that is very small but not zero, so the player feels out of it. | p. 39
term: Playing for points | Playing to score as much as possible, or for second place, after having no chance to win. | p. 43
term: Interactivity | The ability of players to influence the progress of other players. A boxing match or chess is highly interactive, a footrace almost not at all. | p. 44
term: Targeted interaction | An interaction where the acting player chooses which other player it lands on. The effect can help the target, as in trading, and not only harm. | p. 46
term: Press-your-luck strategy | A reaction to a race where a player who is ahead plays safe and a player who is behind takes bigger risks. Yahtzee and Can't Stop have it. | p. 46
term: Chip-taking game | A game where each turn a player may remove a resource from a chosen opponent and the last one left wins. Skill in the rest of the game hardly matters. | p. 48
term: Politics | In this book, players choosing others to target in arbitrary ways that affect their positions differently. It grows with both the amount of interaction and the freedom to choose targets. | p. 50
term: Voting game | A game with so much politics that the players are in effect electing the winner by choosing who to hurt, whatever the rules say about skill. | p. 50
term: Pick on the leader | A common move in political games where the other players all attack whoever is ahead. It works as a catch-up feature. | p. 54
term: Fall on the grenade | Making a sacrifice to stop the leader from winning. Players may argue that someone else ought to do it, or wait so that another player is forced to. | p. 51
term: Kingmaking | A player with no chance of winning choosing which of two contenders will win, by choosing whom to affect. It needs targeted interaction and a player who is logically eliminated. | p. 56
term: Blind kingmaking | A player out of contention who changes who wins by random choices, without favouring any one contender. A low-skilled player in a poker pot does this. | p. 58
term: Role (team) | The part a game gives a team member, which sets how that player can contribute. Roles can be written in the rules or only a habit of play. | p. 62
term: Cooperative interaction | How much team members can affect each other's performance. A swim relay team has little, a football pass has a lot. | p. 64
term: Role usurpation | A teammate telling another exactly what to do, so that the second player's part is taken over. Turn-based cooperative games are open to it. | p. 66
term: Cooperative game | A game where all players are on one team and win or lose together. The book treats it as a mix of a single-player game and a team game. | p. 62
idea: A game is whatever people call a game | The authors reject a tight definition of the word game, since the things called games share only overlapping family likenesses, as Wittgenstein said. They ask how gamelike or how political a thing is, not whether it is one. Of Risk they ask how political it is. | p. 5
idea: Characteristics are the questions a friend asks | A game is most usefully described by features like length, player count and luck, because those tell a possible player more than the rules do. Calling something a card game tells you about length and luck at once, and a game of thin cardboard pieces that is nothing like a card game would feel like a trick. | p. 3
idea: Some qualities belong to the players, not the rules | Length, favourite player count and speed depend on who plays. Mancala is played very fast in Africa and slower by Europeans, and chess is slow in the West but quick in the East. A game can change without a rule changing. | p. 8
idea: Evolved games solve problems that designed games keep meeting | Classic games have been weeded out over long play, so they often show solutions to problems that new designed games run into. Politics has been largely evolved out of classic games, which are mostly two-sided or low in interaction. | p. 4
idea: Short atoms let a session end well | Players like to end a session by finishing a satisfying unit of play. Poker hands and basketball possessions are short, so people stop when they like. A chess game has no unit shorter than the whole game, so it is hard to squeeze into a spare half hour. | p. 14
idea: Points make atoms satisfying | Games that record points at the end of each short unit let each unit feel like a small complete game. Chess and go decide only at the end, so they have no such unit, even though go has points. | p. 20
idea: Long games turn into campaigns | Designers often add content to please serious fans and the games grow, with the atoms getting longer too. Short play then becomes harder to find, and a gap opens for newer shorter games. | p. 19
idea: A short game costs the player nothing | A short game can be played again for a longer experience, but a long game cannot easily be made short. Poker and basketball do not suffer from short atoms. | p. 20
idea: The number of sides matters more than the number of players | Football has many players but two sides, so it acts like a two-player game. Politics and kingmaking only arise with three or more sides. | p. 21
idea: Single-player games run out of content | With no human opponent the surprise has to come from a random setup, hidden information or a simulated opponent. A crossword is spent once it is solved, while a card solitaire deal makes a fresh puzzle each time. | p. 25
idea: Single-player games let the designer pick the win rate | Because there is only one side, a designer can aim for players winning about 70 percent of the time, which two-player games cannot do. Difficulty levels let players choose their own rate. | p. 26
idea: Changing the player count changes the game | Scrabble for two and for four play differently, because blocking an opponent and setting up the board pay off differently. Turning a two-player game into one for three or more has many unintended effects. | p. 27
idea: A game needs rules of thumb to be a game | If a game has no heuristics the players can find, it is random for them and they might as well roll dice. Guess the Digit of pi is the extreme example. | p. 29
idea: Telling who is ahead and knowing what to do are separate | Money in the Bag has a known winning strategy but nobody can see who is ahead until the end. Money on the Table lets you see the score but gives no clue which button pays more often. Winning play usually needs both kinds of rule of thumb. | p. 31
idea: Rules of thumb that are too powerful end a game | Nim and tic-tac-toe have heuristics that tell you everything, so once known the game is solved and dull. Rules of thumb that suit a game are clear, rich and satisfying, and need not be powerful. | p. 32
idea: Beginners need an easy first rule of thumb | In Uno a beginner can even out suits. In go and many European boardgames with many equal options a beginner cannot tell what to do and may give up. Low-powered cards in a trading card game give new players choices they can make. | p. 35
idea: The same game has different rules of thumb at different skill levels | Chess is friendly to beginners and advanced beginners but intermediate players mostly avoid obvious blunders and wait for a mistake. In go the intermediate player finds many moves that help a bit. | p. 33
idea: Rules of thumb change as the player base learns | Tic-tac-toe has decent heuristics for small children and few useful ones for adults. Dots and Boxes was played by schoolchildren at random until mathematicians found productive early-game play. | p. 35
idea: Races and brawls are two ways to make a multiplayer game | A race glues together copies of a one-player game, so players barely affect one another. A brawl adds players to a two-player game, so they hurt one another and eliminate one another. Many multiplayer features follow from which one the game is. | p. 38
idea: Being out of the game is not the most costly outcome | Playing on with no chance of winning is often more costly to the player than being strictly out, since an eliminated player can rest. How long until the next game starts decides how much it hurts. In poker the next hand starts quickly, in Monopoly it may take an hour. | p. 41
idea: Three ways to handle elimination | A game can knock players out for ever, leave them in with no chance, or give everyone a chance until the end. The first upsets the player knocked out, the second leaves them to disrupt others, and the third may make only the last part of the game matter. | p. 42
idea: Tracking points removes the elimination problem | When each subgame or hand scores points, even a losing player cares about how much they lose by. Money in poker does the same after the game is over. | p. 42
idea: A losing player can still play for something | Players with no chance of winning often play for points or for second place if the game has a simple measure like score or distance. Groups expect this, and dislike it when a player instead steers the result. | p. 43
idea: Concession is a social rule | Chess has a culture of resigning that solved the problem of hopeless games, and breaking it in a tournament can cause a scandal. Online, players who do not concede may hide a building in a strategy game to drag the game on. | p. 40
idea: Let the winner remove a hidden loser | Where losing players might drag a game out, a rule that reveals the hidden buildings of a player with no army or economy turns a logically eliminated player into a strictly eliminated one. | p. 41
idea: Winners can be the ones eliminated | In Old Maid and in footraces the first to finish drops out and the potential losers carry on. The reward of winning softens the annoyance of sitting out. | p. 42
idea: A race can be made interactive by adding a rule | Mario Kart's shells and the blockades and sending pieces home in Parcheesi add interaction to a race. Without such additions a race has little politics. | p. 46
idea: Interaction with no choice of target is not politics | In poker you can only take chips from players who choose to play against you, so it is not a chip-taking game. In Risk you can only attack those next to you. Limits on who can be hit limit politics. | p. 60
idea: Skill stops mattering when everyone can target the leader | In the chip-taking game with chess matches, Kasparov is no likelier to win because others pick him first. The real skill becomes persuading others to attack someone else. | p. 48
idea: Political play follows the same pattern in every game | Lying low, begging, threatening revenge, offering favours outside the game and waiting to mop up appear whatever the mechanics are. With enough targeted interaction, many games become the same game. | p. 51
idea: Politics wipes out ways to read the position | If any player can be picked on by group choice, ordinary measures of who is ahead stop telling you much, and so do ordinary strategic rules of thumb. Only social guesses about who will do what to whom stay useful. | p. 52
idea: A move that is clearly forced causes fewer arguments | If three players have two chips each and one removes a chip from another, there are complaints. If one player has eight chips and is about to win, taking his chip is accepted. | p. 53
idea: Politics can let lower-skilled players join higher-skilled ones | In free-for-all Magic or Starcraft a low-skilled player can survive because the high-skilled players attract attention. This widens the pool of people who can play together, which matters most for families. | p. 54
idea: Simple games suit political play | Diplomacy needs no detailed combat tables, because negotiation and betrayal would wash out any such detail. Complex mechanics tend to be lost under politics. | p. 54
idea: Picking on the leader can be a mistake | In the balloon game with hit chances of 60, 50 and 40 percent, B has a 19 percent chance of winning by aiming at the most accurate thrower and 23 percent by aiming at the least accurate. Instinct says to go for the leader, but that can hurt the attacker. | p. 55
idea: Hold back and nobody fights | If everyone waits for the others to wear each other down, the game stalls. Rewards for attacking, such as taking resources or cards in Risk, or hero experience in Warcraft III, give players a reason to fight. | p. 60
idea: Kingmaking survives even when incentives exist | Giving players reasons to attack one player over another cuts politics, but a player who is out of contention no longer cares about those reasons. It needs its own controls, such as keeping losers from having much influence. | p. 61
idea: Kingmaking is resented because it overwrites everything | A single choice by a player out of the running decides the winner, and the skill and luck of the contenders count for nothing. Fantasy sports leagues have a commissioner to approve trades for this reason. | p. 57
idea: Players need to feel they contribute to a team | A team game works when each member can see their effect. Designers either balance the roles or give each role something unique, like healing or teleportation in World of Warcraft. | p. 63
idea: Roles can sit on top of rules | Basketball positions have no special rules, and World of Warcraft tank, healer and damage dealer sit on top of its written classes. Roles that players make up can matter as much as those the rules give. | p. 63
idea: More dependence on teammates means more anger at them | A swim relay lets you do your utmost whatever your team does. In football or Defense of the Ancients a teammate who drops the ball or dies often costs you the game, so players turn hostile to beginners. | p. 65
idea: Bridge cuts off table talk on purpose | Partners may not say what they hold, but have to signal through bids and plays. Once the dummy is down, the information flow stops, since otherwise the more skilled player would just tell the partner what to do. | p. 66
term: Skilled control | A physical skill such as aiming or jumping that an adviser cannot simply tell someone to do. It stops an expert from playing for a beginner. | p. 67
term: First-order rule | A rule everyone needs to know to play or follow the game, such as how pieces move in chess. | p. 74
term: Second-order rule | A rule that needs to be known for the game to happen but that a player may not need to know, such as the fine punishments for breaking rules in a chess tournament. | p. 74
term: Blind test | Handing a new game's rulebook and parts to players who know nothing about it, with nobody else in the room, to see whether they can learn it. | p. 75
term: Standard | A commonly accepted pattern players already know, such as the fifty-two-card deck, taking turns, or the WASD keys. It can be a small detail or a whole genre. | p. 76
term: Bundle of standards | A group of standards that come together in a genre, such as track, piece and dice in track boardgames. Knowing the bundle lets players start an unknown game quickly. | p. 78
term: Dedicated deck | A deck made for one card game, such as Uno or Rook, as opposed to the standard fifty-two-card deck. | p. 81
term: Genre | A group of similar games, at any level from boardgames down to real-time strategy, whose shared features are standards for those who know the group. | p. 78
term: Nonorthogame | A game with no formally defined winners and losers, where players set their own goals, such as an MMO or a sandbox game. | p. 82
term: Unique loser | An outcome where one player loses and everyone else counts as a winner, as in Old Maid. | p. 83
term: Scaled performance | An outcome where the game gives each player a score, as points or as time, and not only a win or loss. | p. 83
idea: Rules are not the game | The rules of basketball have changed many times and it is still basketball, as Little Red Riding Hood stays the same story with different words. Rules are often changed to keep play the way it was, as when a card is banned in Magic. | p. 73
idea: Rules can be a barrier to play | Every first-order rule has to be learned before play starts, so too many of them keep people out. Few people learn from a rulebook longer than a page. Most learn from someone who already knows the game. | p. 75
idea: Fewer rules for the same play | The same play with fewer rules is preferred to more rules, and adding a rule to fix each small problem builds up a messy game. First-order rules cost most, and rules that players have to judge themselves cost more than ones a referee or computer judges. | p. 76
idea: A computer can hold the rules for you | Players of a computer game can begin with little knowledge because the machine decides what is allowed. Clear rules for the player's own parts still matter, though, while rules for non-player parts may stay vague. | p. 75
idea: A penalty can turn a ban into a price | If a rule says that doing x causes y, players may do x on purpose and pay y. A time limit meant to speed up a game can make players use all the allowed time. | p. 76
idea: Teach first-order rules first | A teacher gives a new player only the rules everyone needs, and some rules of thumb. A beginner in chess may know piece values before knowing en passant. | p. 74
idea: Innovate only where it pays | Players enjoy a comfortable game and always like convenience. A new idea that is no more useful than the old one is harder to learn and probably rougher. Successful games usually have only one or two new things. | p. 77
idea: The polished later game often wins | Warcraft followed Dune II, the Pokemon trading card game followed Magic, and World of Warcraft followed Everquest. The first game with a new idea rarely gets its details right. | p. 78
idea: Standards teach the game for free | A player who sees a first-person view with a gun in the picture reaches for the WASD keys. Someone who knows one poker game can pick up another fast, and rules of thumb carry over as well. | p. 81
idea: A standard may stay for pacing, not for fit | Mining resources to build units in strategy games came from one setting but stayed because it paces the game and gives players an economy against army trade-off to think about. Following a standard also shortens the designer's balancing work. | p. 80
idea: Break a standard for a reason | Bicycle racing has time trials because drafting makes a mass start measure team help and not individual strength. Taking away health bars in a shooter to feel more real upset players who expected them. | p. 79
idea: Dedicated decks come from business as well as play | Games like Uno often could be played with a standard deck. Printing a special deck became easy, and a company can own a special deck but cannot sell rules alone. | p. 81
idea: One game can hold several games that share standards | Hex wargame scenarios, draft and sealed formats in Magic and different battlegrounds in World of Warcraft can be seen as separate games on shared standards. They reuse what the player has already mastered. | p. 81
idea: Players of open-ended games write their own goals | In an MMO a player may chase levels, gold, friends, looks or helping beginners. Friends playing Hacky Sack may keep the bag up or try stunts. Satisfaction depends on goals the player chose. | p. 83
idea: Outcomes come in kinds | A game can end with one winner, one loser, a winning alliance, a ranking, or a score of points or time. The kinds can combine, as when a footrace ranks players by their times. | p. 83
idea: Advice can be stopped by hidden information or time | An expert cannot run a beginner's turn if the beginner holds hidden cards, if there is no time to give orders, or if the game is played in character. The book lists these with skilled control and limits on talking as ways to keep each player in the game. | p. 68
idea: Cooperative games came with the computer | An opponent gives uncertainty and replay, so people used one when they could find one. The computer supplies an opponent and a network supplies teammates, so players can team up against the machine and win more than half the time. | p. 68
idea: A cooperative game lacks someone to object to bossing | In bridge the opponents object if a partner tells the other what to do. In Pandemic nobody objects but the player being bossed, who can only say they would rather play. | p. 68
term: Draw | An outcome where the rules name no winner. Chess is the classic case, and some games add rules such as a penalty shootout to prevent it. | p. 85
term: Komi | A point bonus given to one player in go, usually 7 and a half to white, to offset black's advantage of moving first. The half point stops ties. | p. 85
term: Victory condition | What a player needs to do to win. Either achieving it ends the game, or an independent end condition occurs and the winner is whoever is in the right state then. | p. 86
term: Ending condition | An event that ends the game, such as the tiles running out or nine innings passing, after which the winner is worked out. | p. 86
term: Points | Numerical scores given to each player, usually with the highest score winning, though the lowest wins in golf and hearts. | p. 87
term: Dual-track point system | A scoring design with one track that decides who wins and another that can be spent for power in the game, such as victory points and money in European boardgames. | p. 89
term: Multiple victory conditions | Several different ways to win in one game, such as reducing life to zero or emptying the deck in Magic. | p. 90
term: Asymmetric victory conditions | Different players needing different things to win, as in a wargame where the historically losing side wins by holding out. | p. 91
term: Positional asymmetry | A difference in the starting state of players, such as moving first, a different character class or seat order, that could change the likely result if two equally skilled players swapped. | p. 92
term: Pole position | The preferred starting place in a race, named in auto racing. | p. 95
idea: Winning is partly agreed by the players | The rules may give a points track, but one group plays only for the winner and another plays for place or points. In Starcraft the game gives a score, yet nearly everyone plays only to eliminate the other side. | p. 84
idea: The kind of outcome shapes play | With a unique winner, players take wild chances near the end and picking on the leader appears. With a unique loser, players tend to pick on whoever is behind. Where first and second place score the same, people still compete for first. | p. 84
idea: A game where everyone can lose is awkward | In Nuclear War a player has to decide whether second place is preferable to everyone losing. If enough of the group prefers the second, games end with everyone losing. | p. 84
idea: Long games get called off early | Groups often agree to stop a long multiplayer boardgame when some players are out, and treat the remaining players as winners ranked informally by score. | p. 85
idea: Real value for points reduces kingmaking | A poker player in fifth place has little reason to give away his chips so that the second-place player comes first. Money makes the value of lower places clear, so players out of the running have something concrete to play for. | p. 85
idea: Draws fit low-luck games | In a game of little luck, a draw can fairly show that the sides were evenly matched. In a game with a lot of luck, letting luck decide the winner feels natural. | p. 85
idea: Easy draws spoil the ending | If a player in trouble can force a draw easily, as in tic-tac-toe or chess, games rarely reach a satisfying finish. In children's games, a draw can be welcome because it invites another game. | p. 86
idea: Who controls the ending matters | When players can influence when the end condition is met, winners rush to end the game and losers try to delay it. Watch for losers who delay forever and for tedious sums that leaders make about when to end it. | p. 87
idea: Games grow scores even when the rules have none | Chess pieces have point values that players use to tell who is ahead, because a single number says who is winning and by how much. | p. 88
idea: A game with no ending can be given one | Tetris and arcade games are played until you lose, so some add a goal such as beating a boss. After winning, the game restarts but harder, as in Donkey Kong or Diablo. | p. 88
idea: Complex games have many point tracks | An MMO has gold, experience, reputation, craft skill and even gear tiers, and the player picks which to pursue. | p. 88
idea: Points that are spendable invite playing for points | Given the choice, most people take being up 200 dollars at poker over being up 100 when everyone else is lower. Marathon runners care about beating four hours more than finishing 163rd instead of 167th. | p. 88
idea: One spendable point track tends to snowball | If points can be spent to gain more points, whoever leads spends their way further ahead. Splitting the track into money for power and victory points for winning breaks this loop. | p. 89
idea: Splitting power from victory has side effects | The player behind in victory points may hold power to close the gap. Players face a choice between a quick win and building up. The loser can say they were close, and can claim most wood as a consolation. | p. 90
idea: Change the victory condition to fix a play problem | If armies hang back, winning by holding a central area for some turns forces a fight. A Monopoly-like game that ends only when all but one are bankrupt can be changed to reaching a sum of money. The fixes cost extra complexity. | p. 90
idea: Several ways to win act like several games | Magic and Age of Empires each offer different routes to victory, adding depth and replay. Players have to learn each, and designers find it harder to keep two routes both viable than two pieces. | p. 91
idea: Rare alternate wins exist for completeness | In reversi a player can win early by turning every piece their colour, and in go a triple ko can end in a draw. These come up so rarely that they are in the rules for logical completeness. | p. 91
idea: Different goals can even out unequal sides | A wargame can start with one side far ahead and let the other win by surviving for a number of turns. In HORSE, the more skilled player starts with letters against them and needs five shots while the other needs three. | p. 91
idea: Almost every game starts unevenly | Moving first in chess, seat order at a poker table or a duel between different character classes are all asymmetries. Such effects often wash out at lower skill levels, and moving first in Monopoly hardly seems to matter. | p. 93
idea: Few games are fully symmetric | Only games where everyone moves at once with the same options, like rock-paper-scissors, have none. Marathons are nearly symmetric because the race is long enough that the start barely matters. | p. 93
idea: Designers use asymmetry on purpose | Asymmetric starts bring variety and replay, as in Cosmic Encounter, Magic, Starcraft and RPG classes. Single-player RPGs are built on complete asymmetry between player and monsters. | p. 93
idea: Rotate the advantage | Football teams take turns kicking off, deals rotate in card games, chess matches alternate black and white, and go gives komi for moving second. Short games, or a campaign of many games, also wash an advantage out. | p. 94
idea: Equipment is a kind of asymmetry | Sports allow own equipment within limits, and some objections to Magic come from deck-building as equipment. Stock-car racing and sealed-deck Magic were created to limit the advantage of equipment. | p. 94
idea: Going after a low-skilled player helps | In three-player Scrabble, playing right after a low-skilled player is a big advantage, because that player gives you scoring chances. In hearts and poker, seat relative to low-skilled players is a hidden asymmetry too. | p. 95
idea: Asymmetry marks the difference between one and one and a half players | Thinking of the computer as a player, a highly asymmetric game feels like a pure one-player game and a symmetric one feels like a one and a half player game. | p. 95
term: Interface | All the ways a game sends information to a player and takes the player's choices back. Every game has one, from a chess piece to a mouse click. | p. 97
term: Floating combat text | Damage numbers that rise from a target's head in a computer game. The book treats them as the screen's version of watching dice roll. | p. 99
term: Control feel | How a player's input becomes action in the game, from the heft of a chess piece to the bounce of Q*bert. Physics games have a pronounced feel. | p. 100
term: Abstract subgame | A simpler game that sits inside a bigger game, such as rock-paper-scissors among unit types in a strategy game. | p. 101
term: Essential game | The simple abstract game a larger game is a disguised version of, such as a chip-taking game. | p. 105
term: Explicit subgame | A distinct game with its own rules inside a larger game, like the tactical map battle in Titan or the minigames in Mario Party. | p. 103
term: Staged game | A game with stages so distinct they are almost separate games, such as bidding then play in bridge or drafting then play in Magic. | p. 103
term: Implicit subgame | A hidden rock-paper-scissors relationship among game elements, such as melee beating ranged, ranged beating flying, and flying beating melee. | p. 103
term: Rush-eco-turtle | A strategy-game triangle where a fast attack beats an economic buildup, the buildup beats a defensive posture, and the defence beats the attack. | p. 104
term: Resource commitment game | A subgame where players secretly split a fixed number of points across several areas and compare, winning the areas where they have more. | p. 104
term: Catch-up feature | A rule meant to help players who are behind close the gap, such as the shells in Mario Kart. | p. 106

idea: Blackjack rewards skill against the house | It is unusual among house games because with flawless play the player is favoured, and the house relies on how hard it is to play without a flaw. | p. 275
idea: Nim is random for a beginner | A new player cannot tell one move from another until close to the end, so they play at random, and once the strategy is known every move but one is equal. | p. 284
idea: Cosmic Encounter shares wins on purpose | Each turn a random opponent is attacked, which stops lasting alliances, and more than one player can win together. Dune has a complex set of victory conditions that let groups share a win. | p. 278
idea: Settlers of Catan uses typical European design | A light theme, random elements, victory points that differ from the resources used for actions, a random board and several routes to victory. | p. 281
idea: Rules that change each deal | In Barbu the scoring changes every deal in a set order, so every round asks for a different way to play. | p. 275
idea: One stage of a game can differ from another | Skitgubbe has a stage where cards are collected and one where they are got rid of, which work completely differently. Pinochle has bidding, melding and trick taking. | p. 277
term: Sandbox game | A game with no set objectives where the player tries actions and sees the results, such as SimCity. | p. 290
term: Single elimination | A tournament format where a player is out as soon as they lose a match, and the last one left wins. | p. 299
term: Double elimination | A tournament format where a player is out after losing two matches. | p. 299
term: Round robin | A tournament format where every player plays every other, and the top record wins. It suits small numbers of players. | p. 299
term: Swiss | A tournament format where after each round, players with the same record are paired against each other. | p. 299
term: Seeding | Assigning tournament pairings from existing rankings and not at random. | p. 299
term: Qualifier | A tournament or race meant to pick a group who go on to another, where entrants care only about getting in and not about the final place. | p. 298
term: Pari-mutuel betting | Betting where all bets go into one pool and payouts depend on how much was bet on each racer, so the odds are set by the bettors. | p. 298
term: Rating system | A set of numbers for each player showing skill. Point accumulation depends on how often you play. Systems like Elo add and subtract points by expected result. | p. 298
term: Werewolf (Mafia) | A group game where secretly chosen werewolves kill townsfolk by night, and everyone votes to kill one suspect by day. A moderator coordinates. | p. 300
term: Twenty Questions | A guessing game where one player thinks of something and others find it with twenty yes-or-no questions. | p. 300
term: Killer (Assassin) | A live game where each player has one target to eliminate and inherits the victim's target, and the last player left wins. | p. 297
idea: A game can be a thought experiment | The authors made up toy games, such as the chip-taking game, die-rolling chess and the balloon game, to isolate one feature at a time. They are not meant to be played for entertainment. | p. 296
idea: Scoring by point accumulation rewards frequent play | A rating built by adding points per win mostly shows how often someone plays. Systems that also take points for a loss measure skill more accurately but can discourage players. | p. 298
idea: Elo gives the odds from two ratings | The winner takes from the loser the same number of points, more for beating a higher-rated player, and any two ratings give the odds of each winning. | p. 298
idea: Tournament formats suit different games | Round robin works for few players. Swiss pairs similar records. A mix, such as Swiss then single elimination among the top eight, is common. A season is a tournament spread over months. | p. 299
idea: Werewolf lives on hidden roles and talk | Teams are random and secret, the werewolves kill one townsperson each night, and all vote on a suspect by day. It has two sides, so it has no politics in the book's sense even though it has a lot of discussion. | p. 300
idea: Game shows use few contestants and a final vote | Survivor lets previously eliminated players vote on the last two, which makes it an extreme case of kingmaking. | p. 299
idea: A first game can be a race with a theme added | Senet, perhaps the oldest known boardgame from at least 3500 BC, is presumed a race. The Royal Game of Goose, one of the first commercial boardgames, put a theme on a race. | p. 273
