term: Goal | The specific outcome players work to reach. It holds their attention and gives them a sense of purpose. | ch. 1
term: Rules | Limits on how players may reach the goal. They close off the obvious routes, so players try routes they had not explored. | ch. 1
term: Feedback system | Whatever tells players how close they are to the goal: points, levels, a score, a progress bar, or just knowing when the game ends. | ch. 1
term: Voluntary participation | Everyone playing knows and accepts the goal, rules and feedback, and may enter or leave freely. | ch. 1
term: Unnecessary obstacle | A difficulty that players take on although an easier route exists, such as swinging a club at a golf ball instead of dropping it in the hole by hand. | ch. 1
term: Finite game | A game played in order to win, after which it is over. The term comes from James P. Carse. | ch. 1
term: Infinite game | A game played in order to keep playing for as long as possible. Tetris is the book's example. | ch. 1
term: Flow | The state of working at the very edge of your ability. In it, both quitting and winning feel like a loss. | ch. 1
term: Hard fun | Hard work that a person chooses and enjoys, as opposed to easy relaxing pastimes such as watching television. | ch. 1
term: Eustress | Stress felt as a chosen challenge, with confidence that it can be met. The body reacts as it does to harmful stress, but the person feels motivated instead of afraid. | ch. 1
term: Fiero | An Italian word for pride, used by designers for the rush after beating a hard challenge. People tend to throw their arms over their heads and shout. | ch. 1
term: Experience sampling method | A research method that interrupts people at random times and asks what they are doing and how they feel. | ch. 1
term: High-stakes work | Fast, action-based play with a real chance of spectacular failure, such as racing at top speed or fighting zombies. | ch. 1
term: Busywork | Predictable, repetitive play that occupies hands and mind and gives a clear result, such as swapping jewels or harvesting virtual crops. | ch. 1
term: Mental work | Play that exercises thinking, either in short bursts like timed sums or over long campaigns like a ten-thousand-year strategy game. | ch. 1
term: Physical work | Play that makes the body tired, such as virtual boxing or dancing to a screen. | ch. 1
term: Discovery work | Play that rewards investigating unfamiliar places, objects and characters. | ch. 1
term: Teamwork | Play built on cooperation, where each player has a role in a larger effort, such as a twenty-five-player raid. | ch. 1
term: Creative work | Play in which a player makes decisions that matter and feels pride in what was made, such as designing a house in The Sims. | ch. 1
term: Gaming the system | Using a system's rules for personal gain in a way they were not meant to be used. The phrase shows how wary people are of games. | ch. 1
term: Feedback loop | The cycle in which a player acts and the game answers. In video games the cycle is tight, so there is almost no gap between action and response. | ch. 1
term: Positive psychology | The field that studies how people flourish and which activities make them content. | ch. 2
term: Happiness engineer | The book's name for a game designer who applies research on emotion to shape how players feel. | ch. 2
term: Gamer regret | The worry a player feels about what else could have been done with the hours spent playing. | ch. 2
term: Fatigue system | A rule that cuts the rewards of a player who has played for several hours in a row. Some games in South Korea and China use one. | ch. 2
term: Resting bonus | A rule that raises the rewards of a player who has spent time away from the game. World of Warcraft uses one. | ch. 2
term: Extrinsic reward | A reward from outside the activity, such as money, goods, status or praise. | ch. 2
term: Intrinsic reward | A reward that comes from the activity itself: pleasant feelings, personal strengths and bonds with other people. | ch. 2
term: Autotelic | Describes an activity done for its own sake, which rewards itself. | ch. 2
term: Hedonic adaptation | The way a person gets used to a pleasure, so that a larger amount is needed for the same feeling. | ch. 2
term: Hedonic resilience | A steadier mood built by repeating activities that reward themselves, so that outside events matter less. | ch. 2
term: Blissful productivity | The feeling of being deeply absorbed in work whose results are immediate and obvious. | ch. 3
term: Massively multiplayer online game | A game on a remote server where thousands of players share one world at once. World of Warcraft is the example. | ch. 3
term: Avatar | The character that a player controls and improves in a game world. | ch. 3
term: Leveling up | Earning points that raise a character's level, which then opens harder work. | ch. 3
term: Endgame | The stage at the highest level, where the hardest opponents and tasks are found. | ch. 3
term: Quest | A task given to a player with a goal, a reason, where to go, what to do and a proof of completion. | ch. 3
term: Raid | A mission that only a group of players can finish, which needs roles to be planned and rehearsed. | ch. 3
term: Guild | A long-term alliance of players who take on the hardest raids together. | ch. 3
term: Heads-up display | The on-screen panel that shows a player's points and traits changing as they play. | ch. 3
term: Phasing | A display effect in which the server shows each player a different version of the world, depending on what that player has done. | ch. 3
term: Casual game | A game that is easy to learn, quick to play and light on computer power, often played in short bursts on a browser or phone. Bejeweled and Peggle are examples. | ch. 3

idea: Games meet needs that daily life leaves open | Many people spend long hours in games, and the book reads this as a sign that games supply rewards, teaching and company that daily life does not. Players in World of Warcraft raids and Rock Band practice are examples. | Introduction
idea: A game can be a deliberate response to hardship | Herodotus tells of the Lydians, who played games on one day and ate on the next to survive a famine for eighteen years. The book reads this as play that gave people structure and a sense of power when they had little of either. | Introduction

idea: A game is a chosen unnecessary obstacle | Bernard Suits defined playing a game as the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles. In golf a player could drop the ball in the hole by hand, but agrees to stand far away and use a club. | ch. 1
idea: Rules turn an ordinary task into a game | Scrabble is spelling words, which is typing. It becomes a game because players hold only seven tiles, cannot pick letters, have to build on words already played, and have a fixed number of each letter. | ch. 1
idea: A game need not be winnable | Tetris cannot be won, because the pieces only fall faster until the player fails. Players still play because of the strength of its visual, number and difficulty feedback. | ch. 1
idea: Three kinds of feedback in one game | Tetris shows rows vanishing on screen, a score that climbs, and a difficulty the player can feel rising. The book names these visual, quantitative and qualitative feedback. | ch. 1
idea: Digital games tighten the feedback loop | In a computer game the response to an action is immediate and countable, and the game can raise difficulty only when the player is doing well. This keeps players near the edge of their skill. | ch. 1
idea: Players may prefer playing to winning | In games with strong feedback, being absorbed can please more than winning, and winning ends the game. The book uses this to argue that gamers are not mainly people who want to win. | ch. 1
idea: Players can learn the rules from the feedback | In Portal the player starts in a room with no instructions and works out the goal and physics by watching how the game responds. The four parts stay the same, but feedback comes first and the rules come last. | ch. 1
idea: Players take a game seriously | Because the goal and feedback are worth caring about, players wrestle with limits for a long time. The book says a game matters to the person playing it, even though it is chosen. | ch. 1
idea: Chosen hard work differs from imposed hard work | Work done for someone else often fails to fit our strengths, gives no view of what it adds to, and has no visible pay-off. A game offers work that suits the person and is chosen. | ch. 1
idea: The opposite of play is depression | Brian Sutton-Smith is quoted saying this. The book describes play as an optimistic sense of one's ability together with a rush of activity, the reverse of depression. | ch. 1
idea: Relaxing pastimes can leave people lower | Experience sampling studies find that watching television or window-shopping leaves people less motivated afterward, while playing sports, board games and video games leaves them more interested and in a higher mood. | ch. 1
idea: A game is built from many kinds of work | A single player may meet high-stakes work, busywork, mental, physical, discovery, team and creative work. Different kinds suit different people at different times. | ch. 1
idea: Fiero grows with difficulty | The harder the obstacle that was beaten, the bigger the rush of triumph. It involves reward circuits tied to addiction, and people everywhere show it with the same gesture. | ch. 1
idea: Games make flow quick and reliable | Csikszentmihalyi found flow in chess, climbing and dancing, where it took years of skill. Video games gave a goal, rising challenge, voluntary play and fast feedback, so flow could start within seconds. | ch. 2
idea: Reality can be given a game's structure | Csikszentmihalyi argued in 1975 that schools and workplaces leave people bored and anxious, and that work with chosen goals, matched obstacles and constant feedback would give more flow. | ch. 2
idea: Sudnow and Breakout | David Sudnow played Breakout for three months. The paddle shrank and the ball sped up as bricks fell, so difficulty tracked his skill, and each brick gave a beep and a flicker. He stopped playing after one flawless game. | ch. 2
idea: Too much flow and fiero can wear a player out | Flow uses up mental and physical resources and cannot be kept up all the time. Fiero is linked to the feeling of addiction. Designers add fatigue systems and resting bonuses to limit hours, but players find ways around limits. | ch. 2
idea: Hours of play can cause regret | Clive Thompson found he had played thirty-six hours in one week and felt pride and emptiness together. The book says players tend to start doubting once play passes about twenty hours a week. | ch. 2
idea: Outside rewards do not last | The book cites a University of Rochester study of 150 graduates. Those who reached money, fame and looks were not happier, while those who worked on strengths and relationships were happier for two years. | ch. 2
idea: Four rewards that come from within | The book groups intrinsic rewards into satisfying work, the hope or experience of success, social connection, and meaning. Games supply all four without paying money or raising status. | ch. 2
idea: Self-made rewards are renewable | Sonja Lyubomirsky is quoted saying a reward a person made themselves can be made again. Activities that reward themselves build resilience, while bought pleasures need to grow to keep working. | ch. 2
idea: Work needs a goal and actionable steps | A clear goal with no known way to progress is a problem, and a goal with next steps is work. The book says designed work carries a guarantee of progress, and that this guarantee is what draws players. | ch. 3
idea: A World of Warcraft quest spells out its parts | A quest scroll gives a goal, the reason it matters, where to go, step-by-step instructions and the proof to bring back. Players seldom doubt they can finish a quest, only when they will fit it in. | ch. 3
idea: More work is the main reward | In World of Warcraft, finishing a quest brings further quests, and the book calls this a bigger reward than points or gold. Each new task is slightly harder than the last, enough to interest and not enough to cause anxiety. | ch. 3
idea: Players ask for long levelling | Players take about five hundred hours to reach the top level. When Age of Conan was said to need two hundred fifty hours, fans called that too little effort. The process of improving is the draw. | ch. 3
idea: Visible results make work satisfying | The heads-up display flashes +1 strength and +1 stamina, the avatar wears finer armour, and quest histories can be shown. Each way of seeing progress lets players watch their abilities grow. | ch. 3
idea: Phasing shows a player's effect on the world | After a player helps a faction win an area, that player sees a camp there where others still see enemies. The book says seeing an effect on the world is among the things people want most from work. | ch. 3
idea: Office work often hides its results | Matthew Crawford and Alain de Botton are cited to say that when the chain from effort to result is long and many people share it, people struggle to feel what they did. Games give a small group direct and fast results. | ch. 3
idea: Casual games give short bursts of satisfying work | Games such as Peggle and Bejeweled take minutes to learn and play. The book reports that most senior executives surveyed play them while working, to feel less stressed. | ch. 3
