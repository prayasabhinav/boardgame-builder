term: Locative game | A game played in real places, where the spot a player stands on changes what the rules give them. Phones, GPS or paper maps often track where players are. | p. 197
term: Locative media | Art and games made with GPS, mobile phones and maps, so that position in a city becomes part of the work. | p. 197
term: Psychogeography | Debord's method of walking a city to notice how streets and places change the feelings, thoughts and behaviour of the walker. | p. 195
term: Dérive | A drift: an unplanned walk through a city, taken to find new views of familiar streets. | p. 195
term: Flâneur | A stroller who walks city streets to watch and be seen. The figure began as a leisured European man who could walk wherever he liked. | p. 193
term: Constructed situation | The Situationists' name for a short moment of life set up on purpose, by changing a place and what people do in it, to break up boredom. | p. 195
term: Tactic | De Certeau's word for a small everyday move by someone with little power, made inside a system they did not set up. Tricking a game administrator or reskinning a character are tactics of this kind. | p. 190
term: La perruque | De Certeau's term for doing your own work while on someone else's time. | p. 221
term: Site-specific work | Art tied to one place, so that the viewer has to be there for the work to be complete. | p. 201
term: Abstract space | Lefebvre's name for space defined so that it can be set up anywhere and changed at will. Flanagan says many urban games treat a city this way, as a board with no history. | p. 199
term: Representational space | Lefebvre's name for space as people live it, tied to the history of a group or a site. It need not be tidy or consistent. | p. 207
term: Non-player character (NPC) | A character in a game that no player controls. In street games, passers-by who do not know about the game can end up in this role. | p. 204
term: Unaware participant | A person drawn into a pervasive game without knowing a game is going on. | p. 205
term: Temporary autonomous zone | Hakim Bey's idea of a short-lived space that slips outside formal control. Souzis made one in which people agreed not to use their phones. | p. 211
term: Lead object and release object | In Chain Reaction, a lead object (a Starbucks cup) sends the walker off in the direction it is moving. A release object (an independent bookshop) lets the walker stop. | p. 214
term: Society of the spectacle | Debord's phrase for a society in which people watch and buy images of life instead of acting for themselves. | p. 204
term: Located media | Flanagan's term for everyday media tied to a place and the people in it, such as community and grassroots projects, set against high-technology locative media. | p. 200
term: Hybrid play | Play that blends public space with online game space. | p. 216
term: Frontierism | A theme in many games and software: conquest, survival and rule over local people and land. | p. 205
term: Incidental learning | What people pick up from the messages built into media without being taught them. The Sims is said to teach spending, because players have to earn money to buy things. | p. 223
term: Irony (in games) | A deliberate gap between what a game shows or says and what it asks the player to do, which leaves room for the player to think about it. | p. 228
term: You-never-win game | Shuen-shing Lee's name for a game built so that the player cannot win, as in a tragedy, in order to make people think. September 12th is an example. | p. 244
term: Serious game | A game made for education or training, such as disaster relief, hazardous work, military use or redesigning parks and transit. | p. 243
term: Game for change | A game that takes on social concerns such as poverty, racism, war or human rights, often through education and outreach. Also called a social impact game. | p. 243
term: Emotional content | Chris Crawford's phrase: a game gives a simplified, deliberate picture of emotional reality. | p. 240
term: Conflict (in Crawford's sense) | A goal plus obstacles that answer back to the player. If the obstacles stay passive the challenge is a puzzle; if an intelligent agent blocks the player on purpose it is a game. | p. 244
term: Simulation | A model of a real situation that players can act on, with clear cause and effect. September 12th shows a town where each missile has set results. | p. 239
term: Learning curve | How the difficulty of a game rises as the player gets more skilled. | p. 245
term: Game modification | Changing an existing game's art, story or code to make a new version. Bilal hacked a game to make The Night of Bush Capturing: A Virtual Jihadi. | p. 238
term: Mini-game | A small, short game. Bookchin's The Intruder links ten arcade-style mini-games along one story. | p. 227
term: Casual game | A short, easy-to-start game, often played online. Flanagan says such games are seen as the main product for women players. | p. 225
term: MMORPG | A massively multiplayer online role-playing game, in which very large numbers of players share one online world. EverQuest and World of Warcraft are examples. | p. 223
term: Rhythm game | A game in which the player has to press keys at timed moments. In Hush the player types falling letters at the instant they are brightest. | p. 241
term: Hidden transcript | James C. Scott's term for the account that people under power share among themselves, which runs against the official account. Flanagan links it to unplaying. | p. 260
term: Thirdspace | Soja's word for lived and imagined space that sits between physical places and abstract ideas of space. Flanagan treats games as one such space. | p. 253
term: Iterative design | A cycle of setting a goal, making the minimum rules and pieces, building a prototype, play testing, revising and repeating. | p. 254
term: Playable prototype | A rough version of a game, made on paper or by acting it out, so the idea can be tried early. | p. 254
term: Play test | A session where players try a game while the designers look for dead ends, dull stretches and the difficulty of each task. | p. 254
term: Design goal (mission statement) | The aim the designer sets at the start of a project, which later steps are checked against. | p. 254
term: Values goal | A human concern, such as equity or diversity, set at the start with the same weight as the design goal. | p. 257
term: Critical play method | Flanagan's version of iterative design with added steps: set values goals, build rules that support them, allow many play styles, test with varied players, and check the values appear in play. | p. 257
term: Reflective practice | Donald Schön's way of working in which makers step back from the work to see the whole and change their priorities as they learn. | p. 255
term: Fourth wall | The invisible barrier between a performance and its audience. Boal worked to break it down. | p. 245
term: Values at Play | A research project that helps game designers take human values into account. It uses an exercise called Grow a Game. | p. 241
idea: Play belongs to a place | Flanagan holds that play is universal but each experience of it is tied to a place and culture. The same act may count as play in one square and not be seen as play in another. Steve Symons's Aura is moved to many squares unchanged, which she reads as treating the site as secondary. | p. 192
idea: Play needs agreement | Play can only start when the people involved decide it can, and this includes bystanders. A game played in a public square asks passers-by, who never agreed, to be part of it. | p. 192
idea: A street game hides who is playing | In Cruel 2 B Kind, teams of two "kill" others with acts of kindness such as a compliment. Players cannot tell who is a player and who is a bystander, so strangers may be hit by accident. | p. 202
idea: Captured players join the capturer | A Cruel 2 B Kind player who is picked off joins the team of the person who did it. Teams grow until two crowds meet in one final showdown. | p. 202
idea: Passers-by become game pieces | Flanagan notes that Cruel 2 B Kind uses outsiders as non-player characters, and players act kindly for points rather than out of kindness. In Vem Grater, players left clues and graffiti around a university, and the cleaning staff had to undo them. | p. 204
idea: Taking play outdoors does not by itself give power | Flanagan argues that moving a game onto the pavement is not empowering on its own. If the aim is to help people reclaim a city, that aim has to be built into the mechanic and the setting. | p. 206
idea: A hopscotch grid can be a map-making tool | In mapscotch, cards ask players to chalk hopscotch patterns on the pavement on themes such as labour or human rights. A very simple old rule set is used to draw out how a city treats different people. | p. 200
idea: A walking game can be a short rule set | Chain Reaction has players change direction whenever they see a lead object, such as a Starbucks cup, and stop at a release object, such as an independent bookshop. They map each walk, and the maps differ from one neighbourhood to the next. | p. 214
idea: Three rules can make a game | Suyin Looui's Transition Algorithm has three steps: visit a neighbourhood that is changing, photograph it, and bring home a souvenir that was not bought. The game sends players to places of change and records what they see. | p. 210
idea: Players can set their own rules | In the Cell Phone-Free Temporary Autonomous Zone, people in a set place and time follow rules that they ideally decide for themselves. Badges mark who is part of the zone. | p. 213
idea: Less technology can leave more room for the place | Flanagan observes that the locative projects most aware of space often use little or no technology. She suggests the lived experience of a place comes first and the technology second. | p. 216
idea: A giant game piece draws a whole city in | In the Big Urban Game, three teams moved huge inflatable pieces through neighbourhoods towards one bridge. The public voted online or by phone on the route, and neighbours who rarely met ended up playing together. | p. 217
idea: A game asks who has time to play | Flanagan asks of drift games and street games: who is free to wander, and who walks because work or the day requires it? A game that assumes free time and free movement is built for some players and not others. | p. 196
idea: Swapping the picture does not change the system | Butler's argument, as Flanagan uses it, is that reskinning a stereotype is not enough. Larger change comes from altering the categories and relations that a system rests on. | p. 222
idea: Games carry beliefs whether or not designers intend it | The Sims rewards earning and buying, and Grand Theft Auto rewards success through crime. Neither was made to teach, but players take in the world view anyway. | p. 223
idea: A game with no win state has no built-in conflict | September 12th lets the player only fire or not fire missiles at a town. With no goal there is no obstacle, no opponent and no learning curve, and any conflict is inside the player. | p. 240
idea: One action can carry the whole argument | September 12th has one move: fire. Each shot brings rubble and mourners, and the survivors turn into terrorists, so the rules themselves say that violence breeds violence. | p. 240
idea: An impossible task can be the point | In Madrid the player keeps candles lit for those killed in the bombings, but cannot move fast enough to save them all. The failure is built to make the player think about remembering. | p. 241
idea: A mechanic can pull against its theme | In Hush the player has to stay calm and type a lullaby on time to keep a baby quiet during a raid. The wish to be calm clashes with the fear of being found, and the baby's cry grows harsher as the player fails. | p. 242
idea: Random cards can start a design | The Hush team drew the mechanic "Singing" and the theme "Human Rights" in a Values at Play exercise. The odd pairing led to the core idea. | p. 241
idea: A story and its controls can disagree on purpose | In The Intruder the player earns each line of the story by winning a mini-game, yet the story is about harm to a woman. The gap between easy play and a dark tale is meant to make the player feel part of the harm. | p. 229
idea: A failure state can move the story on | One mini-game of The Intruder moves the story on when the character falls into a trap. In most games that would mean failure and a restart. | p. 229
idea: Most games give the player the command post | Chesher is quoted saying the cursor signals that the machine is waiting for the player's order. Flanagan notes that players are nearly always cast as powerful agents, and The Intruder puts them in an uncomfortable spot instead. | p. 230
idea: A game can play itself | Eddo Stern's Fort Paladin has robotic fingers play America's Army again and again, with the same grenade, the same enemies and the same respawn. Watching it shows how little a player's choice changes in a closed system. | p. 233
idea: Game events can reach the player's body | Tekken Torture Tournament passes injuries to the on-screen fighters on to the real players. In Dark Game, two rivals lose their sight. | p. 233
idea: Players can undo a game from inside it | In Domestic Tension, viewers could shoot paintballs at a man by webcam. Some formed a human shield that took turns aiming the gun away from him, so the play was turned against the rules. | p. 237
idea: A win state can be kept up as an illusion | In Darfur Is Dying the player has a clear goal, to survive one week, so it feels like an action game with a smooth learning curve. Reaching it does not end the real crisis. | p. 246
idea: Success can raise the price of play | A camp that does well in Darfur Is Dying attracts raiders, so the player has to rebuild again and again. The more the player achieves, the more danger the game sends back. | p. 246
idea: The character chosen changes what is possible | A Darfur Is Dying player picks a character by age and gender. A girl who fetches water carries more than a small boy and less than an adult, and she also faces extra danger. | p. 245
idea: A game can point to action outside itself | Darfur Is Dying has a Take Action Now button that lets the player write to the US president, petition Congress or send the game on, which links the game to the world outside it. | p. 246
idea: Hidden effects can make every win look the same | Oil God has the player raise oil prices by starting wars and disasters, with no way to tell which acts raise the price most. Persuasive Games' game rewards every move, so Flanagan calls it a you-always-win game. | p. 249
idea: Games can start talk instead of giving answers | Frasca writes that these games aim to trigger discussion, not to find solutions. The model need not be accurate if it prompts people to think about other ways of handling an issue. | p. 247
idea: A serious game can fail in two ways | Flanagan says such a game has to keep what makes a game enjoyable and still send its message. It can lose either one, ending dull and didactic, or entertaining and hollow. | p. 249
idea: Facts between levels can change how a game reads | Vagamundo is an arcade game in which a migrant avoids flying bottles and fights a giant cockroach. Between its levels it shows facts about border crossing and deaths, so the comic play sits next to serious data. | p. 220
idea: A game can be a safe place for hard subjects | Flanagan says a simulated world lets players step back and think about problems, and may be the safest outlet for exploring terrible ones. She also says play only happens when players feel comfortable. | p. 261
idea: A tragedy form needs the player to lose | Lee links games for change to tragedy, where the player is not meant to win because that keeps the form. Brecht and Boal ask audiences to stay aware that they are watching or playing a model. | p. 244
idea: Work and play can be given the same screen | Metapet has players train office workers in a made-up biotech firm to be more efficient. The game copies the constant checking and upkeep of networked jobs, and asks what that costs. | p. 231
idea: Games about care drew many women players | Flanagan reports that games of everyday tasks, such as social talk and tending characters, attracted many women players. Women are still few among the people who make games. | p. 225
idea: Rules are constraints that start invention | Flanagan says even playing house has implied rules for actions and space. Rules of operation give both designer and player constraints that provoke new ideas. | p. 252
idea: Designers follow repeatable steps | Flanagan lists the usual cycle: set a goal, build a minimum set of rules and pieces, make a paper or acted prototype, play test, revise, repeat. The cycle can be run on one small goal or on the whole game. | p. 254
idea: Add a values check to every loop | The critical play method asks the designer to set a values goal next to the design goal and test both on each round. A game is checked for being playable and for whether its values come through in play. | p. 257
idea: Plan for several play styles and for subversion | Flanagan suggests a noncompetitive way to play beside a competitive one. She also asks designers to leave room for players to bend the system. | p. 258
idea: Test with people who do not usually play | Designers are asked to leave the studio and test with a wide range of players, including nongamers. They look for dead ends, dull stretches and uneven difficulty. | p. 258
idea: The finished game can say something other than intended | Flanagan notes a gap between what a maker intends and what gets made. Varied testers can show whether the concept still comes through once the game is done. | p. 258
idea: A design process can share authority | Flanagan asks for less hierarchy in making games, with the usual designers, ideas and players not always the same. Playtesting with the community an artist wants to reach is part of this. | p. 256
idea: Made things have politics | Following Langdon Winner, Flanagan says artifacts carry politics, and games hold the norms and biases of their makers and their time, often unnoticed. The method a maker uses shapes what a game means. | p. 253
idea: Past examples give designers a list of tactics | Flanagan turns the book's cases into moves a designer can try: write instructions, use rude language, make puppets of people, make words touchable, shift the point of view. She says this gives a name to techniques that might go unnoticed. | p. 259
idea: Games distil daily life | Flanagan says games take the everyday actions of players and boil them down, and they carry the motives and values of their designers. The game then makes a place unfamiliar enough for play to begin. | p. 262
idea: Play is never innocent | Citing Sutton-Smith, Flanagan reminds readers that play has been used to pass on culture and to change it, and has also served other purposes. A locative game cannot be taken as freeing just because it is play. | p. 204
