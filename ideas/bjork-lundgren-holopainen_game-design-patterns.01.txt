term: Game design pattern | A description of interaction that keeps coming up in game play. The authors build their model on such patterns, and use them to design, analyse and compare games. | p. 2
term: Game mechanic | A part of a game's rule system that covers one general or specific aspect of the game. Roll and move is the example given: it says a die is rolled and something is moved by the result, but not how it moves or why. | p. 4
term: Formal Abstract Design Tools | Doug Church's attempt at a shared design vocabulary for designers. Each tool is a one-sentence description, for example Perceivable Consequences, a clear reaction from the game world to what the player does. | p. 4
term: 400 Design Rules | A project to collect game design rules that have proved useful, written as instructions. Each rule has an imperative statement, a domain of application, dominated and dominating rules, and examples. | p. 4
term: Harvesting patterns | The authors' way of finding patterns by analysing existing games and other fields: recognise a candidate, analyse it in example games, describe it, test it in a small prototype, and evaluate it. In practice the steps became loose and repeated, with candidates fusing, changing and being created at almost any stage. | p. 5
term: Structural framework | The companion half of the model, which describes the parts of a game. At the top it separates a game instance, a game session and a play session, and below that it sorts the parts of a game into bounding, temporal and objective components. | p. 6
term: Bounding, temporal and objective components | Bounding components are goals, rules and game modes, and say what is allowed. Temporal components are actions, events, end conditions, evaluation functions and closures, and cover what happens over time. Objective components are players, interfaces and game elements such as tokens, dice, cards and tiles. | p. 6
term: Pattern template | The fixed headings each pattern is written under: name, description, consequences, using the pattern, and relations. The description can note the game where it was found and examples of games that show it. | p. 7
term: Conflicting patterns | One of the three kinds of relation a pattern can have, the other two being superior patterns and subpatterns. A conflicting pattern is one that is difficult to put into a game together with the given pattern. | p. 7
term: Superior patterns and subpatterns | A superior pattern describes a more abstract characteristic and can be brought about by applying the given pattern. A subpattern is a more specific pattern that can be used to bring the given one about. | p. 7
term: Paper Rock Scissors | A pattern taken from the children's game, where players try to outwit each other by guessing what the other will do. There is no winning strategy, and that is the point of the pattern. | p. 8
term: Non-transitivity | The logic behind Paper Rock Scissors: even if A beats B and B beats C, A does not beat C. The authors say designers sometimes call this triangularity. | p. 8
idea: Genres are a weak base for a common language | Genres mostly follow what is popular and come from game journalism rather than careful study, and sorting by interaction alone can give 42 genres. The authors look instead for the components that could describe genres. | p. 4
idea: Mechanics need relations to be useful | The idea that a game is put together from smaller components is useful, but the authors argue that mechanics need a clearer structure, with information on how they relate to each other and how to apply them. | p. 4
idea: Build the language on interaction, not story | Most earlier game research borrowed from literature, theatre and film, which risks losing sight of interaction. The authors take interaction, between players and between player and game, as the more defining feature of games. | p. 5
idea: Patterns are not only problem and solution | The authors do not define patterns as problem and solution pairs. They give two reasons: that would treat patterns only as a way to remove unwanted effects, and many patterns describe a characteristic that automatically brings other characteristics with it. | p. 7
idea: Names are for remembering | Pattern names are meant to be short, specific and idiomatic, to help recall once the description has been read rather than to explain it. Aliases are left out to keep the number of names small, and references to similar ideas in other models do that job. | p. 7
idea: Test a pattern by taking it out | In analysing a candidate pattern, the authors describe how it is used in example games and then try removing it from those games to explain how the play would change. | p. 6
idea: Designers use few named mechanics | In interviews with nine professional designers, all used genre, theme and mechanisms easily but named few mechanics. The exceptions for board and card game makers were Bluff, Tension, Action Cards, Storytelling, Trading, Action Points and Cooperation. | p. 6
idea: Paper Rock Scissors keeps choices open | The pattern promotes tension, either until the choices are revealed or until the result of a long-term strategy shows. It brings randomness unless players can learn about others' activity or keep a record of their behaviour. | p. 8
idea: Ways to use patterns | The authors list uses without picking one method: idea generation, developing a concept, explaining a design in pre-production, finding competition and patent issues, solving problems, analysing games, sorting games and genres, and exploring new platforms. | p. 9
idea: Patterns for idea generation | A designer can pick a few patterns at random and try to imagine a game that uses them, or take one pattern and look for a new way to build it. | p. 9
idea: Patterns for developing a concept | A game concept can be described as a small set of patterns, then fleshed out by choosing subpatterns and studying how the patterns interact, repeating until the detail is enough. | p. 9
idea: Patterns for analysing games | A pattern collection can be gone through one pattern at a time to see whether, and to what degree, each one is present in an existing game. Games can then be sorted by what they share and differ in. | p. 10
idea: The same pattern shows up in different games | The authors found the same patterns in very different games, such as Carcassonne and Qix, and Pac-Man and King of the Hill variants of first-person shooters. They take this as a sign that patterns help in understanding interaction. | p. 11
idea: A pattern collection is a network | The patterns do not form a strict hierarchy but a network with several base nodes. The authors also say that some groups of patterns are normally used together to bring each other about, which sub and superior relations do not cover. | p. 11
idea: Finding the right pattern is hard | With over 200 candidates, finding the relevant ones for a given situation is a problem, especially for new users. The authors look at ways to sort patterns by use, theme and the structural framework. | p. 11
idea: Patterns do not take the creativity out | The authors answer the fear that patterns make designers copy each other by saying it comes from mixing up the everyday meaning of pattern with the one used by Alexander. They compare it to an artist who has a greater chance of making something new when familiar with the basic elements of the craft. | p. 12
