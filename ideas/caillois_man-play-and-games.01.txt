term: Play (Caillois's definition) | An activity that is free, separate in time and place, uncertain in outcome, unproductive, governed by rules or by make-believe. Caillois lists these six qualities as the common core of all games. | p. 9
term: Agon | Caillois's word for competitive games, where players start with equal chances and one quality, such as speed or skill, decides who wins. Chess, football and races are examples. | p. 14
term: Alea | Caillois's word for games of chance, where the result comes from fate and not from the player's effort. Dice, roulette and lotteries are examples. | p. 17
term: Mimicry | Caillois's word for games of make-believe, where a player takes on another character or a pretend world. A child playing train and an actor on stage are examples. | p. 19
term: Ilinx | Caillois's word for games that seek dizziness, a brief loss of steady perception. Whirling in circles and swinging are examples. | p. 23
term: Paidia | The pole of play marked by free improvisation, noise and carefree high spirits, with few or no rules. A small child banging on a drum plays at this end. | p. 13
term: Ludus | The pole of play marked by arbitrary rules, effort and skill, where difficulty is added on purpose. A crossword puzzle sits at this end. | p. 13
term: Handicap | An advantage given to a weaker player so that the chances of both sides stay equal. In chess, the weaker player may start with an extra pawn. | p. 14
term: Mask (as play) | A disguise worn in play to change who the wearer seems to be. At a carnival the masked person does not claim to be the character, only enjoys the freedom the disguise gives. | p. 21
term: Cheat | A player who breaks a rule while pretending to follow it. The cheat depends on other players keeping the rules. | p. 7
term: Spoilsport (the nihilist) | A person who refuses to play because the rules have no reason behind them. Caillois says this person ends the game, where a cheat does not. | p. 7
term: Wager | A bet placed on an outcome the bettor cannot control. Competitive matches can also become the object of wagers. | p. 18
idea: Six qualities of play | Caillois says play is free, separate, uncertain, unproductive, ruled and make-believe. Chess has rules but no make-believe; a child's game of pirates has make-believe but no rules. | p. 9
idea: A game is ruled or make-believe | Caillois says rules and pretending do the same job: both cut the game off from real life. Playing at pirates needs no rule book, and playing chess needs no pretending. | p. 9
idea: Play is free to leave | A game a person is forced to play stops being play and becomes a chore. A player can always say "I am not playing any more" at any time. | p. 6
idea: Play makes nothing | Gambling moves money between players but produces no goods, and at the end the players can begin again from the same point. Caillois calls play a waste of time, energy and skill. | p. 5
idea: Professionals do not play | Boxers, jockeys and actors who work for a prize or salary are workers. When they play, Caillois says, it is at some other game. | p. 6
idea: Games happen inside a frontier | A game has its own space and time: a hopscotch grid, a board, a stadium, and a starting and ending signal. Sending the ball out of bounds can bring a penalty. | p. 6
idea: Rules have no outside reason | Caillois says the rules of a game are absolute inside the game and meaningless outside it. Nothing explains why a bishop moves diagonally except that the rules say so. | p. 7
idea: Uncertainty lasts to the end | If the result is known in advance, play stops. Card players lay down their hands once the outcome is settled, and a player who wins every time without effort stops enjoying the game. | p. 7
idea: The player needs room to answer | A game asks for a fresh response after each move, free within the rules. Caillois calls this margin of the player's action a main source of pleasure. | p. 8
idea: Classifying by equipment mixes categories | Dividing games into card games, sports or parlour games compares unlike things: tool, skill, place. Marbles can be a skill game or, when guessing odd or even, a game of chance. | p. 12
idea: Four kinds of play | Caillois sorts games by what dominates: competition, chance, simulation or vertigo. Football, roulette, playing pirate and spinning around fall into the four. | p. 12
idea: Equal chances are made on purpose | In agon, designers build equal starting conditions so a win shows real superiority. Players draw lots for sides and swap positions, and a handicap fixes differences between players. | p. 14
idea: Exact equality cannot be reached | Moving first in chess, bidding last in bridge, or having the sun at your back all give small advantages. Games answer this by drawing lots and by taking turns at the favoured position. | p. 15
idea: Agon asks for training | Competitive play needs attention, practice and the wish to win, and the rules apply equally to all. Caillois says this makes the winner's superiority beyond dispute. | p. 15
idea: Animals have agon, not alea | Young bulls, horses and willow wrens fight within a marked space and do not injure each other. Caillois says only humans wait passively on a verdict they cannot influence. | p. 16
idea: Alea removes differences between players | In games of chance, skill and training count for nothing, so a beginner and an expert wait on equal terms. A lucky throw can win more than years of work. | p. 17
idea: Agon and alea are opposite attitudes | In agon the player relies only on himself; in alea he relies on everything except himself. Games such as bridge, dominoes and backgammon join the two by dealing chance hands that skill then plays. | p. 18
idea: Betting turns agon into alea | Because a fair contest has an uncertain result, outsiders can bet on horse races, football or cock fights. Stakes may change as the match goes on. | p. 18
idea: Games build cleaner worlds | In real life luck and merit are tangled together. Games, whether agon or alea, replace this with a situation where it is clear what comes from skill and what from chance. | p. 19
idea: Mimicry is not deception | A child playing train does not try to persuade anyone he is a locomotive. Only spies and fugitives disguise themselves to deceive, and they are not playing. | p. 21
idea: Spectators play mimicry | Fans who lean with a bowled ball or copy a runner's strain take part in the match by imitation. Caillois says identification with a champion works like a reader's with a novel's hero. | p. 22
idea: Mimicry has one rule | The only rule of make-believe is that the actor avoids giving the audience a reason to break the spell. The audience in turn agrees not to question the mask or the set. | p. 23
term: Teetotum | A children's game in which the player spins on one foot as fast as possible. It is Caillois's example of a game that seeks dizziness. | p. 24
term: Mais d'or | A Haitian children's game in which two children hold hands, lean back and turn until breathless, then stagger about. It is a game of dizziness. | p. 24
term: Volador | A Mexican ritual in which men in eagle costume hang by ropes from a high mast and spiral head down to the ground. Caillois uses it to show vertigo at its most extreme. | p. 23
term: Kredati | A Sanskrit word for play that covers adults, children and animals, and especially sudden, lively movement. Caillois compares it to paidia. | p. 27
term: Wan | A Chinese word for relaxed play: handling a jade piece, chess, collecting, enjoying a view. Caillois says it leaves out contests, dice and acting, and points the energy of paidia toward calm and slow thought. | p. 33
term: Hobby | Caillois's word for a side activity done for pleasure, such as collecting or building scale models. He says it grows in factory societies as a reply to repetitive work. | p. 32
term: Game of patience | A solitary card game such as solitaire, where skill has little effect on the result. Caillois places it where ludus meets alea. | p. 30
term: Cup-and-ball | A toy in which the player swings a ball on a string and catches it in a cup. Caillois uses it to show a game of solitary skill that turns into a contest. | p. 29
term: Counting-out rhyme | A chanted verse children use to pick one of them by chance, such as who is "it". Caillois places it among chance games with little structure. | ch. 2, Table I
term: Obstacle (in ludus) | The difficulty a ludus player works against. Caillois says the conflict is with the obstacle and not with a rival. | p. 29
term: Corruption of a game | What Caillois calls the loss of a game's closed frame when its pull spreads into ordinary life. Each of the four kinds corrupts in its own way. | p. 44
term: Alienation (in mimicry) | Caillois's word for the state of a player who stops pretending and believes the role is real. He says the limits of stage and time protect an actor from it. | p. 49
idea: Dizziness as a purpose | Some games aim only to unsettle the senses: spinning, swinging, sliding, fast descents. Caillois says children and adults freely choose this brief panic and the players often rush to repeat it. | p. 24
idea: Vertigo needed machines | Caillois says dizziness for adults needed powerful machines, so fairground rides arrived with the Industrial Revolution. People leave the rides pale and dizzy and walk straight to the ticket booth for another turn. | p. 25
idea: Rides keep the marks of play | A ride is still play because the rider chooses it, it has fixed limits, and it ends when the machine stops. Caillois says watching the riders from a balcony adds to the spectacle without changing this. | p. 26
idea: Animals play at falling and spinning | Dogs spin to catch their tails, chamois slide down glaciers in turn, gibbons fling themselves from bent branches, and birds drop and open their wings late. Caillois reads these as the animal form of ilinx. | p. 25
idea: Play begins as noise | Caillois says the first play is shaking, tearing, scribbling and uproar, which he calls paidia. A cat tangled in wool and an infant laughing at a rattle are early examples. | p. 28
idea: Children test themselves | Children soon bet each other who can stare at the sun, hop, or hold a painful posture longest. Caillois says these are the first small forms of agon, made before any rules are taught. | p. 29
idea: Rules arrive with equipment | The first named games, such as hide and seek, kite-flying and doll play, appear when conventions and tools appear. At that point the four kinds of play separate. | p. 29
idea: Ludus is fighting an obstacle | In ludus the player tests skill against a problem made for the purpose, such as a yo-yo, kite or ring puzzle. No rival is needed, though a rival easily gets added. | p. 29
idea: Ludus joins other kinds | Ludus mixes with agon in chess, with alea in patience and slot machines, and with mimicry in model building. Caillois says it does not mix with ilinx, because its job is to keep a calm mind. | p. 31
idea: Solitary puzzles turn into contests | A crossword or solitaire game is easily turned into a competition by newspaper prizes or a higher score. Caillois says ludus left alone tends to fade or become a private obsession. | p. 31
idea: Fashion crazes need rivalry | Yo-yos, diabolos, rebuses and charades rise and vanish together with the crowd playing them. Caillois explains this by the hidden competition inside ludus. | p. 32
idea: A hobby answers work | A factory worker repeating one movement may build a complete scale model of the machine at home. Caillois reads this as a worker getting back at dull work in a creative way. | p. 32
idea: Different cultures send paidia different ways | Caillois says Western cultures turned free energy into ludus, with invention and difficulty, while classical China turned it into wan, calm and idle thinking. The choice shows in the games a culture prefers. | p. 33
idea: Chinese words split play into kinds | The Chinese characters hsi, choua, teou and ton cover acting, skill, conflict, and chance and daring. Caillois says they match his four categories, which suggests the split is not only a Western habit. | p. 34
idea: Even solo games want an audience | Kite-flyers and top-spinners gather where others can see, and Eskimo cup-and-ball turns into a spoken duel with a crowd. Caillois says play lacks something when it is only a solitary exercise. | p. 39
idea: Each kind of play needs company | Agon needs rivals, gamblers prefer a crowd at the track to a phone bet, masks are worn for others, and swinging is livelier in a group. A game also holds only a small number of players before it needs an organisation. | p. 40
idea: Each kind of play has a public institution | Agon becomes sport, alea becomes casinos and lotteries, mimicry becomes theatre and puppet shows, and ilinx becomes the travelling show and the yearly festival. | p. 41
idea: Each attitude has a matching corruption | When the frame around a game is dropped, the attitude spills into daily life: agon into ruthless ambition, alea into superstition, mimicry into alienation, ilinx into drink and drugs. Pleasure turns into obsession. | p. 44
idea: Cheats stay inside the game | A cheat pretends to respect the rules and depends on others keeping them, so thrown out he leaves the game intact. Caillois contrasts this with the player who no longer accepts any referee. | p. 45
idea: Agon is spoiled where no referee is accepted | Players are taught to accept a referee's ruling even if it is wrong, and to take defeat calmly. Caillois says competition turns brutal where no such decision is recognised. | p. 46
idea: Superstition is chance outside its frame | A gambler who treats dice as a heartless machine plays alea well. One who reads omens, lucky numbers or horoscopes tries to bend chance, which Caillois calls its corruption. | p. 46
idea: Applause ends the pretence | The fall of a curtain, the end of a masked ball at dawn and the return of a costume to the shop mark where make-believe stops. Caillois says such sharp limits keep players from losing themselves in a role. | p. 49
idea: Seriousness is allowed, spill-over is not | A player may play very hard or risk a fortune, as long as the game stops at an agreed time and the player returns to ordinary duties. Caillois says the harm comes when the rules of the game keep applying outside it. | p. 50
idea: Drink replaces the ride | A ride's dizziness stops when the machine stops. Alcohol and drugs bring a similar loss of balance but change the body permanently, which Caillois says is the reverse of play. | p. 51
term: Roundelay | A children's circle song with actions, such as "Old Rogers" or "Jenny Jones". Caillois reads some as the leftover scripts of old rituals. | p. 58
term: Tournament | A regulated combat in an enclosed field, with limits and no consequence outside it. Caillois sets it against war, which has no such frame. | p. 62
term: Lottery (simple, complex, continuing) | A simple lottery has one draw; a complex lottery has many possible combinations; a continuing lottery has two or more stages, the first winner entering a second draw. | ch. 2, Table I note
term: Divination | The practice of reading the future from cards, stars or omens. Caillois says it grows from the same attitude as games of chance. | p. 48
term: Survival (of a game) | A game that stays in use after the belief or ritual behind it has been lost. Caillois says many children's games began as ceremonies. | p. 58
idea: Ilinx can turn into addiction | A fairground ride ends when the machine stops, while alcohol and drugs bring the same loss of balance but change the body permanently. Caillois gives the example of ants that feed on a beetle's intoxicating secretion and let it eat their own young. | p. 51
idea: Ludus and paidia are ways of playing | Caillois says ludus and paidia are not kinds of game but two manners of play. They pass into daily life as opposite tastes, such as noise over a symphony or scribbling over drawing in perspective. | p. 53
idea: Rules protect from the drives | Competition, chance, simulation and vertigo are powerful urges, and Caillois says they do harm when left free. Games let each urge be satisfied in a limited way, so that players learn to live beside them. | p. 55
idea: War used to resemble a game | Older war kept to frontiers, strongholds and set objectives, much like a tournament. Caillois says once those limits are dropped, war turns to mass destruction. | p. 55
idea: Two theories of games and culture | One view says games are leftovers of old rituals; the other, Huizinga's, says culture grows from play. Caillois answers that the spirit of play shapes culture, while particular games are what remains of earlier institutions. | p. 58
idea: Masks and toys began in ritual | The mask, the kite, the top and hopscotch all served sacred purposes before they became playthings. In Korea a kite carried the community's sins away, and in China it measured distance and carried a rope across a river. | p. 59
idea: Games from rites | An Egyptian tomb shows the dead playing a board game for their fate in the afterlife, and the Greek games were offerings to a god. Caillois uses these to show that game structures can serve religion before play. | p. 60
idea: Children play at what adults do seriously | Children play soldiers while armies still exist, play Mass while the church stands, and play with dolls of sacred figures. Caillois says the children's game and the adult activity run side by side. | p. 62
idea: A new weapon quickly becomes a toy | Children play with water pistols, toy tanks and toy bombers as soon as such things exist. Caillois says Monopoly does not come after capitalism but copies how it works. | p. 61
idea: A game differs from the activity it copies | A tournament is a game and a war is not; roulette is a game and playing the stock market is not. Caillois says the difference is that in a game nobody may influence the result, and nothing outside the lists is affected. | p. 63
idea: Games and daily life are opposed | Caillois says play stays closed off and has no lasting effect on institutions, while daily life is full of consequences. A player can say "I am not playing any more"; an adult cannot say that about work. | p. 63
idea: Games and laws share structure | Rules of law, rhetoric, perspective or liturgy resemble rules of play, yet one does not come from the other. They keep to different domains that cannot be swapped. | p. 64
idea: Inherited rank became a contest | Where a role was once set by birth, it is now won by merit, competition or examination. Caillois says this carries the principles of agon and alea out of the game and into society. | p. 64
idea: A list of shared urges | Play and social life share the same impulses: proving superiority, beating a record, hoping for luck, secrecy and disguise, fear, repetition and variation, solving puzzles, respecting and dodging rules, longing for ecstasy. Caillois says a culture honours some and pushes others into play. | p. 65
idea: Some games push against the culture | Greek stadium games supported the ideal of the city. National lotteries in modern states go against the ideal of earned reward, yet give a chance-based substitute for what work alone cannot promise. | p. 66
idea: Play absorbs adults fully | Caillois says an adult at play can spend as much energy, skill and attention as at a job. He links the cultural weight of games to this freedom and intensity, which play offers without lasting harm. | p. 66
idea: A sociology derived from games | Caillois proposes that the kinds of play a culture prefers could explain its path. A culture that favours rank and chance differs from one that favours masks and trance. | p. 67
idea: Six pairings of the four kinds | The four attitudes can pair in six ways: agon-alea, agon-mimicry, agon-ilinx, alea-mimicry, alea-ilinx, mimicry-ilinx. Caillois sorts the six into forbidden, contingent and fundamental. | p. 71
idea: A horse race holds three attitudes without mixing them | The jockeys compete, the crowd watches a show and the public bets. Caillois says these meet but do not blend, and betting does not change how the race is run. | p. 72
idea: Vertigo cancels competition | Dizziness or blind fury removes controlled effort, respect for rules and willingness to accept a referee. Caillois calls agon and ilinx a forbidden pairing. | p. 72
idea: Disguise cannot cheat chance | Nobody can trick a die or a wheel, and a person asking for luck does not want to appear as someone else. Caillois says alea plus mimicry would turn into magic, which tries to force fate. | p. 73
idea: Gamblers get dizzy | Winners and losers at a casino lose track of time and money and gamble beyond their means. Caillois says alea and ilinx can sit together because dizziness does not break the rule that the player has to accept the result. | p. 73
idea: Competition is a show | A match has scenes, applause and a crowd, so a champion and a film star become interchangeable. Mimicry adds to agon provided the athletes do not fool the crowd. | p. 74
idea: Agon and alea mirror each other | Both need exact fairness and careful measurement, but in agon the player relies on himself and in alea on everything except himself. Card games, dominoes and golf sit between, where the player does as much as he can in a situation he did not make. | p. 74
idea: Chance can stand for the world | In games that mix skill and chance, chance plays the part of nature, the weather or the gods resisting the player. Caillois calls such a game an ordered, limited image of life. | p. 75
idea: Mask and trance together | Playing another person can be both liberating and intoxicating, and the mask can pull a player into trance. Caillois says mimicry joined to ilinx leaves play behind and moves into the sacred. | p. 75
idea: In each pairing one side makes things, the other does not | Agon builds sport and mimicry builds theatre, while alea and ilinx rarely produce anything lasting. Caillois says agon trains faculties, while waiting on chance is passive. | p. 77
idea: The danger in negative games | In alea the risk is not being able to limit the stakes, and in ilinx it is not being able to stop the disorder. Caillois says people do not grow resistant to either; they are tempted until they give in. | p. 78
idea: Fairness grows in places that honour merit and chance | Caillois says societies built on merit joined with chance keep trying to raise the part played by justice and lower the part played by luck, and he calls this effort progress. | p. 78
idea: Games change slowly yet travel widely | Hopscotch, chess and dice show up across ages and continents, while dolls and kites were unknown in Europe until the eighteenth century. Caillois says games last because they matter little. | p. 81
idea: Games change with the culture | Hopscotch was a labyrinth for the soul, then a plan of a basilica. Chess came from India with four kings, and in medieval Europe one king became a queen, the strongest piece, under the cult of courtly love. | p. 82
idea: A game trains the habits it reflects | Games popular in a society show its tastes and, by being played, strengthen those tastes. Caillois suggests a society can be read from its games. | p. 82
term: Truco | An Argentine card game in which partners signal their cards to each other with set faces, winks and pouts without letting opponents read them. The signals are part of the rules. | p. 83
term: Guignol | A French glove puppet show, like Punch and Judy, where a rude puppet breaks every rule and the crowd cheers. | p. 84
term: Dionysian society | Caillois's name for societies held together by masks and possession, that is by mimicry and ilinx. He sets them against orderly societies built on rank and merit. | p. 87
term: Bull-roarer | A flat piece of wood whirled on a cord to make a roaring sound. In some ceremonies it is the secret sound of the spirits. | p. 88
term: Seidhr | A Norse trance ritual of music, costume and travel to the other world. Caillois uses it to show the Germanic preference for vertigo. | p. 102
term: Cursus honorum | The fixed ladder of offices a Roman official climbed. Caillois uses it as an example of agon entering administration. | p. 109
term: Competitive examination | A test used to choose officials by ability and not by birth. Caillois counts it as agon spreading from games into the state. | p. 109
term: Choosing by lot | Picking officials by drawing lots. The Greek founders of democracy saw it as completely equal, since no one is favoured. | p. 109
term: Possession (trance) | A state in which a person acts out the character of a god or spirit while losing awareness of himself. Caillois treats it as mimicry joined to ilinx. | p. 93
idea: A game fits the culture that plays it | Caillois says golf rests on the player's honesty about his own score and truco rests on codified trickery. Each game trains the habit of mind its culture already prizes. | p. 83
idea: The same game can train different things | The Chinese count chess and checkers among the arts of a scholar, to train a taste for combinations and a calm mind. Caillois warns that such readings of games are unsure and need qualifying. | p. 84
idea: A game can let off what the culture bans | The Punch and Judy puppet beats his wife, kills the devil and hangs the hangman. Caillois says the audience does not approve; it enjoys a cheap release from rules that bind it in real life. | p. 85
idea: Four principles spread unevenly | Caillois asks whether agon, alea, mimicry and ilinx are used in different amounts in different societies, as baseball is played more in one country and chess in another. He says the play world is only an island, but the principles affect the whole society. | p. 85
idea: Two kinds of society | Caillois says some societies run on masks and possession (mimicry and ilinx) and others on offices, careers and rank (agon and alea). In the second kind, birth is a kind of chance and ability is judged by competition. | p. 87
idea: A festival turns the order upside down | In festivals masks make everyday order vanish, stored wealth is destroyed and rules are inverted. Caillois says this shared dizziness binds loosely joined groups together. | p. 89
idea: Mask-making is a secret | Masks are made in secret, and women and children are kept from learning they are made by the men. Caillois says the spirits seem real because the makers believe in them as well. | p. 88
idea: Trance follows a script | Voodoo and Bori possession look wild, yet each god has set costume, props and behaviour, so the person possessed acts as the audience expects. Caillois says the loss of control is drawn from a known pattern. | p. 93
idea: The audience helps the entranced | Shaman and possessed person are watched by helpers who throw a blanket, strike sparks as stars, and catch them if they fall. Caillois says this cooperation keeps the performance safe and correct. | p. 91
idea: Make-believe and belief mix | A shaman may hide the pebble he then draws from a patient, and the audience may know it and still accept it. Caillois sets aside which part is acting and stresses that mimicry and trance nearly always occur together. | p. 93
idea: Children's pretending and possession look alike | An observer likens a person possessed to a child who imagines he is an animal and strengthens it with a piece of clothing. Caillois says the difference is that possession leads to vertigo and does a job for society. | p. 94
idea: A mask becomes a tool of rule | Secret societies use masks to frighten non-members, punish crimes and keep order. Caillois says initiation teaches the novice the mask is a man in disguise, and then asks him to frighten others. | p. 96
idea: Civilization pushes mask and dizziness aside | Where a culture grows into order, mimicry and ilinx lose their central place and shrink into games, fiction and fairs. They give the same satisfactions but without madness. | p. 97
idea: A secret mask is a fragile system | The mask works only while the secret stays unknown, and knowing it spoils the terror. Caillois says this forces strict prohibitions, and when they relax the mask becomes costume and theatre. | p. 105
idea: The veiled prophet's mirrors | Hakim al-Mokanna wore a veil and said any who saw his face would die. According to one chronicle he showed his soldiers a blaze of sunlight reflected from a hundred women's mirrors, and when he was defeated the mask was called a trick. | p. 106
idea: Number brings order | Greek thinkers moved from numbers with shape and virtue to abstract numbers for calculation. Caillois says this world of measure has no place for ecstasy and disguise, and leaves room for games of rules and lots. | p. 107
idea: Stadium games teach loyalty | Greek games, Aztec pelota, Chinese archery and medieval tournaments judged how well a person shot or helped a fallen rival, not only who won. Caillois says limited rivalry teaches respect for the referee. | p. 108
idea: Exams are agon in the state | Officials chosen by competitive tests are placed on a ladder of ranks. Caillois says this carries the structure of a game into government, though birth often still decides the top posts. | p. 109
idea: Lots as equality | Greek democrats held that choosing magistrates by lot gave everyone the same chance, and that voting favoured the well-born. Caillois shows alea used as a fair procedure in a city. | p. 109
term: Disguised lottery | Caillois's name for a contest that looks like a test of talent or knowledge but works like a lottery, because few can win and the odds fall as the prize rises. A television quiz with "double or nothing" is his example. | p. 118
term: Identification (with a champion) | Caillois's word for a diluted form of mimicry, where fans share in the success of a star or athlete by imagining themselves in their place. | p. 120
term: Moira | A Greek word for the portion each person is given by fate. Caillois uses it with tyche, luck, to describe birth as a kind of lottery. | p. 111
term: Kairos | A Greek word for the right moment, an occasion that cannot be repeated. Caillois places it among the Greek ideas of luck. | p. 111
term: Suerte | A Spanish word for one's luck and lot. Some Central American groups believe each person is born with a fixed suerte that decides character and fortune. | p. 111
term: Carnival | A short period of licence in which people wear masks and break the usual rules. Caillois calls it nearer to paidia than ludus. | p. 131
term: Uniform | A permanent, official costume that leaves the face bare. Caillois sets it against the mask, which hides the face and stands for sudden, frightening power. | p. 132
term: Fairground | A marked-off space of crowds, lights and machines where all four kinds of play sit together, with dizziness the main one. | p. 133
term: Black mask | A small plain mask worn at balls and plots. Caillois says it gives anonymity and a promise of secrecy, and marks a game of flirtation or conspiracy. | p. 130
idea: Agon and alea answer one problem | Democracies and games both want everyone to start equal. One way is to draw lots so no ability counts; the other is to make everyone use their abilities to the full. | p. 110
idea: Birth works like a lottery | The Greeks saw talents and privileges as drawn at birth, and some Central American groups believe each person is born with a fixed luck. Caillois sets this against merit, which is won by competing. | p. 111
idea: Equal rules, unequal start | Wealth, schooling and family decide who starts well, so a rule of fair competition can be formally equal and still be unequal in effect. Caillois compares this to a race in which the runners do not begin on the same line. | p. 112
idea: Chance enters even a fair test | An examiner who happens to ask the one question a candidate studied helps that candidate, and the others lose. Caillois says an element of chance sits inside agon. | p. 113
idea: Card games blend both | A winning hand comes from the cards dealt plus the player's knowledge. Caillois uses this to show agon and alea as opposed yet allied. | p. 113
idea: Chance makes unequal people equal | People who doubt they can win by effort turn to lotteries, where the less able, the unskilled and the able stand on the same footing. Caillois reads this as a comfort for those with little hope in fair contests. | p. 114
idea: A lottery needs a huge top prize | Tickets are cheap and divisible and the top prize is very large, so the few winners are dazzling. Caillois gives the example of a French sweepstake whose first prize equalled about 250 years of a worker's wages. | p. 115
idea: Winners are made public | Official publicity follows the lottery winners and reports their plans, which invites readers to try again. Caillois says the large prizes are set to inspire hope of sudden wealth. | p. 116
idea: Continuous play grows turnover | A casino keeps its wheels turning without pause, and the small steady margin per bet brings a constant profit. A rare solemn draw brings fewer bets than continuous play. | p. 117
idea: Gambling towns | Las Vegas and Monte Carlo draw visitors who stay a few days of luxury and return to normal life. Caillois calls the stay a pair of brackets in their ordinary lives. | p. 117
idea: A quiz is a bet | A television quiz looks like a test of knowledge, but each round doubles the risk and shrinks the chance of winning. Caillois calls it a series of wagers dressed as a contest. | p. 119

idea: The two suppressed kinds need an outlet | Mimicry and ilinx cannot be removed, so societies give them limited places, such as carnivals and fairs. Caillois says the safest way to contain them is to keep them apart, since their strength lies in the pairing. | p. 129
idea: The mask has lost its power | In a society without ecstasy the mask no longer changes the wearer. A burglar's mask only hides identity and a gas mask only filters air. | p. 130

idea: Fear that turns into laughter | Rides, mirror mazes and haunted houses cause fright and nausea, which becomes relief and laughter as the person steps off. Caillois says the length and strength of the shock are fixed in advance. | p. 134

idea: The Cuban charade lottery | A banker draws one of thirty-six picture symbols, wraps it, sells tickets, and gives a riddle that may point or mislead. Winners get thirty times their bet, and symbols come with companions and valets, which players have to bet together. | p. 148
