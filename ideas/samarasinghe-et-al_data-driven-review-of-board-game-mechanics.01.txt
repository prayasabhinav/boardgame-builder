term: Mechanic | A feature of a board game, as tagged on BoardGameGeek, that captures one side of how the game works: how it is set up, how resources and powers are shared out, what triggers a win, how turns run, or how actions are carried out. The paper works with 182 of these tags. | p. 114059
term: Complexity rating | The average score from 1 to 5 that BoardGameGeek users give for how hard a game is to understand. It takes in the rulebook, the play time, the technical skill needed and how much time goes on planning rather than acting. | p. 114054
term: BGG rank | A ranking built from a Bayesian average of user ratings, which pulls games with very few votes towards the overall average so that a handful of high scores cannot push a game to the top. A game needs at least 30 ratings before it is ranked. | p. 114054
term: Domains | The eight broad groups BoardGameGeek places games in: abstract, children's, customisable, family, party, strategy, thematic and war games. The paper uses them to see which mechanics turn up in which kinds of game. | p. 114065
term: Co-occurrence of mechanics | How often two mechanics are tagged on the same game. The paper counts the pairs found together in more than 1% of its games, and notes that the most popular mechanics dominate the result simply because they are popular. | p. 114062
term: Mutual information between mechanics | A score from -1 to 1 for how much seeing one mechanic in a game tells you about another, with the popularity of each taken out. The paper looks at pairs scoring above 0.65, which it reads as mechanics that turn up together because of something they share. | p. 114063
term: Hand management | Use of cards in which players are rewarded for playing them in certain sequences set by the rules. | p. 114058
term: Card play conflict resolution | Players play one or more cards, together or one after another, to change the outcome of a conflict and to trigger special abilities. | p. 114058
term: Worker placement | Players pick single actions from a pool of available ones, usually by putting a token that stands for a worker on the action they want. | p. 114058
term: Variable player powers | Each player has a special action only they can take, or a power that changes one of the standard actions. | p. 114062
term: Dice rolling | A die is used to bring randomness into the game. | p. 114060
term: Pattern movement | Pieces that can move only in a set pattern on the board. | p. 114063
term: Static capture | A piece is captured by another piece passing over it or landing on its space. The paper points out that this only works if the pieces have some pattern of movement. | p. 114063
term: Action/event | The player plays a card that shows both action points and an event, and chooses to use one or the other. | p. 114063
term: Tug of war | A sliding marker on a track decides who wins. | p. 114063
term: Alliances | Players keep formal relationships with one another. | p. 114064
idea: Complexity is not a count of mechanics | The paper reports that how complex and engaging a game is does not follow simply from the set of mechanics it uses. What counts more is how the mechanics interact and how each one is carried out in that particular game. | p. 114051
idea: Few mechanics, deep game | Chess is tagged with four mechanics and Go with two, yet both open up a huge range of strategies. A six-year-old can learn the moves and an adult can spend years on the tactics, so a short mechanic list says little about how hard a game is to play well. | p. 114058
idea: Same mechanic, different weight | Worker placement is simple in Agricola, where each action space holds only one family member in a round. It is harder in Stone Age, where spaces need different numbers of workers and a die decides the reward, and harder again in Paladins of the West Kingdom, where workers come in several types. | p. 114058
idea: Same number of mechanics, different complexity | A game that pairs hand management with card play conflict resolution can be more complex than one that pairs acting with card drafting, although both use cards and both carry two mechanics. | p. 114058
idea: Complexity and rating move together only a little | Across the top 10,000 games, a higher complexity rating goes with a higher average rating, with a correlation of 0.48. The paper adds that BoardGameGeek mostly hears from keen players, so this cannot be read as true of all players. | p. 114056
idea: Number of mechanics is moderately tied to complexity and rating | The number of mechanics a game has correlates at about 0.34 with its complexity and 0.33 with its rating. Some highly rated games, such as Gloomhaven with 19 mechanics, carry a lot of them. | p. 114057
idea: The type of mechanic does not change the rating | When the paper averages the ratings of the games that use each mechanic, it sees no clear difference between mechanics. It concludes that which mechanics a game has does not decide how well it is rated. | p. 114062
idea: Mechanics that go with higher complexity | Of the 182 mechanics, 25 have an average complexity above 3 out of 5 across the games that use them. Order counters, loans and ownership have the highest averages. | p. 114062
idea: Hand management is rising and dice rolling is falling | The share of games using hand management grew from 4.4% in 1980 to 25.7% by 2020. More than 55% of the 1980 games used dice rolling, and the share has fallen since, even though dice rolling is still the most common mechanic overall. | p. 114062
idea: Popular mechanics bias co-occurrence counts | The pairs that turn up most often together are made of the mechanics that turn up most often anyway: dice rolling, hand management and variable player powers. The paper therefore turns to mutual information, which removes that effect. | p. 114062
idea: Worker placement is the most connected mechanic | Worker placement appears in 48 of the 66 pairs with mutual information above 0.65. It is most often paired with auction and turn-order mechanics, which the paper reads as games where players choose single actions also tend to bid for items or resources that improve their position. | p. 114063
idea: Worker placement leans on other mechanics | The paper reads worker placement's many links as reliance on other mechanics: turn order, auction, pattern movement, pieces as map and physical removal help settle the order of turns, how the action spaces are built and what the outcomes are. | p. 114067
idea: Auctions and turn order depend on each other | The paper explains the high link between auction types and turn-order mechanics by the fact that the order of bidding is usually set by a turn-order structure. Five Tribes, Keyflower and Railways of the World are named as examples. | p. 114063
idea: Pattern movement and static capture go together | Their mutual information is 0.77. Chess and Onitama are given as games that use both. | p. 114063
idea: Alliances and negotiation go together | The pair has a mutual information of 0.68 and appears in games such as Rising Sun and Cosmic Encounter. | p. 114064
idea: Most strongly linked mechanics concern actions | Most pairs with mutual information above 0.65 are about what players can do and how it is resolved, rather than about how the game is laid out. | p. 114064
idea: Mutual dependency between mechanics does not predict appeal | The average mutual information of the mechanic pairs in a game shows no clear link to its rating, complexity or BGG rank. The paper finds no relationship between using mechanics that depend on each other and how appealing a game is. | p. 114065
idea: Some mechanics are for all kinds of game | Dice rolling, hand management and variable player powers sit in the cluster of mechanics used in every domain. The paper explains this by the many ways each can be set up, and by the fact that each fits both simple and complex designs. | p. 114067
idea: Abstract games leave out some mechanics | Mechanics used in every domain except abstract games, such as worker placement and network and route building, tend to rely on hidden information, a theme or randomisers like dice, which games such as Chess and Go do without. | p. 114066
idea: Mechanics that suit one domain only | Pattern movement appears only in abstract games, ownership only in strategy games, and singing and hot potato only in party games. | p. 114067
idea: Mechanics are coarse labels | A single mechanic name covers many different ways of setting it up, so the label alone does not settle how a game feels. The paper also notes that its data comes from avid players, not casual ones. | p. 114068
