term: Game | An activity that gives the players a common goal whose result has no bearing on anything outside the game. DeKoven treats a game as a social performance that exists only while the players keep making it. | under 'Descriptions'
term: Play | Acting out anything that is not for real, so that nothing carries over as a consequence. A play fight leaves nobody hurt. | under 'Descriptions'
term: Playing a game | The special case where play and game are both present. The game is the script, and the players choose how to act it out. | under 'Descriptions'
term: Well-played game | A game that reaches a high point of skill and ease because of the way everyone in it plays. It is judged by the quality of the playing together, not by the score or the winner. | ch. 1
term: Play community | The group of people who play together and take each other's safety, willingness and trust as more important than any one game. The group decides whether a game suits it. | ch. 3
term: Game community | A group that has come together for one game, such as a bowling league or a bridge club, and judges itself by winning at that game. DeKoven sets it against the play community. | ch. 3
term: Intention of playing well together | The shared aim that sits above any game or score. Players have to renew it again and again, because it does not stay fixed once stated. | ch. 2
term: Convention | A general rule that holds across many games, such as taking turns or playing a game to its end. Breaking a convention usually costs more than breaking one rule of one game. | ch. 2
term: Familiarity | How well players know a game and each other. It lets people trust each other's moves, and a variation of a known game can stay within it. | ch. 2
term: Hint rule | An agreement made before play about when a stuck player may ask for a clue and how the clue-giver answers. It can be written into the rules, such as allowing a hint after the fifth guess. | ch. 4
term: Guessing game | Any game where one player holds information and another tries to reach it. The kinds of clue allowed decide which guessing game it is. | ch. 4
term: Twenty questions | A guessing game with three limits: only yes or no answers, a fixed number of questions, and a free category clue at the start such as animal, vegetable or mineral. | ch. 4
term: Mastermind | A code game where one player sets a hidden row of coloured pegs and the other guesses it. The setter replies with pegs that show how many colours and positions are right. | ch. 4
term: Fairness | A game's promise that all players follow the same rules and have the same chance to win. Young children use the word to mean that they get the prize. | ch. 4
term: Cheating | Doing something to give yourself more than an even chance to win. DeKoven separates this from rule-breaking done to keep a game going. | ch. 4
term: Well-timed cheat | Breaking a rule at a moment when the game is stalling and everyone takes the break as a way back into play. In musical chairs, a child carried his chair along and the group laughed and joined in. | ch. 4
term: Boundary | The line between a game and everything outside it. It lets a player step out without stopping the game, and keeps outside matters from entering. | ch. 4
term: Base | A marked place or posture, such as a tree or a squat in stoop tag, where a player is safe but cannot play. The player is in the game without taking part for a while. | ch. 4
term: Time out | A call that suspends a game without ending it, so that a player can ask for a rule to be explained. It is not part of the play itself. | ch. 4
term: Interference | A call that something from outside the game, such as a pole or a stranger, has spoiled the last play so that it does not count. | ch. 4
term: Fair witness | A person outside the contest, such as an umpire or referee, who is given power to stop the game and judge. Players hand over this duty so that they can play fully. | ch. 4
term: Regulations | Rules that have grown into an official code kept by officials, so that players no longer own them. DeKoven says this moves a community towards being a game community. | ch. 4
term: Spectator | A person who watches and does not play. Watchers can push the players to put on a display instead of playing. | ch. 4
term: Display | Playing to be seen, with the watchers' satisfaction counted ahead of the players' own. DeKoven sets it against play. | ch. 4
term: Coach | A person who looks after the players' ability to play, including taking a player out when the game has become a matter of personal proof. | ch. 4
term: Practice game | A game declared void before it starts, so that everyone can learn the rules. It can begin with a few rules and add more in later rounds. | ch. 4
term: Playing mind | The side of a player that is inventive and loose and follows the moment. It works together with the gaming mind. | ch. 5
term: Gaming mind | The side of a player that is concentrated and set on the goal. DeKoven says a game stays well played while it keeps a dialogue with the playing mind. | ch. 5
term: Bent rule | An exception that everyone sees and accepts, made for one player to help the game along. In volleyball a tired player asked to play near the net and was allowed. | ch. 5
term: Borrowed rule | A rule taken from another version of a game and attached to the one being played. In solitaire a player can switch from turning every third card to turning each card. | ch. 5
term: Changed game | DeKoven's definition is a variation that needs a new strategy. Swapping X and O for other marks changes the look and leaves the game as it was. | ch. 5
term: Handicap | A rule that makes the challenge more even when players differ in skill. Examples are starting with fewer pieces, playing blindfolded or using the wrong hand. | ch. 5
term: No takebacks | A convention that once a move is made it stands. It slows a player's thinking less, because the other player does not have to plan for a move being undone. | ch. 5
term: Score | A system of points that rewards chosen things. Giving points for bravery, grace or keeping a rally going instead of for winning shots makes a different game. | ch. 5
term: Volley | Hitting a ball back and forth to keep it in play, with no points. In Ping-Pong it can serve as a game of its own. | ch. 1
term: Hypocrite | A tic-tac-toe variant DeKoven names, where either player may place either mark as long as the move finishes three in a row. | ch. 5
term: Old Nick | A character in a tic-tac-toe variant called tit-tat-toe. Points from tied games go to him, and the next player to win takes them all. | ch. 5
term: Quitting | Leaving a game for any reason without the leaving meaning anything about the player. DeKoven treats the freedom to quit as the partner of the freedom to play. | ch. 6
term: Giving up | Letting yourself lose so that the other players can carry on. It is a further step than quitting, used when the game has no way out. | ch. 6
term: Endless game | A game with no boundary or end point that can be played anywhere and pulls others in. In Echo, a player repeats whatever the other says, and words cannot stop it. | ch. 6
term: Safe area | In DeKoven's classroom, a place set aside where anyone could sit out for any length of time without being asked to join. One girl stayed there for weeks before she played. | ch. 6
term: Elimination game | A game where players who lose drop out and the rest play on, as in dodgeball, Simon says and musical chairs. Eliminated players wait on the side. | ch. 6
term: Cooperative game | A game that ends when all players have reached the goal or all have given up. Building a human pyramid is the example. | ch. 6
term: Thar's a bar | A silly line game where each player points at an imaginary bear with a growing set of body parts, until everyone leans and falls. Nobody can succeed at it. | ch. 6
term: Interlude | A planned break, such as halftime or a huddle, that suspends the game without ending it. Rituals like a handshake or a bow can serve the same purpose. | ch. 6
term: Postlude | A shared ending after play, such as golf's nineteenth hole, or beer and pizza, where players review moments of the game and rejoin each other. | ch. 6
term: Tournamentality | A state of mind where each game is a chance to get even for earlier losses. DeKoven says it removes the freedom to quit and to change the game. | ch. 6
term: Prelude | A ritual or set-up before the main action. In the children's game lemonade, the chanting and miming lead into the chase that players actually want. | ch. 7
term: Lemonade | A children's game where one team mimes a trade and the other guesses it, then the guessers chase the miming team to a base line. DeKoven calls the miming part a prelude to tag. | ch. 7
term: Games preserve | A place built for play communities, with many kinds of games, quiet and active areas, and care for the pieces. DeKoven built one in a barn on a farm. | ch. 8
term: New Games | A movement and set of games in which rules are a starting point and players adjust them. The games are called new because they are kept new by how people play them. | ch. 8
term: Hug tag | A tag game where a player is safe while hugging someone. The length of a hug is not fixed, and a rule such as no repeat hugs was tried by the group. | ch. 8
term: Earthball | A six-foot ball painted like a globe, used in New Games events for games with large crowds. | ch. 8
term: Playing for keeps | Playing with real consequences, such as money, outside the game. | ch. 9
term: Con game | A fairground gambling game where the operator's skill or hidden advantage means the player is likely to lose. The player who keeps betting is called a sucker. | ch. 9
term: Well-struck bargain | A haggle where both sides reach a price and each leaves thinking they got the larger share. DeKoven reads the haggling itself as a form of play. | ch. 9
term: Stakes | What the players put at risk or play for, such as money, meaning, learning or dignity. DeKoven says stakes help give a game focus, as a score does. | ch. 9
term: Ante | The sum each player puts in at the start of a round of poker before cards are dealt, also called buying in. | ch. 9
term: Fold | Leaving a round of poker when the chance of winning looks too low to match the stake. DeKoven counts it as a way to quit with honour. | ch. 9
term: Poker face | An expression that gives away nothing about one's hand. It lets a player take part in something outside their control. | ch. 9
term: Indian poker | A poker variant where each player holds one card on the forehead, so everyone sees everyone's card except their own. | ch. 9
term: Dealer's choice | A rule in poker where the dealer picks the variant for that hand. DeKoven adds that all players need to agree on the variant. | ch. 9
term: Dangerous play | Play that includes real physical risk. DeKoven says it is play only when every person involved knows the consequences and has chosen to be in it. | ch. 9
term: Soft war | A form of fighting game, such as with styrofoam swords, where no one is hurt. DeKoven credits the idea to Stewart Brand. | ch. 9
term: Playing to win | Accepting the conditions of the game and judging your moves by how they reach its goal, while staying inside its rules. | ch. 10
term: Having to win | A condition where a player cannot leave the game and will bend any rule or ignore any person to win. The game's rules are unchanged but it no longer feels like play. | ch. 10
term: Overtime | An added period of play agreed so that a game can reach a result when time has run out. DeKoven uses it to show that agreements about ending can be changed. | ch. 11
term: Completion | The sense of a game being finished. DeKoven says winning ends a game but does not give completion. | ch. 11
term: Purposelessness | Play without an aim beyond itself. DeKoven says a goal inside a game lets people reach it, and that play itself has no purpose. | ch. 11
term: Instrumentalization | Treating games as tools for goals outside play, such as teaching or health. Zimmerman uses the word to name what DeKoven's book resists. | foreword
idea: A game is well played only when everyone plays well | DeKoven takes readers to watch a professional match that ends 12 to 0. The winning team stops caring, so the game fails even though it had moments of skill. Skilled play has to be shared across both sides for the game as a whole to count as well played. | ch. 1
idea: A hidden handicap changes what a win means | Two friends play Ping-Pong, and one player uses the wrong hand without saying so. The other player wins and feels cheated, because the win said nothing about the match. Telling the other player first would have kept the shared game. | ch. 1
idea: The score does not measure how well people played | DeKoven's test is how well the players played together, not who won. Two people volley in Ping-Pong for an hour with no points and say it was the well-played game they sought. | ch. 1
idea: Players need to renew their agreement to play well together | A shared intention made at the start does not hold itself. Needs shift and players get absorbed in a rule and forget why they are playing, so the aim has to be restated whenever it slips. | ch. 2
idea: A player has to feel safe before being willing | In the mountain-climbing example, a player accepts the risk of falling but not a partner cutting the rope. Willingness rests on knowing that nothing will be risked beyond what was agreed. | ch. 2
idea: Shared conventions let strangers play | Rules such as taking turns and finishing the game work across many games. They give people who have never met a reason to trust each other without first getting to know each other. | ch. 2
idea: The authority over a game can sit with the people playing | Official rules grow when games spread to national leagues, and the players hand control to officials. In a play community the group decides whether a game suits it. | ch. 3
idea: Rules can fall away as trust grows | As a play community builds trust, players look for games with fewer and fewer rules. Eventually they run through the woods playing follow the leader with no named game. | ch. 3
idea: A hint helps only by the amount the other player wants | A clue that says too little does not touch the problem, and one that says too much gives the answer away. In Mastermind, the person who set the code has to judge what the solver needs. | ch. 4
idea: Rules for help work when agreed before play | If players have settled how and when a hint may be asked, the asking does not read as a sign of failure. DeKoven suggests a rule like tapping twice on a peg to ask for a hint. | ch. 4
idea: Fairness develops as players grow | Young children call a game fair if they get the prize. Older players mean equal rules and an equal chance to win, and later a handicap can restore the chance when skill differs. | ch. 4
idea: Some rule-breaking keeps a game going | In musical chairs, DeKoven removed no chairs so no child was out. A child then carried his chair along, the group laughed and all took chairs on the march. The rule broke but the game was still musical chairs in spirit. | ch. 4
idea: Boundaries and bases serve different needs | A boundary lets a player leave the game. A base lets a player stay in but rest, though the player cannot play while safe. In stoop tag, the player who is it has no base, so the chase would stall if all players squatted. | ch. 4
idea: A game needs a way to rest as well as a way to end | Interludes such as halftime, huddles and a bow before a match let players pause the game without ending it. Baseball is full of small pauses between each pitch. | ch. 6
idea: Handing power to officials shifts attention from the community to the game | Players who give umpires the final word can concentrate on playing. Over time the rules turn into regulations kept by others, so the players lose control. DeKoven suggests players take turns as referee. | ch. 4
idea: Watchers change how people play | Spectators remind players that excellence is expected. Players then begin to perform for them, and a coach is needed to bring attention back to the game. | ch. 4
idea: A player who has to prove something stops playing | A player who takes the game personally cannot afford to lose and hogs the ball. DeKoven's coach takes the player out to notice the tightness and the anger. | ch. 4
idea: Players can change a game when it stops working | When a game stops holding one player's gaming mind or overwhelms the playing mind, the cause is an imbalance. DeKoven says changing the game restores equilibrium, so the community keeps the right to do so. | ch. 5
idea: Change one rule at a time | Playing with tic-tac-toe, DeKoven tries the grid size, the length of the winning row, tied-game rules, extra marks and moving pieces. He advises changing one rule, then watching what else has to change to restore balance. | ch. 5
idea: Many rules can change without changing the game | Using letters or colours instead of X and O, or drawing a line through three in a row, leaves strategies as they were. A change that matters is one that needs a new strategy, such as allowing two turns in a row. | ch. 5
idea: A proven game has reasons for each rule | In tic-tac-toe the winning row has to be three. DeKoven tried two in a row and the first player always won. Changing a rule in a balanced game means other rules usually need changing. | ch. 5
idea: Too many changes leave nothing to play | Groups can get so taken with changing rules that they change something every five minutes. They end up without a game to play, and some members feel lost and leave. | ch. 5
idea: Change the game before changing the people | A game is made up for the players, so adjusting the game is less costly than trying to adjust people. | ch. 5
idea: A handicap equalises familiarity | A skilled checkers player starts with four pieces fewer, so the game is new to both. Letting a child win on purpose does not do this, because the child sees through it. | ch. 5
idea: What the score rewards decides the game | Tennis can give points for returns that the other player misses, for bravery, or for keeping a rally going. Each choice makes a different game, and DeKoven suggests giving it a new name. | ch. 5
idea: A game needs a way out as well as a way in | DeKoven worries about a game that cannot be left, since a player who is tired or hurt would be stuck. He lists quitting, giving up, losing and winning as ways out and says each needs to carry no blame. | ch. 6
idea: Freedom to quit lets others enjoy a game | The host who kept asking whether his drinking game suited everyone spoilt it. If players know anyone can leave at any time, they can assume everyone present chose to be there. | ch. 6
idea: Quitting seldom works as a message | A player who throws down the paddle to protest hopes to raise a question. The others hear a sore loser instead, so DeKoven treats quitting as a personal choice. | ch. 6
idea: People may need to watch before they join | A girl stayed in the safe area for weeks, then joined hide-and-seek and played hard. DeKoven concluded she had been checking that she did not have to play, which made her ready to play. | ch. 6
idea: Some games happen to a person rather than with them | In a tug-of-war that starts over a skipping rope, the person never agreed to a game. DeKoven says a game played on someone is not play for them. | ch. 6
idea: Winning is a clear end but does not complete a game | Winning gives an unmistakable stopping point. It also splits players into winners and losers, and the winner has no reason left to play. | ch. 6
idea: The way a game ends changes what players feel | Elimination games draw focus to the last players but leave many waiting out. Cooperative games end with everyone together, and a silly game such as Thar's a bar ends in a pile that nobody wins. | ch. 6
idea: Players who carry a grudge from one game into the next stop playing | DeKoven calls this tournamentality. A player uses each new opponent to pay back an old loss, so quitting and changing the game are no longer open. | ch. 6
idea: A well-played game cannot be repeated exactly | After a game goes well, the players have changed and the same game cannot give the same experience. Efforts to prolong it with more rituals and breaks shrank the game, until players were honouring a memory. | ch. 7
idea: Players can try games that might not work | If everyone shares the trial, a failed game blames nobody. The inventor does not carry the fault, and the group has still shown its willingness. | ch. 7
idea: Themes in games a group enjoys point to other games | A group that enjoys tag and capture the flag likes chasing. A group that likes pool may try marbles or darts, which share aim and contact. DeKoven treats what players enjoy as firmer evidence than what they dislike. | ch. 7
idea: Silliness depends on trust and clarity | Players can be silly together only if everyone can tell it is a joke and feel safe. A person who is actually hurt may be taken as pretending, so silly play needs a way to step out of it. | ch. 7
idea: A place for play is part of a play community | Toy stores, chess clubs and arcades each offer few kinds of game or discourage playing. DeKoven's games preserve combines quiet, active and dancing spaces, with games cared for by everyone. | ch. 8
idea: Rules can say how a game could be played | In New Games, referees describe the rules last used and invite players to adjust them. In hug tag, the group tried no repeat hugs so that the player who is it has a chance. | ch. 8
idea: Playing for keeps works while players can afford to lose | Poker played for pennies, with beer, keeps the play. If a player loses too much, the hurt is real and the game stops. | ch. 9
idea: A stacked game needs players to know it first | Players accept roulette or a slot machine where the house has the higher odds because they know it. Cheating secretly makes them feel the game was changed after they had bet. | ch. 9
idea: Chance and skill are both parts of a card game | In poker, a player works with probability, avoiding an inside straight, and with confidence in a bid. The player can fold, which DeKoven treats as a way of quitting with honour. | ch. 9
idea: Danger can be part of play only by choice | Games with real risk, such as a mountain climb, are play only when every participant knows the risk and chose it. A modern war has people on the receiving end who did not choose it. | ch. 9
idea: Stakes give a game its focus and stop working once they replace the game | Players can play for money, meaning, learning or dignity. When the thought of what happens on winning pushes out the playing, the game is no longer play. | ch. 9
idea: Playing to win and having to win are different | Both follow the same rules. A player playing to win keeps a second voice that says it is only a game, while a player who has to win has one driving voice and no way to leave. | ch. 10
idea: Everyone who competes has to be competing for the challenge to work | A golfer who only wanders the course makes the other player's effort seem foolish. DeKoven says the players need to agree that they are all playing to win. | ch. 10
idea: A visible goal helps a game look legitimate to outsiders | DeKoven's class took games out onto the college quad and chose games with clear aims so that watchers could see an achievement. | ch. 10
idea: Agreements about how to end can themselves be changed | Teams agree to end at a given time or when a winner is named, and still add overtime. DeKoven concludes that the need to finish well outweighs the original agreement. | ch. 11
idea: Players can be surprised by their own play | In DeKoven's account, one part of a person works hard at the game while another part watches and is delighted. A game gives the first part a task that holds its attention. | ch. 11
idea: Games are made to be changed | Zimmerman reads the book as a manual for treating games as things to bend, break and rebuild. Examples include the many tic-tac-toe variants and the many ways to play marbles in the appendix. | foreword
idea: A game's worth does not rest on an outside goal | Zimmerman contrasts the book with the claim that games are there to fix education or health. He compares it to a meal valued for taste and company as well as nutrition. | foreword
