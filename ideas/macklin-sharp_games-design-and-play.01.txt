term: Game design | The work of planning how a game runs: its core actions, themes and, above all, the play it gives the people who take part. | p. xiv
term: Game development | The whole job of making a game: design, programming, art, writing, sound, testing, selling and more. Design is one part of it. | p. xiv
term: Game production | Building the game that a finished design describes. The authors compare design to an architect's plans and production to the building crew. | p. xv
term: Play | The thinking and doing that happen when people take part in a game, including how they deal with each other through its rules. | p. xvi

term: Action | Something a player gets to do in a game, such as kicking a ball or running on a field. | p. 7
term: Rule | An instruction that says what players can and cannot do. Rules allow some actions and block others. | p. 8
term: Goal | What the players try to reach while playing. It can be a score to beat or just an experience to have. | p. 8
term: Object | A thing players handle during play, such as a ball, a net, money or a character's health. | p. 8
term: Playspace | The area in which a game takes place and where its objects sit, such as a field, a board or a sheet of graph paper. | p. 9
term: Players | The people who set the game going by using actions and objects in the playspace under the rules. The authors count them as one of the six elements of a game. | p. 9
term: Iterative game design process | A cycle of imagining an idea, building a rough version, testing it with players and judging the results, repeated several times. | p. 10
term: Second-order design | Design where the maker shapes the game and the players then decide how, when and why to play it, so the play is shaped only indirectly. | p. 10
term: Space of possibility | All the different play experiences a game can give, set by how its actions, rules, goals, objects and playspace fit together. | p. 11
term: Game state | A snapshot of one moment in a game: where players and objects are, the score, and how far the players have got towards the goal. | p. 12
term: Constraint | A limit placed on what players can do with objects, other players or the playspace, put there to shape the play. | p. 16
term: Lusory attitude | Bernard Suits' name for players' willingness to accept a harder or less direct way of doing something for the sake of the play it brings. | p. 16
term: Direct action | An action where the player touches an object or the playspace straight away, such as hitting a pinball with a flipper. | p. 19
term: Indirect action | An effect that happens without the player touching the thing itself, such as the ball setting off a bumper after the flipper hits it. | p. 19
term: Challenge | The way a game pushes back against the player. It can come from a hard skill, a hard goal or hard ideas in the content. | p. 25
term: Flow state | Csikszentmihalyi's name for deep focus and enjoyment, reached when the challenge matches the skill of the person. | p. 25
term: Absorption | Being drawn deeply into play and giving oneself over to it, without needing a match between challenge and skill. The folk game ninja is an example. | p. 26
term: Skill | How well a player has mastered an action in a game. | p. 28
term: Strategy | A player's ability to work out the ways of acting that lead to the goal. | p. 28
term: Chance | The use of randomness in a game. The more chance, the harder it is to plan ahead. | p. 28
term: Uncertainty | Not knowing what will happen next. It can exist with no chance at all, as when you cannot tell where an opponent will throw. | p. 28
term: Decision-making | The player's choosing of the next action from what the game state shows. | p. 31
term: Feedback | The information a game gives back about an action and its effect on the game state, such as a character moving when a stick is pushed. | p. 33
term: Action-outcome unit | A pairing of one action and the result it brings, which then leads to the next action. Play is a long chain of these. | p. 31
term: Turn-based game | A game where players act one after another, which leaves time to think about the game state. | p. 32
term: Real-time game | A game where everything moves at once and the state keeps changing, so players have little time to read it. | p. 32
term: Abstraction | Reducing a complex thing to a simpler game form. The book names three uses: no representation at all, simplifying an everyday activity, and simplifying a real-world system. | p. 35
term: Abstract strategy game | A game whose pieces and board stand for nothing in particular, such as Go. | p. 37
term: Theme | The framework a game uses to represent itself, which helps players grasp how its world works. | p. 37
term: Storytelling | Using story ways of shaping experience inside a game, such as text, scenes or the order in which things are found. | p. 40
term: Storyworld | An invented world in which players act as characters, and whose events unfold from what those characters do. | p. 40
term: Context of play | When, where and with whom a game is played, and on what device or in what setting. | p. 43

idea: Six elements make up every game | Actions, goals, rules, objects, playspace and players are present in any game. Soccer shows all six: kicking and running, scoring, the ban on hands, the ball and nets, the field, and the teams. Games differ in how they combine them. | p. 7
idea: Rules both allow and limit | Rules say what players cannot do as much as what they can. In soccer the ban on hands is what makes the bicycle kick or well-timed tackle possible at all. The limits give players something to work around. | p. 8
idea: A goal is what turns a toy into a game | A soccer ball alone is a toy that can be thrown or drawn on. Inside a game with a goal, the ball takes on special meaning. Without a goal, players tend to invent one. | p. 8
idea: A goal can drive play or only start it | In soccer, scoring sits behind every action. In Exquisite Corpse, a folded paper drawn on in turn, there is no score, and the goal of making a drawing together only sets the play going. | p. 9
idea: Changing one action changes the whole experience | Dear Esther keeps the first-person walking and looking of a shooting game but removes shooting. That one removal turns it into a game of exploring a story world. | p. 10
idea: The designer shapes play only at a distance | The designer fixes the elements, but players choose when, why and how to play. Surprising results appear when players act inside the system, which is why testing and revising are needed. | p. 10
idea: Games can have narrow or wide spaces of possibility | Queers in Love at the End of the World gives ten seconds and a few choices, so its space is narrow and focused. Minecraft lets players build ships, rollercoasters and cities, so its space is wide. The more focused the experience, the smaller the space. | p. 11
idea: The space of possibility can grow during a game's life | Until 1980 nobody tried jumping under the basketball backboard from one side of the basket to the other. When Dr. J did it, a new set of moves was added to the game. | p. 12
idea: Constraint makes a goal worth pursuing | In soccer, banning hands makes putting the ball in the net a puzzle, where carrying it would be easy. Flywrench combines flapping with colour-matching, and Octodad uses a floppy octopus body, so each game gets its feel from what it blocks. | p. 19
idea: Direct and indirect actions trade control for discovery | Hitting a pinball is direct, but what the ball then does to ramps and bumpers is indirect. More direct actions give finer control. More indirect actions give less control and more finding out how the world behaves, as in Proteus. | p. 21
idea: Goals range from firm to loose | Soccer has a countable goal. Slam City Oracles has a score so silly it works as a joke. Dog Eat Dog counts tokens, but only to decide how the story ends. Sunset stacks three goals: a task, a day's list and the game. | p. 22
idea: Flow is one experience among several | Flow arises when challenge fits skill, and designers use it to set difficulty. The authors say games can also aim at other things, such as a hard story or the absorption of ninja, depending on what the designer wants to explore. | p. 25
idea: Challenge comes in three kinds | A game can test skill, test whether the player reaches the goal, or test the player's ideas through its content. In the game about a woman's balanced life, the posing task is hard and so is the content, since early life choices make later poses harder. | p. 27
idea: Chance and strategy pull against each other | More chance leaves less room for strategy. Darts has skill and no chance. Roulette has almost no skill and nearly all chance. Poker mixes them, since players see only 7 of the 52 cards by the end. | p. 28
idea: Uncertainty can exist without chance | Basketball has no dice, yet players cannot know how the ball will leave the rim or where teammates will be. Players cope by reading the changing game state quickly. | p. 30
idea: Play is a loop of decisions and feedback | The player acts, the game shows what happened, and the player decides again. In Nidhogg, moving, stabbing, a screen change towards the goal, and the serpent at the end are all feedback. Even Howling Dogs, a text game with no score, works this way. | p. 34
idea: The timing structure sets the kind of thinking | Chess gives each player time to read the board and think. Speed Chess has sixteen players moving at once, so nobody can follow the whole state and play turns instinctive. | p. 33
idea: Abstraction has three uses | It can remove representation altogether, as in Go. It can simplify an everyday activity, as Pong does to tennis. It can model a real system as a game, as Pandemic does with the spread of disease through cubes on cities. | p. 37
idea: Even abstract games carry a theme | Chess is called abstract, yet its pieces suggest a war over territory. The king is slow, the queen quick, the pawns many, and the shapes of their moves match those roles. Theme gives players a handle on how the world works. | p. 38
idea: Theme can be tight or light | Way ties its theme of wordless communication to every goal and action. Kentucky Route Zero uses theme mainly as mood. Chess carries a light theme over a full set of rules. | p. 40
idea: Story can sit in the rules, the world or the players | Braid tells story through level texts, a menu and time rewinding. Gone Home tells it by moving through a house. Dog Eat Dog lets players make the story. In Conversations We Have in My Head, story is the whole activity. | p. 42
idea: The same game can be reshaped for a new setting | Jenga Giant uses big blocks for bars and gardens, where the fall is a spectacle. Tiny Games asks where the player is before offering a game. The Jenga variants show that context can change a game's form. | p. 44
term: Genre | A label such as shooter or platformer that groups games by convention. The authors think it limits design, since it points a maker at other games instead of at the play they want to give. | p. 48
term: Kinds of play | The authors' way of sorting games by what players do, such as compete, cooperate, perform or simulate, instead of by genre. The kinds can be blended. | p. 48
term: Competitive play | Play in which some players win and others lose, and players or teams try to come out ahead of an opponent. | p. 48
term: Local multiplayer game | A videogame played by two or more people in the same room in front of one screen. | p. 48
term: Yomi | A Japanese idea of knowing your opponent's mind. In play it means guessing what the other player will do, and guessing that they are guessing you, in layers. | p. 49
term: Endless runner | A single-player game where a character keeps running and the player jumps obstacles until failing, with the score set by distance. Canabalt is the example. | p. 49
term: Asynchronous competition | Players compete by comparing scores or performances, not by playing against each other at the same moment. | p. 50
term: Head-to-head competition | Players face each other directly, so each has to think about the other's choices as well as their own. | p. 51
term: Competition against the machine | A player pits themselves against the challenge of the game itself, as in Desert Golfing. | p. 51
term: Symmetrical competition | Competition where all players have the same actions and aim at the same goal. | p. 51
term: Asymmetrical competition | Competition where players have different actions, objects or goals, as in a sniper hunting a spy. | p. 52
term: Nonplayer character | A character in a game that the computer controls instead of a player. | p. 52
term: Cooperative play | Play in which players work together to reach the game's goals. | p. 53
term: Symmetrical cooperative play | Cooperation where all players have the same actions and attributes, so the players themselves work out who does what. | p. 53
term: Asymmetrical cooperative play | Cooperation where players have different roles and abilities, as with the Medic and Dispatcher in Pandemic. | p. 54
term: Symbiotic cooperation | Cooperation where players depend on each other to play at all. In Way, one player cannot see what the other sees. | p. 55
term: Skill-based play | Play that asks players to build skill at the game's actions in pursuit of its goal. | p. 55
term: Active skill | Skill in physical timing and movement, such as the precise jumps of Super Meat Boy. | p. 55
term: Mental skill | Skill in thinking, such as remembering a path and tracing its mirror image in The Witness. | p. 55
term: Experience-based play | Play built around exploring, unfolding a story or being together, instead of beating a skill challenge. | p. 57
term: Whimsical play | Play of silly actions and unexpected results that gives a dizzy, amusement-ride feeling. Hit Me and QWOP are examples. | p. 62
term: Role-playing | Play in which stories come out of players taking on roles under a loose set of rules. | p. 65
term: Games of emergence | Jesper Juul's name for games whose possibilities come largely from how players use the actions, objects and playspace, such as story-making games. | p. 66
term: Games of progression | Jesper Juul's name for games where the player makes decisions but every outcome was written in advance by the makers. | p. 67
term: Performative play | Play whose core is performance, so it is dramatic to watch as well as to take part in. Charades and Twister are examples. | p. 68
term: Expressive play | Play that expresses a feeling or an idea, whether the designer's or the player's. | p. 71
term: Simulation-based play | Play inside a simplified model of a real system, which also puts forward a point of view on that system. | p. 72
term: Top-down simulation | A model that shows a whole system from above, taking in many factors in simple form, as The McDonald's Videogame does. | p. 74
term: Bottom-up simulation | A model that puts the player in one small role to show a larger system from within, as Papers, Please does. | p. 74
idea: Kinds of play open more options than genres | Genre points a designer at what similar games already do. Asking what kind of play to give players, such as fast choices, slow strategy, story or cooperation, opens more options. The kinds mix like ingredients in a dish. | p. 48
idea: Yomi gives competitive games depth | In Nidhogg a player guesses her opponent will parry, he guesses she thinks that and jumps, and she guesses again. The layers appear when both players chase an explicit goal and each has to read the other. | p. 49
idea: Competition has several shapes | Nidhogg is player against player. Canabalt compares scores, even in tournaments where a crowd watches players take turns. Desert Golfing is player against the game. Each puts pressure on a different part of play. | p. 51
idea: Symmetry and asymmetry apply to both competing and cooperating | In Johann Sebastian Joust every player has the same moves. In Spy Party one player hides as a party guest and the other is a sniper with one bullet. In cooperation, Portal 2 gives both players the same actions while Pandemic gives different roles. | p. 52
idea: Symmetrical cooperation leaves roles to the players | In Portal 2 both robots shoot portals, run and jump the same way. Because the game assigns no role, players decide between themselves who does what. | p. 53
idea: Symbiotic cooperation makes working alone close to impossible | In Way the player who moves cannot see the full puzzle, and the player who watches can. They have to invent body-language signals to pass the information across. | p. 55
idea: Skill games shape who will keep playing | Super Meat Boy takes hundreds of failed tries to master timing. Games asking for exact skill need more practice, which narrows who commits to them but raises the weight of a win. | p. 56
idea: Experience-based play can sit inside a competitive frame | Ninja has a goal of hitting a neighbour's hand, yet most groups play for the pose and the shared silliness. The designer has to leave room in the space of possibility for this. | p. 58
idea: Chance mixed with strategy needs managing | Threes adds a random tile after each move but shows the next tile's colour. In Android: Netrunner players shuffle decks they built, so they put three copies of a key card in a 45-card deck to improve the odds of drawing it. | p. 60
idea: Too much chance removes decisions | Candy Land is a spinner and a move, with no choice at all. Young children enjoy it for its pictures and make-believe, but the lack of decisions is what many older players find thin. The authors compare chance to a spice that can be too much or too little. | p. 61
idea: Whimsy can come from the body, the controls or the concept | In Hit Me players strain to press buttons on each other's hats. In QWOP the keys map to thighs and calves so the runner collapses. In Tenya Wanya Teens a panel of unlabelled buttons sends the character to the wrong task. | p. 64

idea: Stories can be made by players or authored by makers | In Lesberation players produce the story, which is emergence. In The Path the makers wrote the six sisters and the woods, and the player only unfolds them, which is progression. Howling Dogs does the same with branching text. | p. 67
idea: Absorption leads to unselfconscious performance | Johann Sebastian Joust players are so busy keeping their controller steady that they forget how they look, so the audience sees a dance the players do not notice. idea: Simulations can be seen from above or from within | The McDonald's Videogame shows the whole system in simple form. Papers, Please puts the player at a passport desk, stamping and refusing, and shows a whole government through one repeated job. | p. 74

term: Layers of player experience | Five stages through which a player meets a game: sensory, information, interaction, frame and purpose. | p. 79

term: Story beat | A major moment in a story, which a game may mark with an image or a scene change. | p. 82

term: Attention | The act of picking out the relevant parts of what the senses take in, which turns raw data into information. | p. 86
term: Reflexive attention | Attention pulled involuntarily by something loud, fast-moving or new. | p. 86
term: Executive attention | Attention a person chooses to give, such as reading a sign or checking a health meter. Also called voluntary attention. | p. 86

term: Imperfect information | A situation where some of the game state is hidden from players, as with face-down cards. | p. 88
term: Asymmetric information space | A game where players know different things and think from different roles, as in Android: Netrunner. | p. 89
term: Symmetrical information space | A game where all players can see the same information about the state of play, as in basketball. | p. 90
term: Mental model | A player's picture of how a game works, which can differ from how it actually works and which grows as they play. | p. 91
term: Affordance | What a thing seems to be for before anyone uses it, such as a handle that suggests gripping. | p. 91
term: Perceptible affordance | What a thing visibly invites you to do, such as a keyhole inviting a key. | p. 91
term: Correct rejection | Judging correctly what a thing is not for, such as seeing that a pillow will not work as a hammer. | p. 91
term: Hidden affordance | Something a thing can do that its look does not show, such as rewinding time in Braid. | p. 92
term: False affordance | A wrong guess about what a thing can do, such as a door in a 3D game that will not open. | p. 92
term: Navigability | Whether a player knows how to move through the playspace and the game as a whole, including the menus, options and long-term goal. | p. 94
term: Consistency | A game answering the same action in the same way each time, so players can learn from its feedback. | p. 95
term: Intuitiveness | How little effort a player needs to carry out the basic actions, which grows with repeated play. | p. 95
term: Failure | Falling short of an aim in a game. Juul sorts it by where players place the blame: themselves, the game or circumstances. | p. 96
term: Frame layer | The player's earlier experience of games, life, films and culture, which shapes what they expect from a game. | p. 96

term: Explorer | In Bartle's list, a player who wants to learn the full breadth of a game's space of possibility. | p. 99

term: Killer | In Bartle's list, a player who likes to impose their will on others, by attacking, blocking or dominating them. | p. 100

idea: Record who, when, where and what happened | The authors record tester names and contact details, the date and place, the prototype tested and the note-taker, then notes on gameplay, visuals and audio, bugs, comments, observations and ideas. | p. 213
idea: Video is used with notes pointing to the moments | Recordings help the team review a session, but notes with times or levels marked let them find the relevant bits instead of re-watching hours of footage. | p. 214
idea: Input and feedback are different | Mattie Brice and Lana Polansky argue that designing around players can water down a game's message. Input changes the game to suit testers. Feedback is information the designer weighs without having to act on it. | p. 215
idea: Playtesting can calibrate expression | Jonathan Blow does not want ideas from testers but does test, to check that his games give the experience he intends. Many arts do the same through workshops, table reads and critiques. | p. 215
idea: Evaluation works like diagnosis | Playtest notes are symptoms and the design values are what the game is meant to show. Treatments range from small fixes to large changes. | p. 218
idea: Evaluation follows what, where, why and takeaway | First list small observed moments without interpretation, then note where in the game each happened, then guess why, then agree on a specific takeaway. | p. 219
idea: A suggested change may hide a different problem | If a tester says to make the score bigger, the cause may be that they miss score changes in fast play. A bigger number may not fix it, and a summary screen between matches could. | p. 220
idea: Evaluate against a checklist drawn from the design tools | The authors suggest asking about actions, goals, challenge, information, feedback, decision-making, perceptions, context, takeaways and emotions, after rereading the design values. | p. 221
idea: Responses may be unintended strengths or intended ones | Players may call a game too hard or too short when that was planned. Bending convention marks experimental design but can also signal an oversight, and design values help tell which. | p. 221
idea: A player doing it wrong shows something | A high school student said testers played the game wrong. The authors reply that the design may need work, or the testers' way may be more intuitive than the planned one. Either finding helps. | p. 222
idea: Revision has six steps | The team reviews, incubates, brainstorms, decides, documents and schedules. The time for incubating grows with the size of the problem. | p. 222
idea: Brainstorming fixes can overload the design | New ideas are welcome, but the team picks what the next prototype needs and writes down the rest for a future project. | p. 223
idea: Make a decision that can be built | Deciding is quicker than long debate, the authors say, and reaching the next buildable prototype is worth more than the ideal answer. | p. 223
term: Toy-centric design | Douglas Wilson's name for starting from a plaything, such as a new controller, and exploring what is enjoyable about it before building a game around it. | p. 229
term: Twine | A free tool for making branching text games that needs no programming or visual design, which made it attractive to anna anthropy as a model for new makers. | p. 232
term: Ludum Dare | A 48-hour game jam built around a theme. A call for 10-second games inspired Queers in Love at the End of the World. | p. 232
term: Production plan | A list of who does what by when once design is complete, usually kept as a new version of the tracking spreadsheet. | p. 236
term: Dependency | A task that cannot be done until another task is finished. | p. 236
idea: Each loop of the cycle poses and answers one question | The team theorizes a question in conceptualizing, shapes it in prototyping, answers it in playtesting and interprets it in evaluating. A loop may repeat a question or move to a new part of the game. | p. 226
idea: Failure is raw material for iteration | The authors open the chapter with a line from Beckett about trying and failing again, and say creativity is more about failure than success. | p. 226
idea: One game can pass through many forms while its goal stays fixed | The Metagame was a game show, a conference card game, a boxed party game and a magazine insert over five years. Its aim of giving people a playful structure for talking about pop culture never changed. | p. 228
idea: A conference can act as a large playtest | The conference version of The Metagame was played by more than 3,000 people. It showed the makers what worked about the card information, images and conversations before the boxed edition. | p. 227
idea: A new play setting calls for a new design loop | Moving The Metagame from conference halls and bars to living rooms and classrooms led to new play modes for smaller groups and tight loops on the rules booklet. | p. 227
idea: Playtests can shape the content of a game | For the broader version, testers of different ages and cultures named television shows, fashions and artworks, and much of the card content came from those suggestions. | p. 228
idea: A quick design can rest on long experience | Johann Sebastian Joust took shape in a 48-hour game jam, but it followed a year of work with motion controllers and then over two years of building a stand-alone version. The authors count four years in all. | p. 230
idea: A discovery can come from a happy accident | While testing controller sensitivity, Douglas Wilson and Nils walked towards each other and shoved to make the other lose. That showed that the game they wanted was a duel, not a race. | p. 229
idea: A game that others run alone needs its own teaching | Making Joust ready to run without its designer meant building menus and a setup guide that explained how to play. That took two years, ten times longer than the first design. | p. 230
idea: Some mood-based games need art before they can be tested | The Path could not be playtested for emotional effect until its environments, art and sound were well developed. So early cycles built a world to walk through, with little gameplay. | p. 230
idea: A live-editing tool blurs prototype and playtest | In Quest 3D, changing a character's behaviour or the sky's colour took effect at once, so Tale of Tales played in the world and changed it at the same time, like playing with dolls. | p. 231
idea: A design value can mean removing finished work | After hearing the finished soundtrack, the makers of The Path cut many small puzzle tasks and made the game about exploring. The authors call this being willing to kill your babies, though they would not advise a first game to follow that path. | p. 232
idea: A constraint can carry a theme | anna anthropy took a timer from another Twine game and a call for 10-second games, and set a queer couple's last 10 seconds before the world ends. The time limit stood for the pressure she felt in a long-distance relationship. | p. 233
idea: Playtests can be for function only | anna anthropy tested Queers in Love at the End of the World just for technical faults such as a broken timer or dead links. She did not seek ideas or input on content. | p. 233
idea: Not every game needs a full iterative process | Queers in Love fits the authors' method only partly, they say, and time and resources for iteration cannot be assumed. Sometimes all a maker has is a short time. | p. 234
idea: Iteration and production have opposite attitudes to failure | During iteration the team avoids getting things exactly right and fails faster. During production the work has to be right, such as server code sturdy enough for internet traffic. | p. 234
idea: A design is done when its parts are decided and tested | The authors list a complete game prototype, tests with the target audience, new players and experienced players, met design values, art direction, a sound code base, most final text and a current design document. | p. 235
idea: New and experienced testers show different things | New players show how learnable the game is. Experienced players show that it holds up over repeated play without losing interest or becoming too easy to master. | p. 235
idea: Placeholder assets can remain when design is complete | A game with many dinosaurs may have a few finished ones and only placeholders for the rest. Making the remaining models is production. | p. 235
idea: Production needs who, what and by when | The team lists people, tasks and deadlines, marks tasks that depend on others, and doubles its time estimates because people tend to underestimate. | p. 236
idea: Regular meetings in production keep a team honest | Weekly or more frequent meetings show how much was done. If nothing is reported, the team knows to set aside more time. | p. 237
term: AAA game | A game made by a large studio with a large budget. The book mostly draws its examples from small independent makers instead. | p. 250
term: Altgames | A loose grouping of games and makers who move away from genre and market concerns, tending to be smaller and more experimental in the search for expressive play. | p. 251
term: Dynamics | The shifting relationships between elements in a system, and the outcomes when players and objects interact in a game. | p. 253
term: Independent game | A game developed and published by an individual or a small team. | p. 254
term: Player character | The onscreen figure that represents the player. | p. 256
term: Play-centric | A way of designing games that puts first the experiences players have. | p. 256
term: Pseudocode | Code written in no particular syntax, which sets out the logic of a program in plain steps. | p. 257
