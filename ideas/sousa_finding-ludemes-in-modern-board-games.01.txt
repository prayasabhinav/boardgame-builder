term: Ludeme | A conceptual element of a game, usually a rule rather than a physical component. The paper adopts Browne's wording: a unit of information that can be moved from one game to another and changes how the game works in at least one plausible case. | p. 207
term: Atomic and compound ludeme | In the definition the paper uses, a ludeme can be a single element (atomic) or a combination of elements (compound). | p. 207
term: Modern board games | The recent hobby games that the paper studies. They can contain cards, boards, dice, pawns, miniatures and even electronic devices, and they centre on the players and their face-to-face decisions. | p. 208
term: Mechanism and mechanic | The paper treats a mechanism as the smallest mechanical element of a game, and a mechanic as a combination of mechanisms in motion. It says the literature often uses the two words as if they meant the same. | p. 209
term: Core mechanism and auxiliary mechanism | A core mechanism is what the players activate directly, as their main action in the game. Auxiliary mechanisms are the others, which set the system in motion and help the game progress. | p. 212
term: Progression as growth or movement | The paper groups the ways its games show progress into two kinds: growth or accumulation, where pieces are added to fill adjacent spaces, and moving or advancing, where pieces are added and removed to show movement. Both depend on a board divided into connected sections. | p. 222
term: Interaction level | A game's level of conflict between players, scored in the paper as direct conflict, low conflict or no conflict. It is read together with whether a player can be eliminated. | p. 219
idea: Which games reached number one | Paths of Glory, Tigris and Euphrates, Puerto Rico, Agricola, Twilight Struggle, Pandemic Legacy: Season 1 and Gloomhaven are the seven games that held first place on BoardGameGeek between 2001 and 2022. The author could not check the source list against other records, since none were public. | p. 212
idea: Reading the rules gives more than the database | BoardGameGeek's tags were not complete enough for the analysis, so the author read the rules and played the games. Set collection is not tagged for every game, and the author added it for Puerto Rico and Twilight Struggle after reading the rules. The paper recommends analysing games directly. | p. 219
idea: Card play is the common core | Card play is the core mechanism in three of the seven games and tile placement records players' decisions on the board. Dice rolling is less common among these games, although it is the most common mechanism on BoardGameGeek as a whole. | p. 218
idea: Players handle mostly cards | Cards are the components players handle most and they dictate the actions available. They are played from the hand to add or remove tiles and bits on the board, as in Twilight Struggle, Pandemic Legacy and Gloomhaven. | p. 217
idea: Hexagons suit realistic territory | Hexagonal grids appear in games that model territory more realistically, because the distance between the centres of adjacent hexagons is always the same. Square grids are simpler and appear in many older games. | p. 217
idea: Agricola shows chained mechanisms | In Agricola the core action is worker placement. A worker on a shared space leads the player on to tile placement, managing bits, playing cards and collecting resources, and set collection comes in during production, conversion and scoring. | p. 221
idea: Randomness is low or medium and asymmetry recurs | The seven games have low or medium randomness, and player asymmetry comes up again and again. The author reads this as support for player agency and strategic depth. | p. 219
idea: Conflict is shrinking | The paper says that apart from the two wargames, the games have no direct conflict between players: Puerto Rico and Agricola compete constructively, and Pandemic Legacy and Gloomhaven are cooperative, so all players can lose together. The author reads this as a move away from the classic form of player elimination. | p. 219
idea: Games are getting shorter, and complexity stays high | The more recent number one games tend to play in one to two hours, while complexity is usually above 3.5 out of 5. | p. 216
idea: Game type labels are contested | The paper notes doubts about calling Tigris and Euphrates a Eurogame because of its constant conflict, and about Gloomhaven, which BoardGameGeek treats as thematic. Some players also say Twilight Struggle is not a wargame because it lacks military tactics. | p. 215
idea: Progression runs through connected spaces | The author proposes that showing progress through networks and grids of connected, ranked spaces is a possible compound ludeme in modern board games, handed from game to game and changing the play. | p. 224
idea: Components and themes fit the ludeme idea less well | Physical components, colour schemes and themes seemed less related to the ludemic approach than board structure did, but they matter for analysing a game as a system. | p. 224
idea: Ludemes pull a system apart | The paper found it confusing to analyse a game as a system when the ludemic approach tends to isolate single elements, and that the ludemic approach gave less clear results than expected. | p. 225
idea: BoardGameGeek leans towards long games | BoardGameGeek users tend to prefer long, complex games, some taking over two hours to play and nearly an hour to explain, which the paper treats as a bias in the ratings. | p. 210
