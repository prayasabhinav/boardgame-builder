term: Game theory | A branch of mathematics about how competitors choose when each has a formal model of the choices. Koster notes it describes why people choose as they do in a game but does not help a designer make a game. | p. 12
term: Magic circle | The idea, from Huizinga, that a game is a protected space cut off from ordinary life, where its rules hold and outside actions do not count. Koster uses it to separate moves inside a game from cheating that reaches beyond it. | p. 34
term: Pattern | A regularity the brain can notice and store, such as the way a tic-tac-toe line wins or how cars move forward. Koster treats a game as a pattern the player learns. | p. 14
term: Chunking | The brain's habit of packing a set of steps or features into one unit that runs without thought, like the whole routine of getting dressed. Koster says learning a game means chunking its patterns. | p. 20
term: Noise | In Koster's use, any pattern we do not yet understand. A game that shows a player no pattern they can follow reads to them as noise. | p. 24
term: Grok | To understand something so completely that it becomes automatic and part of you. Koster takes the word from Heinlein and says a player who has groked a game's patterns stops finding it new. | p. 28
term: Muscle memory | The common name for skills done without conscious thought. Koster says the work is really done by nerves and brain, which is why practice can even be mental. | p. 30
term: Practice | Building a library of chunked knowledge that can be reused on new tasks. Koster's example is how years of guitar made the mandolin quick to pick up. | p. 32
term: Formal system | A set of explicit rules, usually mathematical, that leaves out the loose details of real life. Koster says most games are formal systems, which makes them clear to learn from and also limited. | p. 36
term: Fun | Koster's narrow meaning: the pleasure the brain gives when it masters a problem or takes in a new pattern. He separates it from delight, physical thrill and social pleasure. | p. 40
term: Boredom | The state Koster says arrives when a game stops offering new patterns to learn. He treats it as the signal that a game has stopped teaching. | p. 42
term: Sensory overload | The state where there is more information than the player can turn into meaning. Koster pairs it with deprivation, as the two edges a game moves between. | p. 229
term: Informal rule set | Unwritten rules that players share but never state, such as the assumptions of a tea party. Koster says a child who cries foul when one is broken shows the rules were there. | p. 52
term: Possibility space | The full range of states a game can reach, such as the values one to six on a die. The only way to learn one, Koster says, is to explore it by playing. | p. 56
term: Games of chance | Games where luck decides a single turn, such as dice rolls. Koster argues they still teach about odds because players return to them turn after turn. | p. 56
term: Graph theory | The mathematics of points joined by links. Koster says many board games can be read as graphs, which is what players meant when they called a game about vertices. | p. 224
term: Vertex | A point in a graph, such as a square on a board or a person in a social network. In Koster's note, each link between two vertices is called an edge. | p. 224
term: Cartesian coordinate space | Space described by a grid with two straight axes, the usual way a board or screen is laid out. Koster notes it is only one of many shapes a game space can take. | p. 230
term: Directed graph | A set of points joined by links that run one way only, as the chutes and ladders do on that board game. Distance on such a board is counted in moves, not in physical length. | p. 230
term: Topology | The study of what stays the same when a shape is squashed or stretched. Koster uses it to compare games: two games share a topology when one can be reshaped into the other without adding a hole. | p. 70
term: Platform game | A game in which the player crosses a landscape, either reaching the other side or touching every location. Koster uses the family to show how designers change a game by adding dimensions. | p. 70
term: Tile-based | A way of building a game space from equal squares, each holding one picture, so nothing can sit across two tiles. Koster notes early games moved piece by piece in this way. | p. 232
term: Secrets | Hidden objects scattered through a level to reward players who search it fully. In Koster's account they teach thoroughness. | p. 76
term: Time attack | A mode that asks the player to repeat a familiar task within tighter time limits. Koster says it trains speed and reflex, so it suits action games and not strategy games. | p. 74
term: Rote training | Learning by repeating a task until it runs on reflex. Koster compares the practice of an arcade game to military drill. | p. 74
term: Pick-up | A game object that gives the player a new ability when collected. The large dots in Pac-Man are the example Koster gives. | p. 233
term: Power-up | A pick-up that temporarily raises what the player can do. Galaga, Koster says, made it a standard part of shooters. | p. 78
term: Boss | An enemy larger and more powerful than those before it, usually met at the end of a run of linked levels. | p. 235
term: Combo | A scored or rewarded sequence of moves performed in the right order. Koster lists it among the few real advances in fighting games. | p. 233
term: Quantized | Forced onto a fixed set of values, as a grey picture is reduced to 256 shades. Games, Koster says, tend to be quantized models of real things. | p. 233
term: Dressing | The art, story, sound and setting wrapped around a game's rules. Koster says players see through it to the mechanics, but it still shapes how the game feels. | p. 162
term: Fiction | The story or setting a game puts over its rules. Koster compares it to the word problem in a maths class, which the player learns to look past. | p. 80
term: Abstract game | A game with no story or picture standing in for real events, such as an abstract strategy game. Koster notes many players disagree on whether fiction helps one. | p. 236
term: Software toy | A video game with no goal to reach. | p. 241
term: Hard fun | Lazzaro's name for the pleasure of meeting a challenge that tests the player's skill. Koster ties it to the state of flow. | p. 98
term: Visceral reaction | A physical thrill, such as the stretch and smash of a table-tennis stroke. Koster says it belongs to physical mastery and is not fun in his strict sense. | p. 90
term: Schadenfreude | The pleased feeling when a rival fails. Koster counts it as a social signal of pushing someone down. | p. 92
term: Fiero | The triumph shown by a raised fist when a hard task is done. Koster counts it as a signal to others of your worth. | p. 92
term: Naches | The pride felt when someone you mentor succeeds. Koster counts it as a feedback signal that keeps a group going. | p. 92
term: Kvell | The glow of boasting about someone you mentor. | p. 92
term: Delight | Koster's word for the thrill of recognising a pattern and being surprised by it, as when the Statue of Liberty appears in Planet of the Apes. He says it rewards what is already learned and is not the same as fun. | p. 94
term: Flow | A state of full concentration, named by Csikszentmihalyi, where challenge matches skill. Koster says it is not the same as fun, because flow can come from using a skill already mastered. | p. 98
term: Multiple intelligences | Howard Gardner's list of seven kinds of ability, including spatial, musical and interpersonal. Koster uses it to say different people take to different games. | p. 100
term: Systematizing brain | In Baron-Cohen's theory, a mind that favours rules and systems. Koster suggests formal game systems suit such minds, and contrasts them with empathizing minds. | p. 102
term: Cheating | Acting outside the formal limits of a game in order to win. Koster treats it as lateral thinking that shows the player has understood the game. | p. 112
term: Lateral thinking | Reaching a goal by a route outside the obvious one. Koster sees it in the cheat who chooses a wider battlefield than the game offers. | p. 112
term: Exploit | Using a loophole that sits inside the rules to skip the challenge a designer meant. Koster says players call it legal because the game allows it. | p. 114
term: Bottom-feeding | Choosing low-skill opponents or easy levels again and again to gain safe wins or lives. Koster says it follows when easy encounters pay out as much as hard ones. | p. 118
term: Ludeme | Ben Cousins's name for a basic unit of play, such as get to the other side or visit every location. Koster says checkers has just two. | p. 118
term: Preparation | The choices a player makes before a challenge that change their odds, such as healing before a fight or building a hand of cards. | p. 120
term: Sense of space | The area or network in which a challenge takes place, such as a chess board or the web of relations at a bridge table. | p. 120
term: Core mechanic | The small central rule set at a game's heart, into which content is poured. Moving a piece in chess is Koster's example. | p. 120
term: Range of challenges | The different situations a game's rules can produce, which keep the rules from being used up quickly. | p. 120
term: Variable feedback | A result that is not fully predictable and rewards greater skill with a larger return. In chess it is the opponent's reply to your move. | p. 122
term: Mastery Problem | The trap where skilled players keep winning, while beginners are knocked out in the first match before they can learn. | p. 124
term: Opportunity cost | The price of a choice that cannot be undone because the other option is now gone. Koster says a game needs failure to cost something. | p. 240
term: Red Queen's Race | Running hard just to stay in place. Koster uses it for designers who keep having to add challenges because players keep mastering them. | p. 126
term: Emergence | Patterns that arise from simple rules without being planned. Koster notes they often make games easier by opening loopholes. | p. 240
term: Designeritis | Koster's name for the habit of designers who know so many games that they fall back on known solutions. | p. 138
term: Jargon factor | The rise of specialist terms as a form matures, until newcomers cannot enter. Koster says it marks games that grow ever more complex. | p. 136
term: Apollonian and Dionysian | Two styles the arts swing between, the first reserved and formal and the second exuberant. Koster says games have mostly stayed formal. | p. 136
term: Solo, collaborative, competitive | Koster's three ways to take part in an activity: alone, with others, or against them. He lays them across a grid for each medium. | p. 140
term: Experiential, constructive, deconstructive | Koster's three roles: taking in an experience, making it, or taking it apart to see how it works. Playing, modding and hacking a game fall in each. | p. 140
term: Mod | A player-made change to a game's rules, art or content. Koster likens it to house rules in board games. | p. 241
term: Medium | A channel for conveying patterns. Koster says game is not a medium but one use of one, which he calls formal abstract models for teaching patterns. | p. 142
term: Negative space | In art, the meaning carried by what surrounds a hidden subject. Koster's example is Minesweeper, where the unseen mines are understood from the numbers around them. | p. 156
term: Nomic | A game in which changing the rules is itself part of play. Koster says even it has limits, since a rule cannot alter physics. | p. 239
term: Short-term memory limit | The finding that people hold about seven units at once. Koster says it explains why adding too many numbers to track makes a game hard fast. | p. 226
idea: Games are patterns to be learned | Koster says the brain is mainly a consumer of patterns, and a game is a pattern packed in a clear form. A child learns tic-tac-toe by playing it until every match is a draw, and loses interest once the pattern is grasped. | p. 14
idea: Fun is learning | Koster argues the pleasure of a game comes from mastering a problem or taking in a pattern, not from winning or looking. The brain releases a reward when we learn, so a player grinning at a solved puzzle is showing fun. | p. 40
idea: Boredom signals the end of teaching | When a game offers no new pattern, the player says they are bored. Koster lists six ways this happens, such as mastering everything in the first five minutes or facing a pattern that looks like noise. | p. 44
idea: Order and chaos are balanced | Koster says a game moves between too little new information and too much. An adult drops tic-tac-toe because it is too plain, and drops a game with a flood of unexplained rules because it looks like noise. | p. 42
idea: Noise is a pattern not yet understood | Static has patterns, Koster says, and what seems random is often a failure of our understanding. A first listen to jazz sounds odd until the listener picks up the beat and the flattened fifth. | p. 24
idea: Formal games are limited | Koster says the more tightly rules define a game, the sooner it can be fully worked out. Tic-tac-toe has only 125,168 possible games, so a player can see every outcome, while games with human psychology in them last longer. | p. 38
idea: Games are meant to be outgrown | Koster says any game that teaches is destined to become routine, because learning something means it is over. His own example is a typing game where sharks eat words, which gave up trying new tricks after level twelve or fourteen. | p. 126
idea: Games model reality, mostly through maths | Koster says rules are mathematical, so even abstract games reflect something about the world. Many teach odds, territory and power, which he ties to human survival. | p. 52
idea: Space is the first lesson of games | Most games, Koster says, teach the player to read a space, whether a grid or a graph of links. Tic-tac-toe teaches the lines of power across a grid. | p. 54
idea: A game cannot be learned in one turn | Even a dice game, Koster says, is played over many turns so the player can explore the possibility space. Players learn about odds by rolling again and again. | p. 56
idea: New games come from a new dimension | Koster argues shooters stopped changing once they gained power-ups, scrolling and bosses. Tetris, he says, led to variants with hexagons and 3-D, and he suggests a puzzle built on time as a new direction. | p. 78
idea: Add to a game by adding a dimension, not more of the same | Koster says designers often add more weapons instead of a new kind of challenge. A modern platformer keeps the old get to the other side goal and adds secrets and time limits. | p. 72
idea: Speed training suits reflex games | Koster says developing speed before precision is not useful, so action games push the player faster as they improve. Strategy games rarely have time attack because their tasks do not rest on reflex. | p. 74
idea: Thoroughness is a subtler skill | Koster says asking the player to find secrets teaches patience and checking every angle. He sees this as more widely useful than rote reflex. | p. 76
idea: Fiction wraps the pattern and is set aside by players | Koster compares a game's story to a maths word problem. In checkers the crowned piece could be called a wolf and the game would not change. | p. 80
idea: A game's core is separate from its theme | Koster says Deathrace is mechanically a game of catching objects on a flat field. He holds that its violent dressing is not what the game teaches, though he does not excuse the dressing. | p. 84
idea: Games and stories teach in different ways | Koster says games teach by experience and handle objects and actions well, while stories teach by empathy and inner life. The moment Floyd sacrifices himself in Planetfall happens outside player control, so it is not part of the challenge. | p. 88
idea: The word fun covers several different pleasures | Koster names mastery, aesthetic appreciation, physical thrill and social standing. He keeps the word fun for the first, which is mentally mastering a problem. | p. 90
idea: Fun is contextual | Koster says fun comes when an activity is practice, not for real. A team that says it went out to have fun is treating the game as practice, and a team that says it went to win is not. | p. 96
idea: Flow and fun are different | Flow, Koster says, comes from using a skill already mastered, while fun comes from learning. Meditation can bring flow without fun. | p. 98
idea: Different people take to different games | Koster says people vary in how they learn and in what they find easy to see. A person with high spatial or verbal ability leans toward sports or word games. | p. 100
idea: No game suits everyone | Koster says a formal system fits some kinds of mind more than others, so a difficulty ramp is likely to suit some players and not others. He compares it to books, which are bought mostly by people of certain groups. | p. 104
idea: Cheating shows the player has understood | Koster says someone who cheats is choosing a wider battlefield than the game and is using cunning. Duellists who shoot before the other has finished pacing are more likely to have children, so cheating pays in a plain evolutionary sense. | p. 112
idea: Exploits circumvent the challenge | Koster says an exploit uses a loophole inside the rules, so expert players find it first. Others resent it because the game meant to teach a technique and the loophole skips it. | p. 114
idea: Players make a game predictable | People want to win, Koster says, so they reduce risk by learning patterns. Running an easy level two hundred times to bank lives is like storing food for winter. | p. 118
idea: A game's destiny is routine | Koster says fun is a process and routine is where it ends, since learning removes the need to learn again. Designers who want games to stay fun work against how the brain operates. | p. 118
idea: Games need a set of working parts | Koster lists preparation, a sense of space, a core mechanic, a range of challenges, a range of abilities and skill in using them. Without preparation a game is left to chance, and without a range of challenges it is used up fast. | p. 120
idea: Learning needs feedback and a cost for failure | Koster says results vary with skill, easy encounters do not pay experts, and failure costs something. In chess the variable feedback is the opponent's reply. | p. 122
idea: The Mastery Problem favours the skilled | Koster says competition removes the players who most need practice, because they are knocked out in their first match. Head-to-head games supply endless new challenges, yet beginners who face experts tend to quit. | p. 124
idea: A checklist can reveal what a game lacks | Koster gives questions such as whether the player prepares, whether more than one ability helps, and whether failure forces a retry. A no to any one marks a system worth rethinking. | p. 124
idea: Every system serves the game's lesson | A designer's sole responsibility, Koster says, is to know what the game is about and make it teach that. No system belongs in the game if it does not contribute to that point. | p. 126
idea: Fewer systems can be enough | Koster says classic games fit a few systems together neatly, as checkers has just two ludemes. Modern console games pile on many, which Koster ties to the need to keep adding challenges. | p. 126
idea: Designers try to keep games from running dry | Koster lists emergence, story, head-to-head play and player-made content as tactics. He notes each has a catch, such as emergence tending to open loopholes. | p. 128
idea: People repeat what has worked | Koster says players pick the same kind of character or solution each time. A friend of his has played the big silent type in dozens of role-playing games. | p. 132
idea: Mastered games can become a trap | Replaying a game you have fully mastered only to feel powerful betrays the game's purpose, Koster says. Playing for style points at least sets a new challenge. | p. 134
idea: Complex genres shut out newcomers | Koster says a new game genre gains rules until a priesthood of expert players forms and the barrier to entry rises. He wants games that appeal to the broad public. | p. 136
idea: Knowing too many games can limit a designer | Koster says designers sample many games and grok them fast, so they fall back on known solutions. He suggests the most inventive take ideas from outside games. | p. 138
idea: Activities can be mapped on a grid | Koster crosses solo, collaborative and competitive with experiential, constructive and deconstructive, for any medium. He fills it in for music to show that playing, making and analysing all sit on one chart. | p. 142
idea: Games are not always fun in the strict sense | Practising piano or writing can be hard work and still fulfilling, Koster says. Interacting with a game can likewise be thought-provoking or painful and still hold a pattern to learn. | p. 144
idea: Entertainment and art differ by degree | Koster says media provide information, entertainment comforting information and art challenging information. Mere entertainment becomes art when its communication is novel or very skilled. | p. 146
idea: Abstract systems can carry meaning | All media are formal systems, Koster says, so a game made of rules can still say something. Minesweeper works like an Impressionist picture because the thing you seek is never shown and repetition with variation teaches it. | p. 156
idea: Games convey general points more readily than specific ones | Koster says it is easy to make a game showing that small groups can beat large ones, but hard to show a particular rescue like the one in a film about Private Ryan. A designer needs to learn what the medium carries. | p. 158
idea: Content design and experience design are different jobs | Koster compares game design to dance and film, where a director shapes the whole experience above the writer or choreographer. He says game teams are not yet set up this way. | p. 160
idea: The dressing shapes how the rules feel | Chess might not have lasted, Koster says, if its pieces were different kinds of snot. A mismatch between a game's core and its theme can harm the experience. | p. 162
idea: Test a game with the dressing removed | Koster proposes playing with no graphics, music or story. If the bare rules are fun, the dressing can focus and magnify them, but no dressing changes the core. | p. 166
idea: Rules can carry a message | Koster describes a game where power comes from followers but healing comes from friends, and friends leave as you gain power. The goal of keeping the tribe alive makes sitting at the top a choice the player has to weigh. | p. 182
idea: Games shape players like a trellis | Koster says a game steers its players as a trellis steers a plant, and the plant also grows to escape it. He says games now produce only fun and boredom, and wants rules built with intent to teach chosen things. | p. 178
