idea: The same loss feels different when a character is long-lived | In single-player games a character is soon replaced, but in a persistent online game a player may spend months on a chosen character, so its death feels personal. Some players ask for permadeath for that reason, though Hoffman cites a survey where 2 percent asked for harsher death penalties (chapter by Erin Hoffman). | p. 113
term: Character armor | Ernest Becker's name, taken from Wilhelm Reich, for the personality a person builds as a defence against awareness of death. Hoffman links it to the hero a player builds in a game (chapter by Erin Hoffman). | p. 119
term: Screenworld | David Phelps's word for the world of interactive screen technology and online worlds, as distinct from the structure of the physical world (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 126
term: Humanistic Ethos | Phelps's set of four observable qualities for judging an activity: poetic imagination, dialogic relations, systemic thinking and existential vigor (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 128
term: Memento mori game | A game that reminds the player they are mortal, through features such as one life, fixed endings and slow movement, as Jason Rohrer calls his game Passage (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 131
term: Pseudosymbol | A symbol reduced to a trademark or political badge that only identifies a group, as Thomas Merton uses the word, in contrast to a symbol that invites feeling for others (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 130
term: Rhetorical ideology | An unquestioned prejudice or value system; Phelps says a game can help players see it without being able to remove it for them (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 133
idea: Showing the aftermath changes what a kill means | In Super Columbine Massacre RPG! the bodies of victims stay on the map, where in most games they vanish. Hoffman says this, and the use of real photographs, makes the player look at their act, and that it upset many players because it broke the usual rule that death means temporary failure (chapter by Erin Hoffman). | p. 116
idea: Games let a player beat death in a made world | Hoffman uses Ernest Becker's argument that people deal with fear of death through heroism, immortality and aggression against rival beliefs. She reads a game's hero and its repeated defeat of death as that same drive (chapter by Erin Hoffman). | p. 120
idea: Judge the activity, not the game | Phelps says claims such as 'games are good' or 'games are harmful' fail to say what is measured. He proposes the unit of study be the activity that arises where a game's design, the social practices around it and the player's own purpose meet (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 127
idea: Slowing a player down can be a design aim | In Passage the player has five minutes and watches a character age. In The Graveyard an old woman walks slowly and takes minutes to sit on a bench, and a player who wants to rush has to wait. Phelps argues both ask the player "why?" in the face of hurried activity (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 130
idea: Playing the other side can remove stereotypes | Phelps describes playing the two shooters in Super Columbine Massacre RPG! and coming to see them and their victims as people rather than as media images. He quotes Gee that a game lets you experience the other from the inside (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 132
idea: A game can let a player find the system's flaw | In Vigilance 1.0 the player watches a town through surveillance and corrects citizens, and crime seems to fall while paranoia rises. Phelps cites Bogost's reading that the player sees the harm of constant watching by running the system (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 133
term: Phantasy | Phelps's spelling for a made world that shows the sorrowful absence of something real, such as a world without laughter, as against fantasy, which is the playful presence of something unreal such as dragons (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 134
term: Subjunctive | The quality of a story or game that lets the player project their identity onto a character inside it and take part in what follows, after Jerome Bruner (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 144
term: Transformational play | Sasha Barab's name for a game's ability to work upon the player and change them while the player acts in it (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 145
term: Discernment | In Salen and Zimmerman's usage, whether a player can detect that a game action changed anything. A game with 'poor' discernment is hard to read (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 144
term: Integration | In Salen and Zimmerman's usage, whether a player can see how a change affects their later goals in the game (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 144
term: Community of practice | The shared activities around a game, such as FAQs, strategy guides, forums and other players, which Gee calls gaming as against the game itself (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 140
idea: A world can mourn by leaving something out | In Flower the player steers a petal across fields while the flower, stuck on a city windowsill, dreams. Phelps places it with works that show the absence of nature to counter what Jared Diamond calls landscape amnesia (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 135
idea: A game can be like a lesson it comments on | In Portal the player solves test chambers set by an authoritarian voice that promises cake. Phelps felt the early chambers were chore-like and the choice to disobey the voice in the last chamber was the first real choice, and he reads the game as a parody of testing and drilling in school (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 137
idea: A game that expects one reading of its scoring can mislead | Phelps tried to work out the hidden rule for unlocking songs in Rock Band 2 and stopped listening to his friends. His group later found they had unlocked every song already, and the scoring had hidden that (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 142
idea: Play with others can draw out a person's voice | Phelps cites Mary Watkins that play and dialogue let a child see through others' eyes and dream of what has not yet happened. He also cites Justine Cassell's five tenets for games: hand design authority to users, value subjective knowledge, allow many kinds of users, give users a tool to express their voice, and encourage collaboration (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 139
idea: Group goals force different play styles to meet | Thomas and Brown, cited by Phelps, note that in World of Warcraft high-level tasks can only be finished by large groups of players with different play styles. Around Portal, fans fill different kinds of roles, such as map makers, speed runners, story theorists and comic sharers, matching Bartle's four player types (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 140
idea: A simulation needs the player to be in it | Dörner's town simulations had players act as a mayor over ten turns and found that players who asked why questions reached higher scores. Phelps argues they were missing the subjunctive, since a player in absolute control of a town is not implicated by its outcome (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 144
idea: Rules that persist make a system learnable | Phelps names three properties of rule sets: serialised, so events build on earlier ones; persistent, so rules stay the same and any change can be seen; and interactive, so the world responds to the player. Constantly changing rules would not allow systemic thinking (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 144
idea: Plague makes the player carry the result of a choice | In Quest Atlantis's Plague a player gathers views on a scientist's plan to make a living organism to test cures, and the townspeople respond to the player's decisions. Phelps lists guiding questions for such design, including how a perspective is legitimised through player choice and how consequences are made meaningful (chapter by David Phelps). | p. 145
term: Perspective-taking | Using social cues, past experience and imagination to understand the motivations and needs of other players, one of three skills Gilbert finds in young gamers' ethical thinking (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 155
term: Consequence-based thinking | Weighing the effects of one's actions on oneself, other people, groups and the wider game community (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 155
term: Rule-based thinking | Interpreting the rules of a game and judging actions by whether the official or agreed rules allow them. Gilbert notes online games often lack clear rule sets, so disputes remain (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 155
term: Individualistic model | A pattern of ethical thinking in which a player judges actions by personal consequences and considers rules mainly to avoid punishment (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 156
term: Interpersonal model | A pattern of ethical thinking in which a player considers people close or similar to themselves and follows rules to promote fairness and reciprocity (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 156
term: Communal model | A pattern of ethical thinking in which a player considers the wider game community and its stakeholders, and applies the spirit of the rules to new situations (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 156
term: Basic empathy | An intermediate form of perspective-taking where a player knows others have feelings like their own but has trouble keeping in mind how others differ from the self (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 158
term: Griefing | Harassing or bullying other players in an online game (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 157
term: Guild | A formally recognised, long-lasting group within an online game whose members support each other, socialise and work together (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 162
term: Values discourse | The spoken or unspoken expression of personal or social beliefs within a social practice such as classroom teaching or family meals (chapter by J. Alison Bryant and Jordana Drell). | p. 169
term: Co-play | Parents and children playing a game together, which many mothers in the authors' data say they would like to do more with games made for it (chapter by J. Alison Bryant and Jordana Drell). | p. 168
term: Casual game | A game with lesser commitment to play than hard-core play, usually played to relax, escape or fight boredom; there is no agreed meaning (chapter by J. Alison Bryant and Jordana Drell). | p. 168
term: Patterned family interaction | A small, regular family event such as dinner time or planned viewing of a programme; Bryant and Drell treat regular family game play as this kind of ritual (chapter by J. Alison Bryant and Jordana Drell). | p. 171
idea: The same game can draw out different reasoning | Gilbert interviewed fourteen young online gamers and sorted them into three models. One player used two characters, a nice one and an evil one who scams her cousin, and cited only a rule against giving out personal information, while another considered the whole game community (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 156
idea: "It's just a game" shrinks the circle of concern | Three players Gilbert calls individualistic all justified their acts that way. One player altered a game's design to get free items, and when the company acted, it deleted the accounts of everyone who had traded with her (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 157
idea: Fairness can be applied narrowly | A player gave a prized item to a stranger who won a dice roll rather than to a friend who begged for it, because he would have been angry if treated the other way. Later he took kills by tagging every monster first, since the rules allowed it, which others disliked (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 158
idea: A rule can be judged by what it does for the community | A fifteen-year-old explained that a limit on trade values in Runescape stopped real-world trading edges, but also meant players could no longer set prices themselves. Gilbert counts this as thinking about why a rule exists, beyond fairness (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 160
idea: Belonging to a group seems to widen concern | None of the three individualistic players was in an in-game group, while all communal players except two newcomers or less-tested ones were. A group needs members to be considerate and keep roles, and Gilbert suggests it is a stepping stone beyond the self (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 162
idea: Early help from another player may set an example | Two of three players who got 'strong' help from another online player fit the communal model, and most who were excluded or taken advantage of did not. Gilbert guesses players copy those who helped them, and that the game design could reward helping new players (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 163
idea: Talk about a game extends its effect | Gilbert says one of the easiest steps is to encourage young people to talk about ethical issues they meet in games, because his interviews gave a space for that reflection that his subjects eagerly filled (chapter by Sam Gilbert). | p. 164
idea: Games in a home bring a new gathering place | The Wii was placed with the main family television, unlike earlier consoles kept in children's rooms. Bryant and Drell say this puts a game system where families gather, as earlier with television, and where patterns of interaction form (chapter by J. Alison Bryant and Jordana Drell). | p. 170
idea: The child often picks the game | Family ritual theory assumes a parent sets rituals, but Bryant and Drell note video game choice is more often made by the child, both at purchase and at play. They say the theory has to allow influence to run from child to parent (chapter by J. Alison Bryant and Jordana Drell). | p. 172
idea: Games can model sportsmanship | In sports games some show taunting and 'poor' sportsmanship and others show encouragement. The authors add that the way family members treat each other during play also becomes a model for interaction outside it (chapter by J. Alison Bryant and Jordana Drell). | p. 172
