term: Game design patterns | In this paper, semi-formal, linked descriptions of parts of a game's design that keep coming up and that concern game play, with alarms, fog of war and grinding as examples. | p. 2
term: Compound mechanics | Sicart's name for a set of related mechanics that work together inside one limited mode of interaction. Driving is his example, made up of several mechanics that together model driving. | p. 4
term: Wicked problem | A problem with many possible solutions and many personal readings of them. Each one is unique, hard to define formally, without a natural stopping point, and every test of a solution affects the people who use it. The paper places game design among them. | p. 2
term: Design language | What a field of design uses to talk about its work. The paper, following Rheinfrank and Evenson, gives three parts: a collection of available design elements, information on how they can be used together, and descriptions of when elements or combinations suit or cause trouble. | p. 3
term: Contextualization layer | The layer the authors add between patterns and mechanics. It holds scenarios that show how a pattern is meant to be used in a particular design and why that is a sound idea for the game. | p. 7
term: Intentionality | The direction of meaning in what someone does. The paper separates original intentionality, when a person means something by an action, from derived intentionality, which is another person's reading of that action. It uses the idea to explain why design decisions are hard for others to read. | p. 7
term: Intersubjectivity | Schutz's name for shared meaning, which has two parts: the medium used to communicate, such as scenarios, prototypes, speech, writing or drawing, and the shared ways of using that medium that grow up during communication. | p. 10
term: Conceptual relationship model | The authors' model of how the parts of game design relate. General patterns are instantiated through contextualization, which is broken down into mechanics, which developers turn into code. Players then read the finished code through the patterns they already know. | p. 11
idea: Patterns and mechanics overlap in meaning | Pairs such as area control and conquer, bidding and bidding, or aim and shoot and aiming and shooting appear in both collections. The paper concludes that separating the two by level of abstraction does not work. | p. 5
idea: Both patterns and mechanics can serve design and analysis | An earlier view held patterns to be for design and mechanics for analysis. The authors point out that published work uses patterns mainly to analyse games, while mechanics have been used for design insight, so both serve both purposes. | p. 5
idea: Relations between patterns are tendencies | The paper says the relations between patterns are to be read as general consequences or possible combinations, not hard links. They may occur in the mechanics that carry out the patterns, but players are not guaranteed to see them. | p. 5
idea: Mechanics lose meaning outside their game | Mechanics found in two different games are only alike in the way family members are, because a small change in a design can change the play. The paper advises treating mechanics named outside a specific game with care. | p. 6
idea: Patterns, mechanics and a layer between | The authors give patterns the place of social interaction and mechanics the place of the physical space where interaction happens, with contextualization as the layer where the two meet. | p. 8
idea: Players and designers care about different layers | Players are mostly concerned with mechanics and use patterns without noticing. Designers study other games on purpose to find the patterns in them, and use that study as a frame of reference so their design does not drift without their choosing. | p. 9
idea: Developers and researchers care about different layers | Developers work directly with mechanics to turn them into code, and the paper says they always add their own readings of what the designer meant. Design researchers sit mostly between general patterns and the relations among them. | p. 10
idea: A shared language is not enough | A shared design language does not remove differences in how designers, developers and players picture a game. The authors say it works as a base for conversation that builds shared understanding. | p. 10
idea: First impressions decide whether players stay | Because players read a new game through patterns they already know, the paper says the first impression carries weight: a player without a favourable first impression is likely to move on to another game. | p. 9
idea: Same mechanic, different verdict by context | Quickscoping in the Call of Duty games is used to show that a mechanic can be nearly identical in two games and still be judged differently, depending on the context it sits in and the tactics it is compared with. | p. 12
idea: Contextualization is a process of communication | The authors do not think contextualization can be broken down into a template. They see it as a process of communication, described through shared cues such as language, gestures and illustrations. | p. 13
