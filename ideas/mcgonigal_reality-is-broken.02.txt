term: Posttraumatic bliss | A term from Jennifer Michael Hecht for the shift in attention that follows a near-death event or a terminal diagnosis, when a person enjoys the present more and cares less about trivial things. | ch. 10
term: Dancer's high | The mix of excitement, flow and affection that comes from dancing with others, built from movement, touch, synchronised steps and a crowd moving together. | ch. 10

term: Magic circle | The social space of a game inside which players agree to act outside ordinary habits. A crowd playing together makes the circle feel safer. | ch. 10

term: Micropayment | A very small sum of money paid for one small contribution to a crowd project. | ch. 11
term: Distributed computing | Linking many separate computers over the internet so that each works on a small piece of one large calculation. Folding@home works this way. | ch. 11
term: Player investment design | The work of building rewards and long-term goals so that players feel tied to their character and to the world, and contribute because taking part is itself rewarding. | ch. 11

term: Social participation game | A game that gives players real-world volunteer tasks written to feel as clear and doable as quests in a role-playing game. | ch. 12

term: Mutual regard | Players trying hard because each expects a worthy partner or opponent, which makes the game itself possible. | ch. 13

term: Reciprocal rewards | The shared enjoyment that players produce for each other by acting as if the game matters. | ch. 13
term: Co-op mode | A way of playing in which players work together against computer-controlled opponents rather than against each other. Rock Band and Left 4 Dead are examples. | ch. 13

term: Collaboration | Working together in three ways at once: cooperating toward a goal, coordinating effort and resources, and cocreating something new that none could make alone. | ch. 13
term: Ping quotient | How readily a person reaches out over electronic networks to ask others to take part, and answers when asked. | ch. 13
term: Collaboration radar | A sense of who would suit which task, built from a large social network and from tools that show what other people are doing and are skilled at. | ch. 13
term: Emergensight | The ability to work calmly in a large, disordered collaboration, filtering noise and watching for chances nobody planned. | ch. 13
term: Collaboratory | An online space where people from many places practise working together at a large scale. | ch. 13
term: Chaotic story | A story cut into thousands of pieces and spread over many media, so that it does not exist until players assemble it. | ch. 13

term: God game | A game that lets one player shape a whole world, a people or a life over a long span. The Sims, Civilization and Black & White are examples. | ch. 14

term: Ecosystems thinking | Seeing a situation as a web of linked parts, and expecting a change in one part to reach others in surprising ways. | ch. 14

term: Super-empowered hopeful individual | A person who feels both hopeful about the future and personally able to change it, helped by networked tools. Shortened to SEHI. | ch. 14

idea: Happiness can be approached sideways | John Stuart Mill held that people cannot chase happiness directly and gain it as a by-product of other goals. Happiness hacks follow this: Tombstone Hold 'Em is a card game on its face, and the reminder of death comes as a side effect. | ch. 10
idea: A game can use the features of a place as its pieces | In Tombstone Hold 'Em the shape of a headstone gives the suit and the last digit of the death year gives the value. Because every cemetery has headstones, the game can be played in any cemetery and cannot be moved to a room elsewhere. | ch. 10
idea: Touch and partnership bring players close | Tombstone Hold 'Em is played in pairs, and a pair has to touch both stones and each other at the same moment to claim a hand. The designer chose this because cooperation and touch build trust between strangers. | ch. 10
idea: A game gives permission to break habits | Players in a cemetery are doing something that custom does not expect there. McGonigal says a crowd carries the social weight to change what counts as normal, and that the more people play, the safer it feels. | ch. 10
idea: An added obstacle moves attention off the self | Top Secret Dance-Off asks players to dance without moving their feet, or upside down, or with a tree. Because the player is busy with the odd rule, there is less room for worry about looking foolish. | ch. 10
idea: Restricting the usual moves widens who can do well | When the usual dance steps are ruled out, grace counts for less and silliness, invention and enthusiasm count for more. People who do not think of themselves as dancers can still do well. | ch. 10
idea: Feedback can be made to come only in positive form | Viewers of a dance video can add a point of beauty, humour, sneakiness or exuberance, or leave nothing. The rule keeps comments friendly and lets players post videos they would otherwise keep private. | ch. 10
idea: A disguise lowers the player's guard | Every Top Secret Dance-Off player wears the same partial mask in every quest. The mask and the superhero theme free players from the picture they hold of themselves as non-dancers. | ch. 10
idea: A hack can be built cheaply and spread by word of mouth | Tombstone Hold 'Em needs playing cards, chips and a cemetery, and its rules were posted online for anyone to adopt. Top Secret Dance-Off was launched in days on a low-cost social network service. | ch. 10
idea: Players need to see that their action had an effect | The developer of the Guardian game said the main point was to give people an "I rock" feeling, through clear purpose, visible impact and steady progress. The results page showed totals found by the crowd, such as repayments ordered from members of parliament. | ch. 11
idea: Most crowd projects fail for lack of a crowd | More than half of online collaborations never reach the number of participants needed to start. McGonigal estimates 200 million public requests for participation, which works out to about 8.5 people for each. | ch. 11
idea: Wikipedia can be read as a game | Its editors describe pages as places, vandal fights as battles, long articles as dungeons, and edit counts as experience points. They also note that players of online worlds stay at high involvement for about six to eighteen months, and so do heavy editors. | ch. 11
idea: Money can lower the wish to take part | Studies show that paying people for something they would do from interest makes them less likely to do it unpaid later. Money is also limited, so it caps how much participation a project can support. | ch. 11
idea: Match what is asked to what the crowd can do | Folding@home asks PlayStation 3 owners only to leave a console running, which every owner can do. The project reached supercomputing milestones within six months and drew one in twenty-five users of the network. | ch. 11
idea: Difficulty can adjust to the player | In Free Rice, a wrong answer makes the next question easier and a right one makes it harder, so players stay at the edge of their ability. Each right answer also adds virtual rice to a bowl that is later converted into real rice. | ch. 11
idea: A reward system can be shaped so that only honest play pays | McGonigal says players will do whatever earns the most reward, so the rules need to make honest taking part the only way to earn it. The Bungie job listing describes the same aim: rewards that cannot be exploited by players against each other. | ch. 11

idea: A real-world task can be written like a quest | The Extraordinaries gives each mission a story, a step-by-step list and a few minutes of work. In one, a player photographs a defibrillator and tags its location so that emergency services can direct people to it. | ch. 12
idea: Showing the outcome of a small act extends its reward | After a player maps a defibrillator, the organiser links news of any later use to that location. The player can then learn that the object they found helped save a life. | ch. 12
idea: A request for help can be matched to a person nearby | Groundcrew lets people post a wish and alerts others close by. The first wish was a latte for a tired dancer, delivered in five minutes by someone who saw it on the system. | ch. 12
idea: Sensitive tasks can be locked behind earned trust | In Groundcrew, work with older people, such as shopping or companionship, is open only to agents with enough reputation points who also pass a background check. The points work as a record of reliability. | ch. 12
idea: Attention paid to others can be a task in itself | In Lost Joules, players with smart meters try to save energy and others place virtual bets on whether they will. Players without meters still have a role, which is to value other people's efforts. | ch. 12
idea: Every multiplayer game begins with an agreement | Players accept the same rules, turn up at the same time, concentrate together and try hard so that the game works. The word compete comes from a Latin verb meaning to strive together, and players of online games thank each other with the message GG after a match. | ch. 13
idea: Children protect a game from being played wrongly | In Tomasello's experiment, children of two and three learned a new game, and when a puppet played it by invented rules they objected and tried to correct it. He takes this as early evidence of shared intentionality. | ch. 13
idea: Many players prefer to play together than against each other | Surveys cited in the book find three of four players prefer co-op mode to competitive play. Co-op play avoids the anger, aggression and humiliation that can come with losing. | ch. 13

idea: Players can build a shared world without playing at the same time | In Spore, each player acts alone but the creatures and cities they meet were made by other players and fetched from a database. More than 144 million objects had been shared. | ch. 13
idea: A story told in fragments needs players to assemble it | The Lost Ring spread its story over podcasts, blogs, videos, messages from characters and real objects hidden in 27 countries. Players translated and joined the pieces on a wiki of 943 articles. | ch. 13
idea: Spreading content over languages and places forces players to rely on each other | Clues in The Lost Ring appeared on regional sites in several languages and in physical places around the world. No single player or country could collect them all. | ch. 13
idea: Designers cannot predict how far players will take a game | The creative team of The Lost Ring played its blindfolded labyrinth race slowly and badly in testing. Players then trained every weekend in many cities, cut average times from 3 minutes 30 seconds to 59 seconds, and held their own world championships. | ch. 13
idea: Roles built on personal strengths give every newcomer a task | The Lost Ring offered six strengths from positive psychology, each with a role in the sport: engineers, runners, coaches, captains, referees and storytellers. A twelve-question test placed each player. | ch. 13
idea: God games exercise three habits of thought | Games such as Civilization and Spore ask the player to take a long view, to think about a world as linked parts, and to try small experiments before committing. McGonigal links these to the habits needed to manage the planet. | ch. 14
idea: A game's ending can point the player back at the world | In Spore a player who reaches the centre of the galaxy gets a staff of life and a message to look for the third rock from the sun. The game ends with a call to care for real life. | ch. 14
idea: Imagining the worst first can lead to solutions | In World Without Oil, players began with stories of theft, riots and shortages, and about halfway through moved to neighbourhood cooperation and lower use of oil. The game ended in cautious optimism. | ch. 14
idea: Compressed time lets a scenario run long | One real day in World Without Oil stood for one week in the story, so the 32-day game covered 32 weeks. Players were also asked to live parts of their real days as if the shortage were true. | ch. 14

idea: A game played by many can work as a study of a problem | Einstein is quoted as calling games the most elevated form of investigation. idea: A creative task can have set traits | A Superstruct superstructure had to join groups that did not already work together, tackle a problem too large for one group, use the different skills of each part, and be new. idea: A player can be asked to start from real strengths | Superstruct began by asking players to describe themselves in 2019, using real skills and real communities. The instruction was real play, not role play. | ch. 14
