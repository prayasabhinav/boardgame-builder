term: Game (Burgun's definition) | A system of rules in which agents compete by making ambiguous decisions. Burgun offers this as a tight working meaning for designers, and says other uses of the word are not wrong, only wider. | p. 3
term: Interactive system | A space of possibilities set by explicit rules that a person can act inside. Puzzles, contests and games are all kinds of interactive system, as are toys and simulators. | p. 3
term: Toy | An interactive system with no problem to solve and no contest to win. Burgun puts a sandbox, a flight simulator and an unmodified Minecraft in this group. | p. 4
term: Puzzle | An interactive system that adds a problem with a solution. Once the solution is known, the puzzle usually stops holding interest, like a riddle whose answer you have heard. | p. 5
term: Speedrun | An attempt to finish a game with no random parts in the least possible time, often with software that enters the moves for the player. Burgun uses it to show that such a game has one fastest route. | p. 5
term: Contest | A puzzle that several agents race to solve, where only one can reach the solution first. Weightlifting, an arm-wrestling match and a hot-dog eating race are his examples. | p. 5
term: Agent | Any party that tries to win. It can be a person, the game system itself (the viruses in Pandemic), or the same player measured against an earlier score. | p. 6
term: Ambiguous decision | A choice where the player cannot tell for sure which option is correct, even after the result is in. Burgun names this as the feature that separates a game from a contest. | p. 7
term: Guideline | A rule of thumb for playing a game that holds usually but not always, such as keeping away from the Heavy in Team Fortress 2. Burgun says skilled play means knowing when to drop one. | p. 7
term: Possibility space | The full set of situations and moves a rule set allows. Burgun treats exploring it as what playing a game is. | p. 17
term: Abstract (in a game) | A game or part of a game shown with plain shapes or numbers instead of pictures of real things, such as a health bar or coloured squares in place of Mario. | p. 8
term: Thematic | The layer of story, pictures and setting laid over the rules, such as calling a token a soldier. Burgun separates it from the mechanisms, which are the game itself. | p. 8
term: Meaningful decision | A choice whose effects spread through the system and change later play. Burgun contrasts it with a choice that only carries emotional meaning from the theme. | p. 10
term: Endogenously meaningful | Having an effect inside the game's own rules. Throwing a ball out of bounds forward, left or back counts as one possibility because all three mean the same thing in football. | p. 43
term: Mastery | Having worked a game out so fully that no choice is left to explore. Burgun wants it to stay out of reach even for a very long-time player. | p. 11
term: Solved game | A game for which the winning strategy is known, so play has no open choices left. Tic-tac-toe is solved for most adults and unsolved for small children. | p. 12
term: Digital game | Burgun's name for a game played on a computer, kept apart from the wider phrase video game, which also covers puzzles and simulators. | p. 17
term: Exploration | In Burgun's use, the real activity of every game: testing the limits of a possibility space. It is not only a map hidden under fog. | p. 17
term: Design | A plan of an object made before it exists, showing its form, function or mechanics. | p. 24
term: Elegance | Doing a lot with a little. Burgun lists four marks of a sound design: useful, beautiful, easy to learn and efficient, and says elegance covers all four. | p. 24
term: Minimalism | The principle that a work contains only as many strokes, words or rules as it needs. A thick rule book can still be minimal if each rule is needed. | p. 25
term: Core mechanism | The simple central action a game is built around, such as jumping, bidding, deduction, pushing or aiming. Everything else is meant to support it. | p. 25
term: Supporting mechanism | A rule that exists to serve the core mechanism. Burgun asks of Cranium what rolling and moving have to do with answering trivia. | p. 25
term: Nonlinear | Having no single order in which events or ideas come. A game is a web of possibilities, so its rule book and design document keep pointing forward to things not yet explained. | p. 27
term: Discrete space | A space in which every point inside a region means the same to the rules. The hoop in basketball and each square of a chess board are examples. | p. 29
term: Continuous space | A space where exact position matters, so a very small difference changes the outcome, as with where a rocket lands in Quake. Burgun notes every game still ends in some discrete condition. | p. 29
term: Real time | Play in which the exact moment of an action matters. It is the opposite of turn-based play, where only the order of turns matters. | p. 30
term: Turn | A block of game time during which one player acts, so that taking two seconds or two minutes makes no difference to the rules. | p. 30
term: Input resolution | How much information a player can send the game at one moment or turn. A chess move has a low resolution (one piece, one destination); a Quake player can run, turn and fire at once. | p. 31
term: Feedback | What the game does with a player's input: the result the rules return. A flying 100 on screen is only a picture of the actual feedback, the points scored. | p. 32
term: Execution barrier | A physical skill, such as speed or exact button timing, that stands between a player's choice and its result. Burgun says it turns a game toward a contest. | p. 32
term: Randomness | Results that players cannot predict, from dice, shuffled cards or generators. Burgun counts effective unpredictability as enough, since no dice or computer are truly random. | p. 33
term: Windfall | A random lucky event for a player, such as having your number rolled ten times in a row in Catan. | p. 34
term: Pitfall | A random unlucky event for a player. Windfalls and pitfalls cut the link between what a player chose and how the game went. | p. 34
term: Agency | A player's ability to affect the result through their own choices and performance. Burgun says heavy randomness weakens it. | p. 34
term: Score-based game | A game where the goal is to beat a stored score, usually your own earlier one, so it works as a game played by turns across time. | p. 35
term: Difficulty (Burgun's sense) | The size of the obstacle between a player and winning. He applies it to single-player systems, since in multiplayer games the opponent sets the difficulty. | p. 35
term: Lose condition | A rule that ends play in a loss. Burgun says permanent death in a roguelike is only this, not proof that the player has been cheated. | p. 36
term: Balanced difficulty | A state in which, with equal skill and luck, every player has the same chance to win. Burgun calls it an aim that is never fully reached. | p. 37
term: Balance | Putting all available actions on the same level of value for the player. A more damaging move can still be balanced if it costs more or is slower. | p. 38
term: Cooldown | A wait after using a move before it can be used again, or while the user is open to attack. Designers use it to balance a powerful move. | p. 38
term: Dynamic difficulty adjustment | A feature where the game changes its difficulty to suit the player as they go. Burgun says it rewards careless play and punishes skilled play. | p. 39
term: Rubberbanding | A feature that slows the leader and speeds up those behind in a race so the finish stays close. | p. 116
term: Theme (as a layer) | A literal layer of story and picture over the mechanisms. Burgun says it helps players learn what verbs mean, as bombs and arrows do in Zelda. | p. 39
term: Verb | An action a player can take in a game, such as place, jump or bid. Themes help players remember what each verb does. | p. 40
term: Inherent complexity | The complexity written into the rule set itself, such as the number of piece types and special moves. | p. 41
term: Emergent complexity | The patterns and problems that appear only when people play the rules. Go has simpler rules than chess but more emergent complexity. | p. 41
term: Match | One session of play of a game. The game as a rule set only happens when somebody plays a match. | p. 41
term: Meaningful possibilities | Moves that differ in what they mean inside the rules. Go has many more of these than raw moves counted by ignoring their effects. | p. 43
term: Complete information | A state where all players know the rules, but not everything about the current position. | p. 44
term: Fog of war | A rule that hides parts of the map outside your units' view. It turns open information into hidden information. | p. 44
term: Opening | A known start of play, a few first moves treated as settled. Chess has many solved openings, and Go has joseki. | p. 44
term: Joseki | A set sequence of moves in Go that is known to be safe for both sides, usually in the opening stage. | p. 45
term: Asymmetrical forces | A set-up where each side starts with different powers, such as different characters in Street Fighter. Each matchup becomes its own game. | p. 45
term: Symmetrical forces | A set-up where all sides start with the same pieces and powers, as in chess. | p. 45
term: Mirror match | A game between two sides with the same character or set-up. Burgun says a sound design keeps these interesting. | p. 46
term: Tier list | A ranking of characters or units by power, made by players when a game with many asymmetric parts is not balanced. | p. 47
term: False choice | A choice that only looks like one because one option is clearly ahead, as in "do X or die". Burgun says players see through it. | p. 48
term: Saving and loading | A feature that lets a player reload an earlier position. Burgun says it turns every decision into a false one, since any result can be undone. | p. 48
term: Save-and-exit | A way to pause a game and continue later with no ability to reload an earlier position. | p. 48
term: Grinding | Any action a player can repeat with little or no risk to gain a reward, such as fighting low-level monsters again and again for experience points. | p. 48
term: Analysis paralysis | A stall in turn-based play where a player takes a very long time to choose because too many options are open. | p. 50
term: Invisible tutorial | Teaching a game's rules through its design, such as level layout and picture cues, so that no separate lesson is needed. | p. 61
term: Skinner box | A game that rewards repeated clicks with small prizes, in the manner of behaviourist B. F. Skinner's box for rats, to hold players by habit. | p. 58
term: Prisoner's dilemma | A game-theory puzzle in which two people choose to cooperate or betray and the pay-off table shows each outcome. Burgun suggests such tables as a tool for balancing moves. | p. 58
term: Designer board game | A board game that carries the designer's name on the box, from the German-led revival since the 1990s. | p. 53
term: Eurogame | A board game, mostly from Germany, with a light theme such as farming or trade, little direct conflict and little luck. | p. 54
term: Ameritrash | A board game of mostly American origin with heavy theme, many components and many dice. The name began as an insult and was then adopted. | p. 55
term: Abstract game | A game with little or no theme that uses plain shapes on a grid, such as Go, chess or Hive. | p. 54
term: Race game | A game, usually decided by luck, where players move round a track to finish first. Pachisi, Ludo and Sorry! belong here. | p. 64
term: Mancala game | One of R. C. Bell's six ancient groups, also called sowing or count-and-capture games. | p. 64
term: Wager | Money or stakes put on the result of a play. In the Royal Game of Ur, landing on certain squares forced a player to raise their wager. | p. 65
term: Illusion of control | A belief that your actions, such as picking lottery numbers or breathing on dice, change a random result. | p. 66
term: Handicap | A built-in advantage given to the weaker player so a match stays close. Go has used handicaps for thousands of years. | p. 117
term: Metagame | A game wrapped round another game, such as unlockables, high-score boards or customisation. Burgun says it can lower the weight of choices in the main game. | p. 98
term: Z-order | The depth position of a fighter in a side-scrolling brawler; enemies cannot hit you while you stand in a different depth. | p. 99
term: Button mashing | Pressing buttons rapidly with no thought. Burgun says if it works, the game has stopped asking for decisions. | p. 101
term: Combo | A chain of moves where each leads into the next, often playing out by itself once the first hits. | p. 118
term: Health bar | A shown meter of a character's remaining health, where only empty or not empty changes the game. | p. 119
term: Tension and release | The pattern in a platformer jump: unease while the outcome is unknown, relief when you land. | p. 125
term: Roguelike | A game in Rogue's line, with random maps, turns, a single character, score-based play and permanent death. | p. 130
term: Permadeath | A rule where a lost character is gone for ever. In a score-based roguelike it ends one run, not the game. | p. 130
term: Area-control game | A game where players try to hold more territory than their opponents, such as Go or Carcassonne. | p. 136
term: Stalemate | A position in which neither side can make progress. Burgun says simple capture-for-capture area games drift toward it. | p. 136
term: Explosion (in dice) | In Chaos in the Old World, a six on the die that grants an extra free attack. | p. 137
term: Bidding game | A game where players offer resources for an item that several want, and the highest bid wins it. | p. 137
term: Bluff | In bidding, raising the price beyond an item's value to make a rival pay too much. | p. 138
term: Dimension (of a bid) | The number of different things being weighed in a bid. An item with a single point of value gives an empty, one-sided contest. | p. 138
term: Cooperative game | A game where several players work together against the game system, which takes the role of opponent through cards or dice. | p. 142
term: Competitive-cooperative game | A game where either one player wins or all lose, so some teamwork is needed to avoid shared failure. | p. 143
term: Traitor mechanism | A rule where one player may secretly work against the group, as in The Resistance. It gives a reason to hide information. | p. 143
term: Role selection | A mechanism where on a turn you pick a role card that gives an action, and others cannot take it that turn. | p. 143
term: Worker placement | A mechanism where players put tokens on action spaces; some games let several players share a space and some do not. | p. 144
term: Game master | In a tabletop role-playing game, the person who runs the opposing forces, sets scenes and picks the campaign. Burgun calls the job a real-time game designer. | p. 141
term: Party game | A light game whose mechanisms stay simple so they do not get in the way of the social play. | p. 148
term: Collectible card game | A card game whose cards are bought in random packs, so players start with unequal power and the number of cards keeps growing. | p. 148
term: Deck-building game | A game where each player starts with a small deck, buys cards to add to it, and draws from it in later turns. | p. 148
term: Rare card | A card that is more powerful than others and harder to find, used in collectible card games. | p. 148
idea: A game is a fragile kind of system | Burgun says games sit in a narrow strip between puzzles and contests on one side and noise on the other. One rule can break the ambiguity: Tetris lost its tension when later versions added a hold box, five next pieces and a 7-bag that gave pieces in a fixed rotation. | p. xiv
idea: Games sit above puzzles and contests | Burgun maps interactive systems in layers: a puzzle adds a problem, a contest adds competition, and a game adds ambiguous decisions. A jigsaw is a puzzle, a race to finish it is a contest, and chess is a game. | p. 4
idea: A solved game stops being a game | When players know the right move in every position, no ambiguity is left. Tic-tac-toe is a game for children and a solved puzzle for adults, so whether something is a game depends on who plays it. | p. 12
idea: Decisions in a game cannot be proved right | Even a winner cannot be sure their choices were the correct ones. So playing is closer to painting than to calculation. Players follow guidelines they can break when a rare chance appears, as when a quick Scout hops over a Heavy in Team Fortress 2. | p. 7
idea: Theme is not the game | Burgun strips Final Fantasy to numbers and flags to show that a sad death comes from the story, which could sit on a lunchbox as easily. He says the theme is there to support the mechanisms, since the mechanisms are what the game is. | p. 10
idea: A player can be their own opponent | In Tetris the random generator never wins; the opponent is the player who holds the top score. Score games are multiplayer games played at different times, so one person can play against their old self. | p. 35
idea: A system with no goal cannot be hard | Minecraft has dangerous monsters, but nobody said survival is the aim, and surviving can never be finished. Without an achievable goal there is no contest, and so no difficulty. Players who add their own goal become the game designer. | p. 36
idea: Enjoyment is a result, not a part | Burgun says you cannot add enjoyment to a game like an ingredient. It comes from the game working, so he asks designers to talk about interesting decisions and balance, which can be checked, and to describe Puerto Rico by its layered turns and varied strategies. | p. 27
idea: Games can happen without a designer | A job with goals, rivals and hard choices works as a game, and so does a street fight. These natural games are messy because outsiders can interrupt, which is why designed games are cut off from the outside and kept small. | p. 15
idea: Games teach how to learn | Burgun says the main value of a game is that it trains the player to make tactics, doubt their own thinking and commit to a plan. The pleasure comes when people feel they have improved at something. | p. 13
idea: A story is a line, a game is a web | A story has one order of events, but a game offers choices that keep changing as play goes on. A prewritten story is tied to a fixed order, so games that carry one usually use cutscenes, force the player down a corridor, or let play outrank the story. | p. 22
idea: Ask first whether you need a game | Before designing, Burgun suggests asking if your aim is a story, a character, or a place or object. If so, prose, film or painting may do the job, since a game's rules and rivals do not show a father's love or a magic sword. | p. 20
idea: Start from one core mechanism | Burgun says a design begins with one simple action and everything else supports it. Parts that do not serve the core can be cut. Cranium shows the problem: its dice-and-move rule has little to do with trivia. | p. 25
idea: Fewer rules, same depth | Sound design in this book is minimal: only as many rules as it takes to say what matters. Go has about five rules and a possibility space of 2 x 10 to the power 170, while chess has more rules and fewer games. | p. 42
idea: Rule count and play depth are different things | Chess has more inherent complexity than Go, but Go has far more emergent complexity. What counts is how many moves are meaningful, not how many exist, so a rule set can be short and still deep. | p. 41
idea: Large amounts of content can hide a thin game | When a game has 100 items or 250 unit types, players chase synergies and stop looking at the core. Magic: The Gathering would wear thin quickly without thousands of cards. Burgun also says the designer cannot see the flaws in such a pile. | p. 43
idea: Games that need all the space a computer gives | Burgun says people use new technology without restraint at first. Board games, limited by their parts, tend to be more sensible, so he sends digital designers to them for ideas. | p. 26
idea: Put the design in a web, not a list | Because games are nonlinear, a design document written as a line keeps using terms it has not yet explained. Burgun uses a wiki, or sticky notes on a wall, so that pages can link to each other and the links show. | p. 28
idea: The designer is wrong often | By the time a game is done, Burgun says no design part is left that the designer had not got wrong at some point. Long planning and prototypes cut the waste, but cutting a spell a programmer spent hours on will still annoy the team. | p. 29
idea: Input size shapes what the game tests | A chess move is a small input, so the game tests thought. In a real-time strategy game the input is almost unlimited, so speed of hand and managing the interface decide results, and a right plan can lose to faster clicking. | p. 31
idea: Restrictions are what design is | Burgun says imposing limits on players is not a fault; it is the task. He questions whether looking up and down in Quake added much, since most play happens on one axis, and says input is as open to cutting as features are. | p. 31
idea: Skill at execution is not a decision | Failing to hit a note in Guitar Hero is no more a decision than tripping over a rug. If one option is clearly ahead, the question was settled before play. Execution tends to pull a game toward a contest, as bowling shows when a rival always strikes. | p. 32
idea: Harder inputs do not balance a powerful move | Fighting games make a powerful move hard to type in, but players master it in time and the balance breaks. New players are also kept out. Burgun says to balance by cost or delay instead. | p. 33
idea: Single-player games need randomness | Without it, a game with execution becomes a contest (Guitar Hero) and one without becomes a memory puzzle (Castlevania). Another human mind creates uncertainty in multiplayer games, but alone the player needs random information to keep ambiguity. | p. 33
idea: Use no more randomness than you need | In a multiplayer game other people supply most of the variety, so extra dice add little. In Catan one player can have their number rolled ten times in a row, and Burgun reports the remark that someone could roll the dice for you and tell you how it went. | p. 34
idea: Randomness cuts the link between choice and result | When luck drives the outcome, players get mixed signals about whether they played well. A loss may not feel like a real loss. Candy Land has no decisions at all, so Burgun counts it as a luck contest. | p. 34
idea: Randomness as a way to level skill | Mario Kart 64 gives powerful random items so that weaker players keep up. This lowers the weight of skill and of decisions, which are the features that make a game a game. | p. 37
idea: Balance is a matter of cost | A move of 50 damage and a move of 10 damage can be balanced if the larger one is slow or costly. A StarCraft Battlecruiser costs more minerals and tech-tree time than a Wraith, and still loses to a crowd of Wraiths in the air. | p. 38
idea: Balance by starting small and testing | Begin with the least content that carries the play, add only as needed, leave time for testing after the game seems complete, and change only a little at a time. Beta reports are not all trustworthy, since people often mistake their own lack of skill for imbalance. | p. 39
idea: Do not patch balance with automatic fixes | Burgun lists dynamic difficulty, scaling of enemies to the player and rubberbanding as bandages. They reward weaker play and punish skill, and he says the game then feels lifeless. | p. 39
idea: Theme helps when a game gets complicated | Go needs no theme, but Zelda's bombs and arrows would be hard to remember as abstract items X and Y. A theme works as a memory aid for verbs. When a player knows the game well it can be removed, as Street Fighter showed when its pictures failed and plain boxes appeared. | p. 41
idea: Theme can also make a game easier to sell | Burgun admits that abstract games are hard to sell, and a theme makes a game seem welcoming. The task is to add one without hurting the play. | p. 41
idea: Hex grids treat all neighbours the same | On a square grid a diagonal step goes further than a side step, so designers add costs as a patch. Hexes make every tile equal in distance from its neighbours, which is why war games use them; the cost is that art and four-way controls fit them less easily. | p. 108
idea: Information type changes solvability | Chess shows everything and can be solved in principle. A game with hidden information has no exact solution but an optimal strategy, such as playing at random in rock-paper-scissors. Adding a mini-map to a scrolling game can silently return it to open information. | p. 44
idea: Games lean on human limits | Go may be as easy to an alien mind as tic-tac-toe is to an adult. A game has to sit just beyond human mastery, and a game that fits a smarter mind would look like noise to us. | p. 45
idea: Each pair in an asymmetric game is a new game to balance | Three characters make six matchups including mirrors, ten make 55 and thirty make 465. Burgun says this growth is why fighting games drift out of balance, and he asks designers to keep the number of sides low, under six. | p. 47
idea: Test an asymmetric game as symmetric first | Burgun asks for a long period of play with one character, since a game that is dull as a mirror match has a thin core. Add differences late, one at a time, and never fix the number of characters in advance. | p. 119
idea: Too many options become false choices | Street Fighter characters have dozens of moves but only a few real choices such as attack high, attack low, block, throw, jump or shoot. Ten ways to attack high mean that some are simply less useful versions of others. | p. 50
idea: Do not waste the players' time | Waiting for loading screens, long walks down halls and repeated chores take time and give nothing back. Burgun says a town used only for buying items could be a menu, and board games are kinder to time because parts limit such padding. | p. 51
idea: Question the parts every game of the type has | Burgun drops equipment, levels and rising stats in his own game Auro, since a hero that grows stronger makes the designer's job harder and the game easier. Characters keep fixed numbers and learn new abilities, such as a counter that reflects damage. | p. 52
idea: Look outside your own kind of game | Burgun says digital designers copy each other because they know only digital games. He sends them to designer board games, abstracts, sports, children's games, card games and pen-and-paper play, since new ideas come from recombining what you have seen. | p. 53
idea: Rule changes have side effects | In American football, hard-shell helmets may have made players move faster and more recklessly. Any new rule can have effects the designer did not plan, so sports histories are full of lessons. | p. 56
idea: Games are machines for behaviour | Because psychology studies behaviour, it informs design. Burgun contrasts games that train the mind with compulsion games like Farmville, which reward every click and leave players empty because the game only uses them. | p. 58
idea: A pay-off table can help balancing | List the options for two players in a grid and give each outcome a value. His example, "attack or dodge", shows that the dodger who then counterattacks gains most, and values can be tuned to rank the moves. | p. 59
idea: A hard-to-organise screen points to a design problem | If the pieces of information cannot be arranged clearly, there may be too many or they may not relate closely. Burgun says studying icons and layout shows such design faults, and a clear icon such as a bomb carries a lot of rules. | p. 60
idea: Teach the rules through the play | Manuals are voluntary and deep but hard to take in; forced tutorials annoy. Burgun's example is the first level of Super Mario Brothers, which teaches by placing things in sequence, and he asks games to teach what they can in this hands-on way. | p. 61
idea: Games evolve by reinvention | Almost every old game has an earlier game behind it. Pachisi led to Ludo and then Sorry!, and a game found with no predecessor would only mean the predecessor is not yet found. | p. 64
idea: Pure luck games are contests | Nyout and the Royal Game of Ur are dice races with almost no choices. Burgun counts them as contests, though a wager adds one outside decision about how much to bet. | p. 65
idea: Gambling thrives without decisions | Roulette and lotteries offer few or no choices and still attract adults, partly through the illusion of control. Burgun adds that poker is a game and that any game can become gambling if money rides on it. | p. 66
idea: Old games were shaped by many hands | Few ancient games had a single designer. They changed over centuries, so one can watch generations looking for balance, which Burgun calls inspiring for a designer. | p. 68
idea: Dual goals in hurling add a decision | Hurling has a low goal worth three points and a higher set of uprights worth one but easier to hit. This gives a player a real choice between safe and ambitious scoring. | p. 70
idea: A playing card deck carries history, not only design | Aces are said to rank high because playing them so was a small act of rebellion by poorer people in the French Revolution. Cultural and economic reasons shaped the deck as well as game design. | p. 71
idea: War games started as research | A Prussian general used small pieces, a map and dice to study battles, and H. G. Wells's Little Wars (1913) was the first made mainly for enjoyment. Burgun says war is itself a game, since it is a contest of ambiguous decisions, so a war game is both a simulation and a game. | p. 71
idea: Computers allow new decisions | Spacewar! had two ships, a planet with gravity and a warp that might blow you up. Burgun says it forced players to make choices that no earlier game had asked for, which he calls the promise of digital games. | p. 75
idea: New features are not new games | Burgun says Doom, Quake and Call of Duty are variants of the dodging-and-shooting game of 1961. Judged by the decisions a player makes, there may be only a dozen real games with thousands of variations. | p. 75
idea: More technology does not make a more interesting game | Burgun says Super Smash Brothers could have been made on the earlier NES and that later hardware mostly added colour and sound. He asks designers for ideas, not new machines. | p. 77
idea: Fantasy simulation is a different aim | Burgun separates a game from a fantasy simulator. A fantasy does not need to be balanced or have ambiguous decisions, so Final Fantasy's repetitive grinding was excused as simulating a world. | p. 83
idea: Some people feel ashamed of games | Burgun calls this game shame. It explains why games imitate film, chase graphics and talk of immersion, and why a Madden football game copies television football instead of being a game. | p. 84
idea: A game with no random content turns into a puzzle | The second time through Castlevania or Mega Man, players have memorised parts. Burgun compares it to giving children the same maths test ten times: after a few tries they recall answers and no longer do sums. | p. 96
idea: More content is not more design | Game boxes brag of 200 spells or 70 guns, and Burgun says this craves bulk when the question is how few mechanisms can carry the idea. Heavy content makes choices false and balance impossible. | p. 97
idea: Metagames lower the weight of the main game | Extra systems such as unlockables multiply the interactions, make dominant strategies likelier and blur differences between elements. In Team Fortress 2, tying weapons to achievements made players farm achievements instead of playing normally. | p. 98
idea: Make the screen small enough to need no scrolling | Burgun asks of real-time strategy games why the map has to be so big. A mini-map ends up being the real game, so he suggests tiny units on one screen or tying scrolling to play, since managing a camera has no link to the decisions. | p. 104
idea: Animations that make players wait have no meaning in turns | In real time, a move's animation is part of its balance. In a turn-based game it only makes the player sit. Burgun's sum is that a one-second animation on every other command wastes twenty minutes in an hour. | p. 107
idea: Fewer, more meaningful units | Advance Wars has about ten unit types and five or six terrains, and every unit has its own role. Burgun prefers this to Heroes of Might and Magic, with seven resources and stacks of 117 units, whose strength is hard to read. | p. 107
idea: Leveling up makes a game easier | As characters grow stronger the game gets easier, so designers raise difficulty to match, and every player levels at a different rate. Burgun says balance then becomes close to impossible and the player is left to guess how long to grind. | p. 111
idea: A discrete map in a continuous costume | The overmap of a Final Fantasy game is really a list of places and a place to grind. Burgun writes it as a menu to show that the walking between them is a chore. | p. 111
idea: Players differ in skill, and fixes cost something | Items with heavy randomness and rubberbanding hide skill gaps in racers, but they take away the reward for skill. Burgun says handicaps are a plain, honest tool that Go has used for thousands of years. | p. 117
idea: Make short tracks and routes with trade-offs | A shortcut that is always the quickest is no real choice. Burgun suggests routes where one is longer but gives an item and one is short but harms your vehicle, and small tracks so racers meet more often. | p. 114
idea: Health bars can be tied to the core mechanism | In Super Smash Brothers a player gains damage instead of losing health, and more damage means being knocked further. Health then feeds the central idea of position. | p. 120
idea: Protect the jump | A platformer jump works through tension and release. Mid-air steering, a cape or a hover pack shrinks the tension, so Burgun says a designer who boasts of giving the player complete control misreads how limits create the game. | p. 125
idea: Skill is shown by choosing, not by typing | A move that needs a half circle twice and then three punches tests memory of inputs. Burgun says the pride lies in making wise choices during play. | p. 120
idea: Third-person views cost information | A big model of the character's back blocks the enemy that may stand behind it. Burgun says to leave large solid things out of the player's view unless hiding information is a mechanism. | p. 123
idea: Team scores can pull against team play | In Team Fortress 2 players see personal scores, which compete with the team goal. Burgun says soccer players seeing live personal rankings would play for their numbers. Distinct roles with large gaps make teammates need each other. | p. 122
idea: 3D adds work unrelated to the game | Without depth perception, a 3D platformer player has to judge distance and angles that have nothing to do with platforming. Burgun asks designers to keep gameplay 2D when the screen and controller are 2D. | p. 128
idea: Monopoly was changed into a luck game | Elizabeth Magie's Landlord's Game made a point about land. When Parker Brothers bought it, the auction on each landing was removed, taking out its core mechanism. What remains has almost no choices and long play with early elimination. | p. 134
idea: New games ask more of the learner | A new, interesting board game means reading the rules, trying a few turns and reading again. Burgun says rule books are non-linear and often written badly, so the more original a game, the more patience it needs up front. | p. 135
idea: Area games stall when each side trades one space for one | Chaos in the Old World uses dice and a six gives an explosion. Burgun calls that the easy way out and suggests feints, where you hint at taking tile A and take B, with costs for switching so it does not become rock-paper-scissors. | p. 137
idea: Long-term plans need predictable futures | Heavy randomness makes planning beyond the next turn impossible. Burgun cites Robo Rally, where pieces move at fixed rates that players alter only a little, and says fewer random outcomes (three tile types, not ten) also helps. | p. 137
idea: A bid needs many sides to be interesting | If we bid for one victory point that both want equally, the question has no dimension. With extra items, such as a matching half of a set worth five points, each person's wishes differ and bids tell the others something. | p. 138
idea: Cap bidding to avoid runaway prices | Two players who both badly want an item can push its price to a level that empties the money supply and unbalances the game. Burgun suggests only three or four bid levels, with a rule change when someone goes higher. | p. 138
idea: Simulation comes second to play | Real conflicts are not balanced, and real vehicles break down. Burgun says to begin with an abstract design and fit a theme on afterwards. Even in war games, area-control games and abstracts offer ways to express movement and combat. | p. 140
idea: Cooperative games can take mistakes away | When one expert tells everyone what to do, the others lose the chance to explore and err. Burgun says two repairs exist, an all-win-or-all-lose rule or a traitor, though both mean the game is no longer purely cooperative. | p. 143
idea: Hidden information needs a reason | Pandemic bans showing your hand, but players need to know it, so they keep asking. Burgun says that is a silly rule, and a possible traitor gives a real in-game reason to keep cards secret. | p. 143
idea: Roles that change during play | In role-selection games the roles are fixed. Burgun suggests letting a role's strength change over the game, so that making a role stronger helps everyone and opens new decisions. | p. 144
idea: Be wary of convenient numbers | A card game that happens to need exactly 52 cards in four suits may be copying the standard deck. Burgun says to try a half deck, two decks, no face cards, or 17 new cards, in the way you would doubt a unit with exactly 1,000 horsepower. | p. 145
idea: Abstract games are hard to make and tend toward solution | It is harder to make something simple and interesting. Arimaa was made because computers had partly solved chess. Burgun says random elements and layers of complexity push a solution further away but do not remove it. | p. 146
idea: A theme is for clarity | Burgun says an abstract game can be as beautiful as a themed one. Add a theme when the game is so complex that actions and pieces need names, not for looks, and not so heavily that it seems silly. | p. 147
idea: Simple games can leave little room for style | A very small rule set gives fewer odd, lateral moves that confuse rivals or show a player's manner. More complexity gives more space for moves that are not obviously the top choice but also not mistakes. | p. 147
idea: Party games need rules that stay out of the way | Burgun says a party game can still be a real game if its mechanisms are so light that they do not interrupt the talk. He credits Apples to Apples and Saboteur for using the talk itself. | p. 148
idea: Collectible cards break balance | Players arrive with different cards, rares are stronger, packs are random and the card pool never stops growing. Burgun says no amount of careful rules cancels these factors. | p. 148
idea: Deck-building has a built-in balancing act | Victory cards score points but do nothing else, so buying them dilutes your deck. This creates a steady choice between building power and cashing in, though Burgun says luck and add-on content weigh heavily. | p. 149
idea: A traitor stops one player from taking over | If anyone might be a traitor, a skilled player cannot safely lead everyone. Burgun notes the traitor element tends to swamp other mechanisms and wants a design where the traitor role keeps shifting. | p. 150
idea: Any activity becomes a game with goals and rules | You can turn Minecraft, Legos or a flight simulator into a game by adding your own goals. Burgun says this adding is the act of game design, so anyone who invents a house rule has designed. | p. 131
