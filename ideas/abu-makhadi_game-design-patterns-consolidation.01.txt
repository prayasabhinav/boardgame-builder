term: Design pattern | In the sense taken from the architect Christopher Alexander, a description of a design problem followed by a description of its solution. The structure is plain: if problem A exists, solution B can be applied to solve it. | p. 158
term: Pattern language | A set of patterns joined in a network, where each pattern points to others that complement it. Alexander held that this lets a user see the language as a whole. | p. 160
term: Fragmentation of pattern collections | The state of game design patterns where each collection uses its own template and conventions, so patterns overlap or contradict across collections and cannot be used as one language. | p. 159
term: Pattern consolidation | A process in which different pattern collections are joined into a single one with a consistent template, naming and structure. The paper says it is hard because templates differ, patterns can overlap or contradict, and a joined collection might be too large to move around in. | p. 160
term: Subtractive pattern | Barney's name for a pattern that mitigates or weakens the effect of another pattern. The paper takes it as the same idea as the conflicting patterns of Björk and Holopainen. | p. 169
term: Related patterns field | A field in a pattern's description that lists other patterns it contradicts or complements. The paper recommends it for consolidated collections, following the network structure of Alexander. | p. 171
term: Keywords | Short labels on a pattern for the parts of a game it relates to, used to find it. The paper treats the domains of The 400 Project as the same thing as keywords. | p. 171
term: Formal Abstract Design Tools | Doug Church's short descriptions of design tools, each with a name and a brief description, kept as a list. They describe how elements of a game interact, not what elements a game has. | p. 164
term: The 400 Project | Hal Barwood and Noah Falstein's effort to collect 400 game design rules, which reached 112 in five years. Each rule has an identifier, title, short description, a domain and a contributor. | p. 164
term: Lens | Jesse Schell's form of a design tool: a numbered title, a description and a set of questions the designer asks about their game from one point of view. His book has 113 lenses. | p. 168
term: Consolidated pattern template | The paper's proposed set of required fields: name, keywords, description, related patterns and reference. Any other fields, such as an image, a confidence level or an example, are optional, so a pattern from any of the six collections can be fitted to it without losing information. | p. 172
term: List and network collections | A list is a plain set of patterns. A network is a set in which patterns have relationships to each other, such as parent, child or related. | p. 163
idea: Three findings | The paper reports that collections with different templates can still extend each other, that keywords are a reliable way to find patterns, and that a related patterns field can hold both conflicting and complementing patterns. | p. 155
idea: Patterns are often called rules or tools | Because Kreimeier brought patterns to game designers in 2002, when only design rules and tools existed, patterns are often reworded as tools or rules. The paper says this confuses what a game design pattern is. | p. 158
idea: A missing field adds to the fragmentation | Kreimeier's template left out the related patterns field that his own examples had. The paper says this omission is one reason collections do not refer to each other or avoid overlap, and that inconsistent templates are another. | p. 160
idea: Too many patterns overwhelm new designers | Barney's criticism, cited by the paper, is that novice designers who learn patterns are given too many tools, and find them hard to find, organise and apply. | p. 159
idea: Keywords are the most suitable way to find patterns | Of the approaches in the six works, the paper favours keywords. They are the one approach used by both collections that have many patterns and take contributions from outside. | p. 171
idea: Search software and mnemonic names fall short | Kreimeier names search software without saying what it would search on, and Björk and Holopainen's mnemonic names do not hold up with hundreds of patterns. Schell's deck of cards works only for a collection that does not grow. | p. 171
idea: Lenses avoid contradiction by asking questions | Schell gives questions instead of solutions, so his lenses do not contradict each other and the final decision stays with the designer. The cost is that a lens no longer pairs a problem with a solution. | p. 168
idea: Conflicts are one kind of relation | Collections that are networks need fields for relations between patterns, and the paper treats a contradiction as one kind of relation. Barney lists four kinds: parent, child, additive and subtractive. | p. 169
idea: Optional fields make joining easier | Barney's template, the widest of the six, lets a contributor leave out fields such as example games or an image. The paper takes this as a reason for optional fields in a consolidated template, with a few fields required. | p. 171
idea: A contributor field is required | The collections that accept outside contributions record who wrote or sourced each pattern, as a contributor, references or authors field. The paper makes a reference field required in its template for that reason. | p. 172
idea: Predictable Consequence and Surprise pull against each other | In the paper's worked example, Predictable Consequence, where the game world reacts the same way under the same conditions, is complemented by Intention and Rules. Surprise contradicts it, and Indirect Control can break it. | p. 173
idea: The selection of works is intuitive | The six works compared were chosen by intuition, which the paper names as an obvious limit of its method. | p. 162
idea: Validation and platform stay open | The paper leaves open how patterns in a consolidated collection would be validated and what platform would hold it. | p. 173
idea: A joined collection is for students | The author says a consolidated collection would need to be more compact than the collections added together to be offered to students. The open question is whether novices find a game design theory easier to learn as a pattern language. | p. 174
