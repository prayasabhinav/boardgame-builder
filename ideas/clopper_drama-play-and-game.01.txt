term: Theatrum | In medieval writing, the Latin word for a place of spectacle that was linked with obscene and immodest behaviour. It did not mean a building where dramas were staged, as "theatre" does now. | p. 3
term: Ludus (plural ludi) | The Latin word for play. It covered games, sports, music-making and acted scripts, so the word alone does not say which of these a record means. | p. 12
term: Drama (as the author uses it) | An acted script that holds, or once held, a whole narrative, so that it is both a written text and a performance. The author uses it to tell a drama apart from a social event. | p. 11
term: Dramatic mode | In medieval rhetoric, one of three ways of telling a story: the poet never speaks in his own voice and only the characters speak. It describes how a text is arranged, not whether anyone acts it out. | p. 6
term: Rhetoric of abuse | A way of speaking in which old complaints against the Roman theatrum were aimed at activities that had nothing to do with a stage. | p. 5
term: Stage play | A phrase in English parish and city records that looks like acted drama but often meant king games and other folk activities, sometimes mocking or parodying. | p. 15
term: Interlude | In some town records, a musical performance with something to look at, such as a pageant. The word did not always mean a short acted piece. | p. 14
term: Player | Someone who takes part in any ludus: an athlete, a musician, or a dice or card player, as well as an actor. | p. 12
idea: Old words mislead modern readers | The book argues that historians have read medieval words like "drama" and "theatre" in their modern sense and so turned many activities into theatre that were not. For example, the Dunmow Corpus Christi "play" turns out, from its receipts, to be a series of shooting, running and leaping contests. | p. 12
idea: Play is a part of game | The author follows a scholar's point that the word "play" is historically a subset of the word "game", not the other way round. Ludus and "play" both covered games and sports, so a record of "a play of Saint Andrew" can describe a parish sports day. | p. 12
idea: A "play" can be a musical event | Payments for "plays" at Durham, Shrewsbury and London may have been for musicians, not actors. If so, a "play book" could be a book of music and a "book bearer" someone who held it for the minstrels. | p. 14
idea: A script need not be acted to be drama | The author says a text can be a drama in form without being performed, and an enactment can exist without a script. Royal entries with pageants and speeches count as enacted scripts under his usage. | p. 12
idea: Medieval drama grew from the lay side | Clerics tried to suppress the ludi of ordinary people, and in some places that pressure led towards a vernacular drama run by civic bodies and guilds, whose members wanted entertainment that also showed care for their souls. | p. 3
term: Joculator | A medieval entertainer who sang the deeds of princes and the lives of saints. One bishop's sorting of performers allowed this kind and condemned entertainers who used lewd movement, masks or wanton songs. | p. 31
term: Pantomime (in Augustine) | An ancient stage performer who danced to a song sung by others, with music. Augustine counted him with the mime and the actor as the three kinds of performer in the theatrum. | p. 35
term: Spectacula | Latin for public shows. The early church wrote against three kinds: chariot races in the circus, gladiator fights in the amphitheatre, and mimes and actors in the theatrum. | p. 35
term: Ludi theatrales | A label Pope Innocent III gave to disorderly games held inside churches and monasteries at certain times of year. The word "theatrical" here meant improper, not staged. | p. 21
term: Ludi inhonesti | Latin for dishonourable games: the rowdy, immoral pastimes that church writers tried to stop, as against religious plays. | p. 21
term: Miracula | In English church records and preaching manuals, a word for rowdy activities like Innocent's ludi theatrales. It comes from an old Latin sense of "monstrosity" and did not mean miracle plays. | p. 21
term: Parish ale | A feast-day gathering held by a parish to raise money for its church, with drinking, games, wrestling matches and tilts. It was the most common kind of ludus in late medieval England and is often mistaken for a saint play. | p. 22
term: Mimesis | The Greek idea of imitation, central to Aristotle on tragedy. Medieval readers working from Arabic commentary replaced it with "likening", a kind of picture that stirs feeling towards virtue. | p. 10
term: Decorum | Fitting conduct, in the Roman sense that John of Salisbury used: grave, restrained, and not drawing attention to oneself. | p. 25
idea: Ban on behaviour, not on a form | The author argues the church's long campaign against the theatrum was aimed at lewd gesture and rowdy games, not at scripted biblical or moral plays. Medieval readers knew the theatrum by its gestures and not by its genre. | p. 34
idea: One word, many activities | Records call a performer a histrio, minstrel, joculator, mimus or player without a fixed sense. In Selby Abbey's accounts, more than 60 percent of the noble companies whose size is given had three or fewer players, too few for the interludes scholars assumed they performed. | p. 18
idea: Cast size as evidence | No surviving interlude that David Bevington lists calls for fewer than four players. So a troupe of three is more likely to have given music and pageant than a dialogue play. | p. 18
idea: No ladder from one form to the next | Older histories ran liturgical drama, then biblical cycles, then moralities, then commercial theatre, each replacing the last. The author says this copied Darwinian thinking and that the forms overlapped and went on side by side. | p. 19
idea: Games used to raise money | Stage plays in churchyards were often king games or other rowdy entertainments run to raise money for the parish. In 1529 London gave one parish a licence for a stage play from summer to Michaelmas and promised none to any other parish in that time. | p. 17
idea: Moderation is judged by where attention goes | John of Salisbury allows music, sport and entertainment in moderation but cannot name the line. His test is whether the activity draws attention to itself for its own sake; if it does, it is excessive. | p. 25
idea: Watching suffering | Augustine was troubled that stage tragedy works by drawing intense feeling from the watcher while asking nothing of him. The watcher does nothing to relieve the pain he sees and takes pleasure in it. | p. 34
idea: Opposition can change a game, not end it | Clerical attempts to stop unholy festivities were often resisted and things went on as before. In some towns the laity swapped the festivities for biblical and moral dramas, which both sides could accept. | p. 22
idea: The same text is read differently over time | Plays were revised, and even unrevised texts meant different things once the Reformation began. The author warns against treating a play as fixed at the moment it was written. | p. 23
term: Agon | A Greek word for contest. Isidore of Seville treats contest, with chance, as the central theme of war and of the games of the circus, theatre and amphitheatre. | p. 39
term: Fortuna | Chance or luck. In Isidore's account of games, chance appears in war and in the arena, and his list of games ends with games of chance. | p. 39
term: Games of chance | In church law, games the clergy and laity were told to avoid. The term could cover anything from gambling to martial contests. | p. 38
term: Thymelicus (plural thymelici) | In Isidore's account, a musician who played organs, lyres and cytharas, named after the platform, the thymele, where he once stood. A church canon told clergy to leave a feast before these performers came in. | p. 40
term: Scena | In Isidore's account, a part of the ancient theatre where performers played. Later medieval readers took it to be a tent where mimes changed costume before coming out to perform. | p. 43
term: Officia scenica | Isidore's name for the six kinds of stage performer: tragedians, comedians, thymelici, histriones, mimes and dancers. | p. 40
term: Larvae | Latin for distorted or monstrous masks. Church rules told clerics not to wear them, and Innocent III objected to "masks of monsters" in churches. | p. 38
term: Representatio | A Latin word for a liturgical re-enactment, such as the visit to the tomb at Easter or Herod's rage at Christmas. Writers used it for devotional pieces in church and kept it apart from words for theatrical show. | p. 52
term: Hypocrita | The Latin word for actor or mime. It carried the charge that the actor puts on a mask and shows emotion that is not his own. | p. 52
term: Theatrical metaphor | Calling something "theatrical" to condemn it. Medieval writers used it for any activity they judged indecorous, worldly or outside the church's own ritual, whether or not any acting was involved. | p. 50
term: Liturgical parody | A mocking copy of church ritual, usually by choirboys or lower clergy in the days after Christmas. An example is the Padua Herod, who bursts into the choir in an untidy tunic and strikes people with an inflated bladder. | p. 45
idea: Contest and chance as the root of games | Isidore of Seville orders his account of play from war to gymnastics, the circus, the theatre, the amphitheatre and games of chance. The author reads this as treating contest and fortune as the two themes running through all of it. | p. 39
idea: A ban kept a group out by rule | Early church canons barred actors from baptism unless they gave up the stage, let converts attend the games except on Sundays and festivals, and told clergy to leave a wedding feast before the entertainers came in. So watchers were tolerated and performers were shut out. | p. 37
idea: Where a word points is not fixed | Over time "theatrum" came to mean any place a show could be seen, even a marketplace. Bede's comment on Acts, quoting Isidore on the semicircular building, still treated a marketplace and a theatre as equivalent. | p. 42
idea: Costume against ordinary dress | The author notes that people in church re-enactments usually wore liturgical garments, not costumes. When Gerhoh complained of masks and men dressed as women, the sources suggest he meant disguise for festive licence, not a scripted part. | p. 45
idea: The same scene allowed or banned by its effect | A standard legal comment on Innocent III's decree said Nativity and Herod scenes were allowed because they lead people to devotion, while scenes that lead to lewdness on the feasts of Saint Stephen, Saint John and the Holy Innocents were forbidden. The test was what the scene did to its participants, not its subject. | p. 55
idea: Pretending to be what you represent | Gerhoh of Reichersberg charged that clergy who played Antichrist became what they played. He gave as proof an actor who played a dead man and was found dead, and an Antichrist who was buried and found dead seven days later. | p. 44
idea: Reform shifts shared meals | Gerhoh complained that his cathedral canons no longer ate or slept together except on rare feasts, when they staged Herod or similar shows. So the canons came together only for entertainment. | p. 46
idea: A rule of silence and its workaround | Gerald of Wales described monks who kept their rule of silence at table but used fingers, hands, arms and whistles to ask for special foods. He felt he was seated at a stage play. | p. 52
idea: Sound against sense | Aelred of Rievaulx objected that singers shook and rolled their eyes to ornate music while the crowd stood and gawked. He said the crowd had come to look, not to pray, and that sound was being enjoyed without attention to the words. | p. 53
idea: Watching against taking part | Aelred and Augustine both separate going to a place to take part in a rite from going to gaze. A hall of onlookers that jeers and snickers counts as a theatre to them, whatever the building is. | p. 54
idea: The world turned upside down as play | Bernard of Clairvaux compares monks to acrobats who stand on their hands, then separates the monks' game from the theatre's. His reason is that monks play at being mocked and humbled for the sake of those who watch, with David's dance before the ark as the model. | p. 56
idea: Restraint against imitation in ritual | Honorius of Autun compares the priest at mass to a stage tragedian but uses verbs of signifying, not copying: the silence of the prayer shows Christ as a lamb led without a voice. The author reads this as restrained and symbolic, with no acting. | p. 52
term: Somergame (summer game) | A lay game held in summer, often in a churchyard or beside it. One 1469 Yorkshire example chose a village king and queen and led them with a minstrel in procession to a "somer house" by the church. | p. 68
term: Chorea (plural choreae) | A song-dance, a dance performed to singing. Church rules listed it with wrestling among the games banned from churchyards on feast days. | p. 68
term: Lord of Misrule | A Christmas ruler of revels chosen in colleges. Cambridge used the figure to channel rowdiness, and in 1548 the university ordered that no one be lord of games at Christmas under any name. | p. 61
term: Boy bishop | A choirboy who held the bishop's role on the feast of Saint Nicholas, with ceremonies he led. Some monasteries and cathedrals called these ceremonies miracula because a boy was acting as a bishop he was not. | p. 77
term: Clerkes play | A play of clerks: a revel by clerics in minor orders and choirboys, with masks, torturers and ragged costumes, in the Christmas season and from Easter to Whitsuntide. | p. 71
term: Cum decorem | The opening words of Pope Innocent III's decree against ludi theatrales in churches. Later bishops copied or quoted it in their own statutes. | p. 64
term: Irrisiones | Latin for derision or mockery. Church writers used it for games that jested with holy things, such as a summer game that mocked the Crucifixion. | p. 74
term: Antiludic tradition | The long run of church rules from the thirteenth to the seventeenth century against rowdy games and dishonourable play. The author separates it from the later Puritan attack on the stage. | p. 63
idea: Colleges swapped rowdy revels for set plays | Cambridge colleges staged Terence at Christmas partly to contain the unruly behaviour of young men, giving the season an educational purpose. Even so, accounts show glass replaced after performances, and in 1578-79 Trinity College hung nets before its windows. | p. 60
idea: A control can need controlling | Lords of Misrule began as a way to steer energy away from choirboys' antics in the choir. They grew unruly in turn, which led to the 1548 university ban on any Christmas lord of games. | p. 61
idea: A stage built for the show grows dearer | Oxford paid 18d. in 1538-39 for three days' work on a stage called a proscenium. By 1559-60 the cost had risen from 11s. 6d. to nearly 50s. with more labourers, and the structure was now called a theatrum. | p. 62
idea: Rules attach to who plays and where | Church rules gave clergy and laity different rules about play. Clerics were told not to watch mimes or play dice, and the rules for the laity began with place: wrestling and dance-songs were banned in the churchyard while matins, mass and vespers were sung. | p. 65
idea: A rule for one group is extended to another | Bishop Quinel in 1287 applied the language of Innocent's decree on clerics' ludi to lay wrestling and dancing in churchyards. Both were treated as marketplace amusements pushed into a sacred place. | p. 66
idea: The place decides what the game is | Bishop Lacy in 1451 wrote that laughter, mocking and rough sports belong in places made for viewing, such as the marketplace or theatrum, and not in sacred ground. The same activity was judged by where it took place. | p. 67
idea: A structure can be the problem | Bishop Grandisson complained in 1339 about a canopy built on posts in sacred ground because it blocked the procession around the church. The author reads this as an objection to the structure and the lay festivity under it, not to a stage play. | p. 67
idea: Replace by imitating, not duplicating | The author says the clergy did not want laity to become clerics. They wanted lay practice that copied clerical practice in part, such as a feast-day procession in place of a very large feast. | p. 69
idea: A single word held up a whole theory | A scholar's claim that "miracula" meant saint play rested at last on one record from Dunstable and on one treatise. The author's own count finds seventeen references to ludic miracula up to the early fifteenth century, none before 1400 that clearly names a religious drama. | p. 70
idea: Roles carry unequal comfort | In a sermon story, players in a summer game took parts as Christ, Peter, Andrew, tormentors and devils. The player who had been Christ said that next time he would be a tormentor or devil, because those parts ate and drank while the others suffered. | p. 73
idea: Audience rewards shape the play | In the summer game the audience gave food and drink to the tormentors, and whoever tormented Christ most was counted as the most skilful player. The author says this unscripted invention is part of why bishops disliked such games. | p. 74
idea: A script limits licence | The author contrasts boy bishop ceremonies, which stayed in check while the boys kept to the script, with unscripted summer games, which wandered into mockery of the Crucifixion. He compares feasts of fools that stray past their script in the same way. | p. 74
idea: An inappropriate person in a role | Some houses called boy bishop ceremonies miracula because a choirboy took the part of a bishop, like the false prophets in Jeremiah who copied true prophets. The practice was allowed, but a Lichfield statute told the subchanter to keep it fitting and honourable. | p. 77
idea: A word's older sense can survive | Miraculum in older Latin meant a wonder that caused horror or was monstrous, and was linked with lies and distorted masks. The author argues medieval clerics who used "miracula" for rowdy clerical revels were using that older sense, not the later meaning of a divine miracle. | p. 76
idea: Pretence plus startling effects | Commentators on Jeremiah described the false prophets as people who pretend to be one thing while being another, win a crowd with horror and loud gesture, and mock the truth. The author reads this as the root of the later view of theatre as show and hypocrisy. | p. 78
term: Tretise of Miraclis Pleyinge | A Middle English tract from around 1380 to 1414 against "miracle playing". Scholars have read it as an attack on religious drama; the author reads it as an attack on rowdy clerical games and their consequences. | p. 63
term: Steracle (plural steraclis) | A rare Middle English word for a show or sight, probably from the same idea as "spectacle". Around 1410 to 1420 a Norfolk witness listed as steracles the shooting, wrestling, stone-putting, running for a spear and football for cakes at a gathering. | p. 80
term: Bourd (bourding) | Middle English for jesting or joking, especially with a familiarity unfit between unequals. The Tretise uses it for people making a game out of sacred events. | p. 95
term: Mimis | The author's shorthand for canon 16 of the Fourth Lateran Council (1215). It told clerics not to watch mimes, entertainers and actors, to avoid taverns, and not to play or watch dice and games of chance. | p. 64
idea: Judge an activity by purpose and effect, not by class | In the Franciscan Dives and Pauper, Pauper allows shows, plays and dances done for devotion and honest mirth, without ribaldry, and without keeping people from church services. He does not condemn them because they belong to a class of things, and cites David dancing before the ark. | p. 82
idea: Two similar dances, different standing | The author contrasts the women of Boston, who danced holding candles as members of a guild, with the cursed dancers of Colbek and the Bury women who walked with a white bull to gain fertility. Each is a dance, and only some were thought fit for holy days. | p. 83
idea: Contests used the word "play" | A Norfolk witness c. 1410 to 1420 described "diverse plays" played on one ground: shooting, wrestling, running for a spear, and football for cakes, and later grouped them under steracles. Sports with prizes were then plays and shows. | p. 80
idea: Sign against the thing | The Tretise argues that a feigned miracle is only a sign. By making false tokens, people cheat themselves and show contempt for the real thing, as the tormentors who mocked Christ did. The author reads the objection as one to mockery, not to devout representation. | p. 84
idea: Play dissolves awe | The tract's theme is that a servant who jokes with his master loses his fear of offending him. In the same way, treating Christ's serious works as play removes the fear of God people are meant to have. | p. 95
idea: Imitation by the watchers | The Tretise says lay people join in such plays because they see the clergy taking part in jesting. A model set by one group is copied by another. | p. 88
idea: Watching can be forbidden as well as playing | The tract cites the canons forbidding priests to be a miracle player or to hear or see such playing. The Psalter's plea to turn the eyes from vanity is used to say a priest cannot give himself to the sight of interludes. | p. 98
idea: Play meaning fight | The Tretise cites Bible verses where people "play" (ludere), and critics have noted that in two of them the word means fighting. The soldiers of Abner and Joab rose to play before the court and were killed; Sarah saw Ishmael playing with Isaac and sent him away. | p. 89
idea: Counting records tests a claim | If the Tretise targets civic cycle plays, then by 1400 only York and Coventry were known to have cycles, and the only Antichrist play, at Chester, is dated about 1467. The author concludes that the "play of Antichrist" would more likely be a clerical masked game. | p. 87
idea: Three kinds of play | Following Aquinas, a scholar finds medieval writers sorting play into wicked or devilish play, recreational play that is judged case by case, and spiritual play such as David's dance. Aquinas ordered the question around Aristotle's mean, excess and defect. | p. 91
idea: Fixed words against loose words | One scholar holds that medieval words for play and players were too indeterminate to say what was meant. The author replies that some terms, such as ludi theatrales as glossed on Cum decorem, had a settled sense even if the activities under them varied. | p. 94
idea: Context changes how a text reads | Jonas Barish found the Tretise's Bible examples absurd, such as using Elisha's mockers to attack stage plays. The author says the examples make sense once the tract is read as against jeering and parody, not against drama. | p. 96
term: Waits | Musicians hired by a town to play on a regular schedule and to attend the town's officials at feasts and on walks through the streets. | p. 113
term: Banns | A public announcement of a coming play or game. New Romney paid town criers from neighbouring places to proclaim banns, and was paid in turn when its own play was cried in other parishes. | p. 114
term: Playbook | The written text of a town's play, kept safe by an official. At New Romney the book was held by the clerk of the Commonalty and later passed to a craftsman who built parts of the play. | p. 115
term: Recognizance (cast list) | A written record of who is to play which part. Three survive for New Romney's Passion play of 1555 and list the disciples, Pilate, Herod, and tormentors with names like Mischance and Untrust. | p. 117
term: Deviser | The person who arranges a pageant, revel or play: its scenes, structures, costumes and materials. Richard Gibson devised court revels for forty years, and Gover Martin was deviser of the New Romney play of 1560. | p. 116
term: Stage (a station in a play) | In the 1560 New Romney accounts, a separate built place for one party in the action: Pilate's stage, Herod's stage, the Tormentors' stage, heaven, the cave and hell. | p. 118
idea: Group practice, not individual taste | The author describes what groups allowed and what was common, and sets aside what was idiosyncratic. Group practice was shaped by status, time, place, wealth and the wish to show off. | p. 108
idea: Processions bind a group | Towns and parishes used processions to show devotion, unity or both, and to mark the group as a body. Groups fined members who shirked their part, and processions never fully replaced the parish ale and its games. | p. 109
idea: Wealth and size did not decide who staged plays | In 1377 only seven English towns had more than three thousand people, and most towns never produced drama even when they seemed large enough. Exeter, a rich cathedral city of about 2,340, shows little sign of drama. The author names money as the first hurdle and the wish to do it as a second. | p. 113
idea: Towns trade shows to raise money | New Romney paid the men of neighbouring towns to show their games or interludes and had its own play cried in return. Small places in thinly peopled areas used this cooperation to raise funds. | p. 114
idea: The sum of group sizes | Of thirty-five Selby Abbey entries that gave the number of players, fifteen were single performers, and about three-fourths of the groups were four or fewer. These fall below the "four men and a boy" Bevington gives for an acting troupe, so the author reads most as musicians. | p. 112
idea: A licence to play | In 1517 the Warden of the Cinque Ports told New Romney it could not play the Passion without the king's licence. In 1528-29 a commission against stage plays came, and the play stopped for years. | p. 115
idea: Building the playing ground | For the 1499-1500 Passion play, New Romney had the playing field mown, built a hell and a heaven, and paid for the tormentors' garments and heads. By 1560 the set had at least eight separate stages. | p. 115
idea: Rehearsal and the cost of the cast | For the 1560 play the town met in church to set who would lend money, labour or a stage. Beer was brought to the church for the players at a rehearsal on 31 March. | p. 118
idea: Run the same show again | The 1560 New Romney Passion was played on Whit Monday and on two Sundays, and a fourth performance seems to have followed. Takings fell from over £12 at the first to about £4 at the third, yet the author counts it a commercial success. | p. 118
idea: Spectacle makers and tiltyard assaults | Richard Gibson built court pageants for assaults on a fortress in 1512 and a timber Castle of Loyalty, with drawbridges, ditches and bulwarks, for the tiltyard in 1524-25. The author notes he was a maker of spectacle and not known for dramas. | p. 116
idea: Allegorical names for roles | The tormentors in the 1555 New Romney Passion play were given names that state a quality: Mischance, False-at-Need, Untrust, Faint-heart, Unhappe and Evil Grace. | p. 117
idea: The name does not give the content | A Shrewsbury "play" of Saint Katherine in 1525-26 included a lord of misrule, a jester's head and materials for explosions and smoke, with nothing about the saint. The author reads it as a revel. | p. 106
idea: An old mistake becomes authority | The author traces the idea that miracula meant saint plays to a 1798 paper that misread a text. Once its author had standing, later scholars debated which records fit that meaning instead of asking whether it was right. | p. 107
idea: Shared illusion against deceit | The author contrasts a devotional play, where the audience knows the illusion is an illusion and enjoys the spectacle, with a clerical Antichrist game meant to entertain with illusions as a trick. | p. 105
idea: Touch and movement in dance | Hugh of St. Cher, commenting on the line about never joining those who play, glosses "play" as dances. He quotes Jerome that temptation is hard to avoid when fingers touch or feet move. | p. 100
term: Pageant | Originally a "show", especially something painted or ornamented. In records it can mean a decorated wheeled object, a living picture in a painted set, a carried effigy or shrine, or a painted cloth; at court it also covered built structures such as a pageant castle and dungeon that Henry VIII and five lords assaulted. | p. 130
term: Tableau vivant | A living picture: people posed in a painted set, shown in a procession or royal entry without acting out a story. | p. 130
term: Hocktide | A spring custom after Easter used by parishes to raise money. On Hock Monday women captured and bound men for ransom; on Hock Tuesday men captured women. | p. 122
term: King game | A parish game, often at Whitsun, in which locals chose a king, sometimes a queen too, to preside over feasts and entertainments. The king could be a fool figure, and bowers were built in the fields. | p. 122
term: Robin Hood game | A parish fund-raiser in which young men dressed as Robin Hood and his band and went about collecting alms. It is not usually a script. | p. 122
term: Quête | A collecting round made by a costumed group, such as a boy bishop, mummers, disguisers or plough boys. The author places it between asking for donations and holding people to ransom. | p. 125
term: Communitas | An idea from Victor Turner: a situation outside the usual social structure in which people are more equal than normal, and which renews the society it briefly leaves. The author applies it to parish ales and games. | p. 125
term: Saint play | An enactment that shows the life of a saint, or a major event in it, usually with a spoken script. The author argues that most records called "play of Saint X" show something else. | p. 128
term: Folk hero-combat play | A traditional play in which England's champion fights a motley crew of clowns and Turks. Saint George appears in some of these. | p. 131
term: Rogation Day procession | A walk marking the bounds of a town and its fields. It gave Christian meaning to an older spring fertility ritual. | p. 139
idea: Role reversal and ransom | On Hock Monday women tied up men and released them for payment, and on Hock Tuesday men did the same to women. Takings on the Monday were usually much larger than on the Tuesday. | p. 122
idea: Public reversal is the objection | A bishop in 1450 complained of Hock days in which women bound men and men women, "in full view of passers-by". The author thinks the worry was that this was done openly, as a threat to the order of ranks. | p. 123
idea: Theory against what the records show | Some scholars read carnival-like games as moments when rulers loosen control only to restore it. The author notes that these games were started by the local people and carried on against reforming bishops, so the theory does not match the case. | p. 123
idea: Substitute, do not only forbid | Bishop Grandisson promoted processions as a lay observance with the decorum of religion without copying liturgy. Lay people in turn took up clerical customs, such as the Easter sepulchre with its watching and lights. | p. 123
idea: A temporary equality | The author says parish ales set aside rank for a time and invited outsiders in. Contests and shared food and drink would dissolve or redirect tension, though he doubts the Wife of Bath gave up wanting to be first. | p. 126
idea: Reformers objected to the place | The author suspects reformers disliked parish games less for what they were than for happening in cemeteries and sacred ground. Drinking and dancing there could pollute the area and challenge clerical rank. | p. 126
idea: Costume in guild processions | The twelve members of the Guild of the Apostles at Louth dressed as apostles. At Beverley a youth dressed as Queen Helen walked with an old man with a cross and another with a spade, to represent the finding of the Cross. | p. 127
idea: "Play of Saint X" may name an owner, not a story | A record of a "ludus de sancto Thoma didimo" at Lincoln was an Easter liturgical piece. The 1505 "ludum sancti trinitatis" cannot be a play about a Saint Trinity, so it names a guild or church that gave the game. | p. 129
idea: A ludus is first of all a fund-raiser | The author concludes that a ludus or game was most likely a parish or civic entertainment to raise money. It might contain a scripted play, or only sports, contests, amusements and pageants. | p. 129
idea: Objects to be seen, not acted | Some ludi were shown, not acted. The Assumption at Lincoln appears to have been a clockwork device, and Canterbury's Becket pageant, carried by four knights, held an effigy and a mechanical angel. | p. 129
idea: Saint George in street and tableau | Saint George was mostly seen in tableaux, in processions, in the street fighting a dragon, or in folk plays with clowns and Turks. Only Lydd offers a possible enacted life over several years. | p. 130
idea: London compared with Rome | William Fitz Stephen wrote that London had "holier" representations of saints' miracles and martyrdoms in place of Rome's theatrical shows, and he also lists ice skating and wrestling. The author reads this as comparing London's liturgy with the obscenity of the ancient theatrum. | p. 131
idea: A book is not always a script | A Hereford list of pageants for a Corpus Christi procession had no acted drama. An Ipswich man admitted to freedom in 1492 agreed to help with the pageant and correct the Corpus Christi book. The book may hold the order of a procession and speeches for tableaux. | p. 133
idea: Absence in many records | The author finds no saint plays in the 1389 guild returns, nor in Edwardian inventories, nor in the richly recorded areas of East Anglia, Essex and Kent. In their place he finds processions, church ales and boy bishops. | p. 135
idea: A mix that satisfied both sides | The church used suppression and replacement, and local people resisted. The result was a blend of folk festivity such as parish ales, Robin Hood games and hockings with approved acts such as processions and Easter sepulchres. When boy bishops were banned they were quickly restored under Mary. | p. 137
idea: Fill the same season | Reformers saw that to end disorderly ludi they had to give people a licit activity in the same season. The author reads the promotion of Corpus Christi, with its procession, as meant to give the laity both a share in religion and a form of festival. | p. 138
idea: A costly failure | In 1562 Chelmsford staged four plays, spent more than £21, lost £11, and never tried drama again. | p. 119
idea: Even rich towns kept to processions | Bristol, second among provincial towns in 1377, left little record beyond processions and pageants for royal visits. Its guild pageants were small enough for one bearer, and by 1530 the Wiredrawers and Pinmakers needed two. | p. 121
idea: A priest tied to the village | The author suggests parish priests supported ales because they helped call the banns, belonged to the village and its families, and farmed glebe land, so they were both parson and ploughman. | p. 121
term: Cycle (of plays) | A series of biblical plays running from Creation to Doomsday. The author says full cycles were rare, and that Coventry and Chester before the sixteenth century probably covered only New Testament subjects. | p. 157
term: Castle (Beverley) | A built structure for the Rogation Monday procession of the relics of Saint John of Beverley. It was a viewing stand with ample refreshment, where prominent guilds sat to watch, and guild members wore livery there. | p. 150
term: Riding of the banns | A procession through a town announcing a coming play. At Chester it followed a route that touched every seat of secular power, so that it announced the city's jurisdiction. | p. 147
term: Pater Noster play | A guild-sponsored play built on the Lord's Prayer, whose seven petitions were linked to the seven Deadly Sins. At Beverley each sin was assigned to a group of guilds, and an eighth pageant, Vicious, to the gentlemen. | p. 154
idea: Government decides who stages plays | The author finds cycles in cities that had a firm trade-guild government and no rival power, such as York and Coventry. Where a cathedral shared power or religious guilds ruled, as at Lincoln, he finds processions and small plays. | p. 142
idea: Conditions make a play possible, not certain | The author says a firm civic government does not guarantee drama. Money and political conditions have to exist first, and then a body decides whether it wants to display itself or feels an obligation to teach. | p. 143
idea: Marching order shows rank | Coventry's Corpus Christi procession began with the victuallers, who by statute could not hold civic office. It went on through the leather and metal trades and then the wool trades, ending with the Dyers, Drapers and Mercers. The order followed how much and how long each trade held office, not how rich it was. | p. 144
idea: Share the cost by joining | In 1494-95 Coventry told every guild not paying for the plays to join one that did. Mostly the lesser guilds paid money to the old guilds, which kept making the pageants. In the 1530s a rise of the Cappers and decay of the Cardmakers forced a reshuffle. | p. 145
idea: A prisoner let out for the play | John Careles, jailed in Coventry in 1556 for his faith, asked to be released on his own recognizance for one day to act in the Weavers' play with his fellows. He returned to jail afterwards. | p. 145
idea: Route as a claim to space | Chester moved its plays inside the walls in the 1500s. The plays gathered at the barrs, played first in the Cornmarket in front of the abbey gates, then at the Pentice where the mayor sat, then in the main streets. The author reads the route as announcing lay control, while the abbey supplied beer. | p. 147
idea: Stated reasons and economic reasons together | Chester's banns say the plays were made to strengthen the faith and to exhort the people to devotion, and they also admit money motives. The author does not treat the first as a mask for the second and sees a city taking up responsibility for its citizens' souls. | p. 148
idea: Summing records can inflate a total | Historians reckoned a Newcastle cycle of twenty-five plays by adding references scattered over 150 years. The author says this ignores changes over time and what is missing. | p. 148
idea: A small play takes one action | The Newcastle Noah's Dirge ends at the entry to the ark. It has a small cast, no children, no loading of animals, no flood and no dove, and Noah's wife is made to get him drunk. The author takes it as a sign of modest aims. | p. 149
idea: Cost per member as a measure of scale | Beverley had about a third of York's taxpayers in 1377 (2,663 against 7,248), and its guildsmen paid 4 to 8 pence a play. At Chester in 1561 most Smiths paid 2s. 4d. and the Shoemakers spent 21s. 4d. on their Jerusalem Carriage. The author reads Beverley's pageants as small. | p. 153
idea: The viewing stand came first | In Beverley's guild ordinances the clauses on the castle and the lights come before any clause on the Corpus Christi pageant, and castles were built every year while plays were not. The author concludes the castles and lights ranked higher than the pageants. | p. 150
idea: A fine for faulty playing implies a performance | Beverley guild fines included some for not playing as required, so a pageant had speeches or action of some sort. The titles, such as "The Pinnacle" for one of three temptations, point to a single emblematic scene. | p. 153
idea: Sins assigned to groups of guilds | For the Pater Noster play the governors gave Pride to the Shoemakers, Saddlers, Fullers, Tilers, Ropers and Capmakers together, and gave an eighth pageant, Vicious, to the gentlemen. They were to play at seven places, the same as for the Corpus Christi play. | p. 154
idea: Taking turns by year | Lincoln's city ludus, called the Corpus Christi or Pater Noster play, usually ran one year, and the cathedral's Assumption and Coronation display ran in the alternate years. | p. 154
idea: Two sponsors, one calendar | York's Corpus Christi guild put on the Creed Play and the Pater Noster guild its play, each at about ten-year intervals. The citizens saw the city's cycle every year and these two at set intervals. | p. 155
idea: A partial list taken for a whole | When Chambers and Craig found a list of Bible stories, they concluded a full cycle had existed and its remaining parts were lost. The author says this came from assuming that drama had evolved from church to street, and that recent work finds cycles rare. | p. 157
idea: Permission to pass through a jurisdiction | The author suggests city governments came to manage religious processions because the processions moved through the city's territory. A mayor welcoming a visitor grants him and his group entry, in the same way. | p. 158
idea: Processions and plays kept apart | If a play went with a procession, it was staged at the end or in the afternoon of the feast day. Where the plays themselves moved through the city, the procession ran one day and the plays another. | p. 158
idea: Old Testament scenes for single plays | The author offers three reasons why places with one play often chose Old Testament subjects: they had limited scope in the sources, they suited moral reading, and some people were wary of showing the Passion. | p. 159
idea: Pictures in the street | The author argues that guilds and city governments had no churches to put paintings and statues in, so they placed their "pictures" in the streets as living scenes for people who could not read. A preacher in the Hundred Merry Tales says to see the creed enacted, go to Coventry. | p. 159
term: London aesthetic | The taste of London's court and ruling guilds for elaborate display and formal speech, not narrative scenes with dialogue. Fully acted biblical or moral scripts were not given in London proper until well into the sixteenth century. | p. 160
term: Presentational (against representational) | A style in which figures do not speak in their own voices but are introduced and "read" by a presenter, with little talk between participants. The author uses it for Lydgate's mummings and court pageants, as against plays where actors take on characters and speak to one another. | p. 164
term: Mumming | An entertainment in which disguised figures arrive at a feast, typically introduced by a presenter, and end with a gift, a song or a dance. Lydgate wrote mummings for London guilds and for the court. | p. 161
term: Débat | A staged argument between two sides, with a judge. The author finds it in court disguisings such as the Hertford mumming, where husbands and wives state their cases and the king decides. | p. 162
term: Pageant car | A wheeled pageant used in court revels. At Greenwich in 1514 one held a castle, a rock, and a gilded cave with silk curtains, with musicians hidden in the rock and a presenter reading its meaning. | p. 165
term: Palm Sunday prophets | A custom in which children in beards sang or read prophecies from a frame or scaffold over a church porch. At Saint Mary Magdalen Milk the prophets then rode in procession. | p. 160
idea: Display and speech, not story | The author says London's civic and court shows were processions, pageants and formal orations. They had elaborate display but little narrative acted with dialogue, even though London parishes had boy bishops, Hocktide, May games and Palm Sunday prophets. | p. 160
idea: A staged effect in a parish | In 1536-37 Saint Mary Magdalen Milk had people play God and Longinus, and filled bladders with liquor that ran out of the Cross. | p. 161
idea: Figures read aloud by a presenter | In Lydgate's mumming of about 1427, a presenter introduces and "reads" Fortune on her island, then four ladies, Prudence, Righteousness, Fortitude and Temperance. They end with a song and Fortune's banishment. The figures do not speak for themselves. | p. 162
idea: A judgement put off for a year | In Lydgate's Hertford mumming of 1430, six husbands complain of their wives, the wives answer, and the king delays his decision a year and leaves the wives in charge. The author reads this as showing the limit of a prince's power: his court has no process to reverse the matter. | p. 163
idea: Debasing the characters distances the audience | Lydgate made the Hertford characters rustic, so extravagant gesture and farce became possible. The author says this lets an audience of high rank imagine the situation is not theirs, although the closing line says it is. | p. 164
idea: Ingredients of a court disguising | The author lists the usual parts of an elaborate disguising: one or more pageant cars with allegorical or romance settings, an interpreter of the scene, a debate, music and finally dancing. At Greenwich in 1514 fifteen men took sides in a tourney before the pageant car was drawn before the king. | p. 166
idea: Dispute settled by combat, then a judge | In the 1527 Dialogue of Love and Riches, two groups of choristers argued for Cupid and for Plutus. When the case stayed open, three knights on each side fought across a barrier, and an old gentleman declared that princes need both love and riches. | p. 166
idea: A shift of verse marks a fall | In Medwall's Nature, Man speaks in rhyme royal at first and moves to a tail-rhyme form when he agrees to banish Innocence. The author notes that a form linked with northern writing marks the turn into a fallen world. | p. 168
idea: Opening as procession and stations | Medwall's Nature begins with processions of Nature and World, who station themselves to point to their opposition. The first part is a formal debate that, in a court disguising, would lead to a ritual battle and a reconciliation. | p. 167
idea: Remaking the conditions teaches | Modern productions of the plays on moveable carts or stationary pageants have shown what simple stage structures can do. They have also shown the range of verse forms that work as stage speech. | p. 171
