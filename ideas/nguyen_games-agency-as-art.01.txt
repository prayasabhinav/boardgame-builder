term: Motivational inversion | In ordinary life people pursue means for the sake of ends. In a game a player takes up an end, such as getting a ball in a net, for the sake of the activity of pursuing it. | ch. 1
term: Agency | The capacity to act for a reason. The author treats a game as something that shapes the player's agency by setting goals, abilities and obstacles. | ch. 1
term: Agential fluidity | The human ability to take up new goals and abilities quickly and drop them just as quickly, as a player does when a game starts and ends. | ch. 1
term: Technical activity | Suits's name for an activity done to reach an end that is valued for itself, so the person takes the most efficient route. A game is the opposite, because the player accepts less efficient routes on purpose. | ch. 1
term: Goal of a game | The target a player aims at during play, such as crossing the finish line first or scoring more points. It is separate from the player's reason for playing. | ch. 1
term: Achievement play | Playing for the sake of winning, either for its own sake or for what follows, such as prize money. Goal and purpose are the same thing. | ch. 1
term: Striving play | Playing for the sake of the struggle to win. The player cares about winning only while the game lasts, and drops that care afterwards. | ch. 1
term: Stupid game | A game that only works if players try to win, yet whose memorable moments are the failures, such as the pile-up in Twister or the garbled message at the end of Telephone. | ch. 1
term: Disposable end | A goal taken up for a short time and put down without harm to the player's lasting values. Collecting green tokens in one board game is the plainest case. | ch. 1
term: Aesthetic striving play | Play pursued for the quality of the struggle itself, such as grace, an elegant solution or a tense turn of events. | ch. 1
term: Artistic medium | The set of technical resources an artist works with, as opposed to the raw material. For painting this includes brushstrokes as well as pigment. | ch. 1
term: Practical environment | The surroundings of a game seen as something that stands against the player's goals and abilities, such as the walls of a climb or the settings of a software level. | ch. 1
term: Scoring system | The part of a game that says what to care about and whether players compete or cooperate. The board game designer Reiner Knizia called it his main tool. | ch. 1
term: Library of agencies | The idea that the games people have made form a store of recorded ways of acting, which later players can enter and learn from. | ch. 1
term: Pre-lusory goal | In Suits's account, the state of affairs a player tries to bring about, described without the rules, such as getting the ball through the hoop. | ch. 2
term: Constitutive rules | Rules that forbid the most efficient route to the pre-lusory goal and so create the game. Dribbling in basketball is one. | ch. 2
term: Lusory goal | Reaching the pre-lusory goal while obeying the rules. In basketball this is making a basket, not merely getting the ball through the hoop. | ch. 2
term: Lusory attitude | The state of mind in which a person accepts the goal and the rules for the sake of the activity they make possible. | ch. 2
term: Game purism | The view that a person only plays a game if they do it purely for the pleasure of playing. The author says Suits did not hold this view. | ch. 2
term: Intrinsic play | Play for the value of the activity itself. Intrinsic and extrinsic play is a different split from achievement and striving play. | ch. 2
term: Extrinsic play | Play for something it brings, such as fitness, fame or money. A professional sprinter and a person running for mental health both play this way. | ch. 2
term: Submersion | Letting a temporary goal fill one's mind so that it feels like a final aim, while the reasons for playing drop out of sight. | ch. 3
term: Self-effacing end | An aim that cannot be reached by aiming at it directly. Absorption in a climb, or relaxation, only arrive when the person aims at something else, such as reaching the top. | ch. 3
term: Agential layering | Holding a temporary game agency inside one's lasting agency. The inner layer wants to win; the outer layer chose the game and can stop it. | ch. 3
term: Maieutic end | An end that is reached by coming to have other ends, such as settling on a career. Once the new end is found, it stands on its own. | ch. 3
term: Normative attachment | A goal depends for its worth on another goal. In play the game goals stay attached to the player's wider wish for an interesting struggle, so the player can leave the game if it fails that wish. | ch. 3
term: Plausible deniability of failure | Juul's name for the habit of treating a game as important when winning and as unimportant when losing. | ch. 3
term: Sore loser | A player who fails to put down game goals when the game ends. | ch. 3
term: Rules-free view of autonomy | The view that the fewer the rules, the freer the person. The author argues against it. | ch. 4
term: Agential mode | A focused way of being an agent: one set of goals paired with one set of abilities, such as mending a house with a carpenter's skills. | ch. 4
idea: Game goals are justified by looking backwards | In daily life a goal is worth chasing because of what it is or what follows from it. In a game the goal is worth chasing because of the kind of activity chasing it produces. A marathon runner does not take a taxi to the finish line, since arriving is not the point. | ch. 1
idea: Goal and purpose in a game can differ | The goal of party Charades is to win, but the purpose is to have a pleasant evening. A player so competitive that everyone is miserable has met the goal and missed the purpose. | ch. 1
idea: A designer shapes agency, not only a world | A game sets a goal, picks the abilities the player may use and builds obstacles that fit both. Super Mario gives a run and a jump that are only just enough for the chasms in front of the player, and the chess knight's odd leap fits the problems of the board. | ch. 1
idea: Games record agency the way other arts record other things | Painting records sights, music records sounds and stories record events. Games write down a way of acting, so it can be kept and handed to another person. The role-playing game Sign hands players the experience of inventing a sign language in silence. | ch. 1
idea: Tokens only matter inside the game | Before a board game starts, nobody cares about the green tokens. During play they cause sweating palms. Afterwards they go into a plastic bag. The caring is created by the rules and ends with them. | ch. 1
idea: Players may hold back their own skill to keep a game tense | The author declines free lessons in racquetball because one player pulling far ahead would end the pleasure of the matches. Between games he guards the struggle; during a game he plays all out to win. | ch. 1
idea: Players cannot stay aware of the reason for playing while playing | A child who slips money to a losing father in Monopoly, so the game never ends, has kept the purpose in view and spoiled the game. Absorption needs the win to look like a real end for the length of play. | ch. 1
idea: Dropping a game is not like losing a value | When a Charades game is making guests bicker, players do not weigh winning against other things. Winning simply stops mattering, and the game's ends fade from sight. | ch. 2
idea: Some games work by stopping skill from growing | In a drinking game where each player names a candy bar nobody has named yet, the failures are the memorable part. A person who memorised lists of candy bars would be missing the point. Such games are played rarely so nobody gets skilled at them. | ch. 2
idea: Choosing a game is itself evidence for striving play | People talk about which game to play in terms of interest, beauty and fascination, not excellence or prizes. A group where each member is likely to lose at some game still plays it, because the struggle is the attraction. | ch. 2
idea: Players of unequal skill keep playing games they will probably lose | In the author's board game circle one member is most skilled at calculation, one at bluffing, one at careful planning. Each still plays games suited to the others, and a local chess club welcomes a grandmaster and does not flee from one. | ch. 2
idea: A game can narrow what a player has to think about | Daily life asks for many values to be weighed at once. A game cuts these to a few clear ones, such as score and position. This makes it easy to enter the game and easy to judge whether a move was elegant. | ch. 3
idea: A scoring system makes values look simpler than they are | In the video game Knights of the Old Republic, a moral choice is followed at once by a Light or Dark score. The critic Sicart says this makes morality seem simple. The author replies that simplicity is the point of a game, and the risk lies in carrying the expectation of clarity back into life. | ch. 3
idea: Player and character stand differently in games and in theatre | An actor playing a bank robber stuck in a vault may choose to have him act foolishly because the story needs it. A game player trapped in the same situation wants to escape. The player's real wish to escape is what produces the thrill of a game. | ch. 2
idea: An outer self keeps watch over an inner game self | The outer self chooses the game, can stop it when it stops being worth playing, and changes the rules with house rules. The author's Sign group agreed never to reveal what they had tried to communicate, which only makes sense if winning is a temporary interest. | ch. 3
idea: A player can feel loss and delight together | A person can be agonised at losing and also see that the game was beautiful. The author explains this as two layers: the inner game self suffers while the outer self enjoys the struggle. His Go teacher Joe replayed a position so a fine game could go on. | ch. 3
idea: Fixed and clear aims are why games are easy to enter | The clear, narrow goals of a game make a new way of acting visible, which helps a player step into it. | ch. 3
idea: Restrictions can add options | A house in an open field limits movement but creates the options of inside and outside. In the same way, making a basket in basketball is a new action that exists only because of the rules. | ch. 4
idea: Games hand over ways of acting from the inside | Spyfall gives one player a spy card and the others a shared location. Team players have to hint that they know the place without telling the spy. Playing it trains a person to listen for vagueness and bluffing, which they might then use in a committee meeting. | ch. 4
idea: Free play and structured games give different things | The critic Sicart argues that a designer ought only to offer a stage and let players do as they like. The author argues that rules and goals are what let a game pass on a specific way of acting, so a store of such games builds a wider range of agency than free play can. | ch. 4
term: Ludic loop | The anthropologist Natasha Dow Schüll's name for a steady stream of small challenges and rewards at a set pace, as in video slot machines, which keeps people playing without the play being interesting. | ch. 4
term: Machine zone | The state described by long-time slot machine gamblers in which awareness of the world, time and self falls away. | ch. 4
term: Harmony of solution | The fit between a move and the problem it answers, such as a chess move that escapes a trap. A spectator can see it as well as the player. | ch. 5
term: Harmony of action | The sense, available to the player, that their own deciding and acting fitted what the situation asked, such as timing a jump in Super Mario just so. | ch. 5
term: Harmony of capacity | The sense that all of one's abilities are working at their limit and only just enough for the task. It appears only in demanding play. | ch. 5
term: Flash | A climbing word for completing a hard problem on the first attempt. | ch. 5
term: Procedural rhetoric | Ian Bogost's name for making an argument by modelling a causal system, so that the player finds the argument by playing. McDonald's Video Game is the standing example. | ch. 5
term: Serious game | Mary Flanagan's name for a game that delivers social or political criticism, such as September 12th. | ch. 5
term: Play stance | Taking part in a game directly, in the practical activity of play. | ch. 5
term: Spectator stance | Watching part or all of a game session without taking part in it. | ch. 5
term: Design stance | Attending to the design of the game itself and how it supports many different playings, not to one session. | ch. 5
term: Disinterested interestedness | The author's description of aesthetic striving play: during the game the player is wholly interested in winning, while the reason for playing is to enjoy the struggle, whatever the result. | ch. 5
term: Strategic perception | Simon Dor's term for how experienced players see a game differently, such as a chess player seeing lines of movement and not the figure of a rook. | ch. 6
term: Prescriptive frame | The set of norms telling an audience how to encounter a work and what to attend to. A painting is looked at from the front; a game is played by its rules toward its goal. | ch. 6
term: Prescriptive ontology | The view that a work is not just its material but the material as met under the right prescriptions. Reading Moby Dick's words in random order is not reading Moby Dick. | ch. 6
term: Material basis | The physical or coded stuff of a work, which anyone may handle in any way, as opposed to the work itself, which is met by following the prescriptions. | ch. 6
term: Ludic imperative | Olli Leino's name for the assumption that to study a game one has to play it in the spirit its designer intended. Leino objects to it; the author does not. | ch. 6
term: General prescription | The author's rule for aesthetic striving games: play following the rules and aiming at the goal, then appreciate the activity that results. | ch. 6
term: Agential center of gravity | The part of a player's activity where their agency was most active, which draws their attention when they look back on play. | ch. 6
term: Party game | A game whose practice discourages skill building, such as Cards Against Humanity, where the winner is picked by a judge's taste. | ch. 6
term: Heavy strategy game | A game whose central features only show after repeated plays and real skill, such as 1830 or Dream Quest. | ch. 6
term: Community evolution game | A game, such as Magic: The Gathering or Android: Netrunner, whose play depends on a player community that keeps changing which decks and strategies are common. | ch. 6
term: The meta | The shifting set of popular decks and strategies among a game's players, which each player has to respond to when building a deck. | ch. 6
term: Rogue-like | A computer game where each playing has a freshly generated map and a character who dies permanently. | ch. 6
term: Speedrunning | A way of playing a game where the goal is changed to finishing as fast as possible, sometimes by using glitches. The author counts it as a different game on the same materials. | ch. 6
term: Agential distance | The gap between what a designer makes, such as rules and pieces, and what the player appreciates, which is their own activity. The designer has to reach the effect through the player's agency. | ch. 7
term: Artist's work | The stable thing a maker produces, such as the rules, pieces and software of a game. | ch. 7
term: Attentive focus | What the audience is told to attend to. For a painting it is the painting; for an aesthetic striving game it is the player's own activity. | ch. 7
term: Object art | An art made for the qualities of the finished object, such as a painting. | ch. 7
term: Process art | An art made so that the audience appreciates the qualities of its own activity. The author lists games, social dances, cooking and fondue as examples. | ch. 7
term: Participatory art | A work whose audience has to take some action, such as a choice, in order to experience it. | ch. 7
term: Generative participatory art | A participatory work where the audience's activity produces the thing they then attend to. | ch. 7
term: Recalcitrance of a medium | The built-in difficulty of working in a medium that cannot be settled by thinking beforehand, such as the slowness of oil paint. For games the author names it agential distance. | ch. 7
term: Path of the puzzle | One way of crossing agential distance: allow only a narrow set of solutions so players' experiences stay close. Text adventures and Myst did this. | ch. 7
term: Player profiles Timmy Johnny Spike | Magic designer Mark Rosewater's three kinds of player: one who wants a feeling, one who wants to express something, one who wants to prove something. | ch. 7
term: Oppositional game | A game where players act against each other. The author notes that a game can be competitive without being oppositional, as when people race to solve the same puzzle. | ch. 7
idea: Strict rules in the short term can widen what a person can do later | Zen monks greet everyone with a loud hello without thinking; climbers practise easy routes without bending their elbows so that hip rotation becomes natural; yoga gives exact postures. The author argues game rules work the same way, by walking a player into a way of acting they would not reach alone. | ch. 4
idea: Games hand over modes of acting that suit particular tasks | Pandemic gives one shared goal with different abilities per player, Monopoly a self-centred drive to ruin others, Risk a self-interest with temporary alliances, Imperial an agency of shaping others' incentives. The author says playing 1830 helped him understand how corporations reason. | ch. 4
idea: Narrow ways of acting are useful if a person can switch | A narrowed mode looks only at a small set of reasons, but a person who can cycle through many modes sees more reasons in total than one broad mode could hold. The author compares this to carrying a Swiss Army knife of agencies. | ch. 4
idea: A game can crowd out other experience | If a person plays one game so often that they skip other kinds of life experience, the range of their agency shrinks. The author says slot machine designs with a ludic loop work this way, and that the same techniques have moved into mobile games such as Candy Crush. | ch. 4
idea: Go teaches comparison of many fights at once | A Go teacher told the author that chess is one fight and Go is ten at once, so the player keeps comparing the value of each and gives up one to play elsewhere. He says this habit of stepping back carried over to his daily choices. | ch. 4
idea: Games are not the only route to new modes of acting but they are quick and exact | Careers and hobbies can also teach a mode of agency. A game compresses one mode into a short, narrow and clearly stated form. That makes it easier to enter than a whole life project, though less rich. | ch. 4
idea: Representation is one value of games, not the whole of it | Critics praise games such as Train, where players ship yellow pieces by rail until they learn they are carrying prisoners to camps, or September 12th, for what they say about the world. The author accepts this and adds that a game can also be valued for the quality of the player's own striving. | ch. 5
idea: A new art form can be hidden by borrowing the standards of an older one | Early art photographers blurred their pictures to look like paintings and only found photography's own strengths after dropping that aim. The author warns that praising games only as fiction or film may do the same. | ch. 5
idea: Players can feel three different kinds of fit | A spectator can see that a move solves a problem. A player also feels that their own decision was right, and, near the limit of their skill, that all their abilities were only just enough. In the author's rock climbing, warm-up climbs give the first two and a desperate climb gives the third. | ch. 5
idea: The tense, awkward movement near a limit looks clumsy but feels harmonious | A climber on easy routes moves elegantly; the same climber at the edge of ability clutches and lunges. The player feels the closest fit in the second case while an onlooker sees the first as more graceful. | ch. 5
idea: A game can be about clumsiness on purpose | In QWOP, four keys control a runner's thighs and calves, so running is hard; in Octodad the player moves an octopus with a limb on each trigger; in Minotaur in a China Shop the controls are erratic. The designers build an experience of not being able to do what you mean to. | ch. 5
idea: Difficulty can be wanted for the fit, not the prize | The author argues players pick hard games for the feeling that their whole ability meets the task. This explains why a first-try flash feels satisfying with no earlier failures to make a story of. | ch. 5
idea: Strategy players stop noticing the look of a game | Dor found that new players soak in graphics and music while experienced players drop them and watch only what matters for winning. This is why chess pieces and Starcraft tiles repeat simple shapes. | ch. 5
idea: Judging a game needs playing it | A review based on a game's code or box art, or after twenty hours spent only in the character creator of Oblivion, is rejected by players. The author reads this as showing that players treat a game as a work met through play. | ch. 6
idea: Variants and speedruns are different games | Speedrunning Super Mario is a legitimate way to play; it is another game played with the same program. Chess variants likewise are different games on the same pieces. | ch. 6
idea: Rules fix what the experience is | In Bridge, the ban on speaking makes the game one of deduction; in Poker, the ban on looking at others' cards makes it one of reading behaviour; in free climbing, the ban on pulling on gear forces a different solution for each rock face. | ch. 6
idea: A climbing gym's colour-coded holds are a rule that sets the experience | A beginner who uses every hold on the wall is playing freely but never meets the movement the route setter shaped. | ch. 6
idea: Some games only make sense after many playings | In 1830, a par value has to be set in the first round, but its meaning shows only when the player knows how stock and track interact. In Dream Quest, cards that look powerful early may lock the deck in. The author concludes reviewers who play a handful of times have not met such games. | ch. 6
idea: A game's community can be part of the game | Netrunner releases new cards monthly and drops older sets after two years, so the pool of play keeps shifting. Designers watch the meta and make new cards to break dominant strategies. The author says this makes the community part of what has to be encountered. | ch. 6
idea: Party games and stupid games are different categories | Cards Against Humanity is a party game where skill is unimportant, but success is the pleasure, so it is not a stupid game. Finger jousting is a stupid game where failure is the pleasure, yet it grows more entertaining as players gain skill, so it is not a party game. | ch. 6
idea: A designer cannot fix where the player looks but can tilt it | The general prescription only says to play and then attend to one's own activity, but the activity itself draws attention to where agency was most active. Climbers think about movement, not the taste in their mouth. | ch. 6
idea: In a game, each player appreciates something different | At a dance performance everyone looks at the same event. In a game each player attends to their own run through it, so no two players have quite the same object of appreciation. | ch. 7
idea: A first playing of a puzzle is its truest one | After a puzzle is solved, repeat plays are shadows of the first. The author uses this to show games do not all call for repeated exploration the way interactive computer art does. | ch. 7
idea: Narrowing solutions tightens the experience | A climbing route with tiny holds forces slow, precise, calm movement from almost everyone. The same is true of single-answer puzzles in adventure games. | ch. 7
idea: A designer can plan for different kinds of players | Magic's designers aim at one player wanting a feeling, one wanting self-expression and one wanting to prove skill, and try to make the game work when they meet. | ch. 7
idea: The same effect can be reached by shaping the roles | In Android: Netrunner, the corporation cannot attack and plays hidden cards; the hacker attacks and plays open cards. These differences produce a game of bluff on one side and deduction on the other. | ch. 7
idea: A designer can steer people without forcing them | In Alexander's A Pattern Language, a shared lounge placed between offices and the kitchen draws casual meetings, and a longer alternative path lets people avoid it when they want. The author uses this to compare a game designer with an urban planner. | ch. 7
idea: A game designer can set the goal itself | City planners and governments can set rules and incentives but can hardly set what people want. A game designer names the player's goal and abilities and expects the player to take them up. | ch. 7
idea: A tiny choice space can produce intense feeling | In The King Is Dead each player has eight cards and plays each once, all in the open. The author reports that players describe a slow cage fight and some feel close to sick, then ask to play again. | ch. 7
idea: Swapping goals quickly can itself be the game | BUTTON and WarioWare fire mini-games for a few seconds each. The player grabs one goal and drops it for another, and the laughter comes from the wrenching of agency. | ch. 7
term: Aesthetic responsibility | The question of who is credited for the qualities of a play experience, the designer who built the conditions or the player who acted in them. | ch. 7
term: Medium of sociality | The social relationships between players, which a multiplayer designer shapes by giving each player a goal, abilities and obstacles. | ch. 8
term: Cooperative game | A game where players share a goal and usually hold different complementary roles, such as Pandemic, Hanabi or Space Alert. | ch. 8
term: Asymmetric game | A game where each side has different rules and aims, so the sides in effect play different games. Root and Android: Netrunner are examples. | ch. 8
term: Morally transformative technology | The author's description of a game that turns an act that would hurt outside play, such as blocking or attacking, into an experience both players value. | ch. 8
term: Psychic offloading | Trusting the game and the match to turn a fierce contest into something useful for both, so a player need not keep the opponent's wellbeing in mind during play. | ch. 8
term: Magic circle | Huizinga's idea of a bounded space and time for play that is set apart from ordinary life, with its own rules. Later writers argue about how sealed it is. | ch. 8
term: Voluntarism about moral transformation | The view that play turns hostile acts into friendly ones only because players decide to read them that way. The author argues that game design does part of the work. | ch. 8
term: Social work | A work whose medium includes the social relationships among its participants. | ch. 8
term: Library of socialities | The idea that multiplayer games store many social arrangements, such as a team, shifting alliances or uneven powers, which players can step into and try. | ch. 8
term: Experiments in living | John Stuart Mill's term for small trials of other ways of life. The author calls games a quick rough-sketch version. | ch. 8
term: All-consuming instrumental attitude | Treating every resource and opponent as a tool for one aim, which many games ask of players for a short time. | ch. 9
term: Value clarity | The author's name for the three traits that make game goals easy to live by: they are obvious to apply, easy to compare on one scale and easy to rank. | ch. 9
term: Commensurable goals | Goals that can be measured on one common scale. A game with victory points gives each kind of achievement a stated rate, such as four points per sheep. | ch. 9
term: Subtle value game | A game with goals that are unclear to apply, plural, and hard to rank, such as informal skateboarding or improv comedy. | ch. 9
term: Gamification | Putting game elements such as points, badges and leaderboards into non-game activities in order to change motivation. | ch. 9
term: Value capture | What happens when a person with rich values meets a simplified, often numbered version of them, adopts it in place of the rich one, and finds their life has narrowed. | ch. 9
term: Perverse incentive | A reward that works against its makers' purpose, such as paying teachers for test scores and getting teaching to the test. It differs from value capture because the person's values do not change. | ch. 9
term: Gaming the system | Choosing actions to move an outside measure and not the thing it measures, such as picking easy courses to raise a grade average. | ch. 9
term: Indicator | A single number that stands for a complicated state of affairs and hides the negotiation behind it, such as the Human Development Index. | ch. 9
term: Heuristic drift | A shift in the daily rule of thumb a person uses for a value, caused by the pleasure of a clear measure, so the rule of thumb stops tracking the value. | ch. 9
term: Coarse-grained values | Values with fewer and blunter distinctions, which result when simple measures replace a person's finer vocabulary for what matters. | ch. 9
term: Sportsmanlike player | A person who plays hard during the game but cares afterwards only that everyone had an interesting match. The author uses this person to show the all-consuming attitude can be kept inside a game. | ch. 9
term: Victory points | A shared currency a game uses to convert different achievements into one score. | ch. 9
idea: Credit for a fine solution is shared between designer and player | In Portal a player can invent a way across a trench that the designers never planned. idea: Comedy can come from showing how fluid a player's goals are | In WarioWare and B.U.T.T.O.N. the player is told to want one thing for a few seconds and then another. The author compares this to books that joke about being books. | ch. 7

idea: A game can be made of social roles alone | In Werewolf a referee taps a few players as werewolves who know each other, while villagers know nothing. Each day all talk and vote to remove someone; each night the werewolves remove a villager. The whole game is built of who knows what. | ch. 8
idea: A game can teach what trust consists of | In Hanabi each player holds their cards facing outward so everyone sees them except the holder, and gives one limited hint per turn. idea: A game can only turn a contest into something valuable when skill, taste and design line up | Players need roughly equal skill, a game that suits their tastes, and a design that channels attacks into interesting problems. An insult contest or a whipping contest is a design that cannot do this for most people. | ch. 8
idea: The same people and same intentions can have different results under different rules | The author's playground bullies were harmless-feeling in basketball, where their efforts went into guarding and blocking, and hurtful in dodgeball, where the ball hit faces. idea: Asymmetric roles create a society | In Root, the Marquise de Cats builds and suppresses, the Eyrie programs a rigid plan that collapses if one step fails, and the Woodland Alliance starts with nothing and spreads sympathy. The uneven roles give uneasy alliances and mutual stomping. | ch. 8

idea: Game goals are unusually easy to read | The author lists three traits: it is obvious whether a goal was met, different goals are measured on one scale, and results can be ranked. Daily life gives none of these for goals like wisdom or a child's happiness. | ch. 9
idea: A game does not need a score | A Scoreless Super Mario would still be a game with goals and obstacles, but players could not rank runs. The author says most players want a score and choose one. | ch. 9

idea: A step counter can replace the thing it was bought for | A person wears a FitBit for health and ends up chasing step totals, and drops yoga and weights because the device does not count them. The author calls this value capture. | ch. 9
idea: Numbers travel well because they lose context | A written report on a student holds much detail but is hard to average. A grade is easy to move and sum into a grade average, yet it hides how it was reached and looks more objective than it is. | ch. 9
idea: Games can be used for self-help where the target is simple | Duolingo uses points and clear targets for learning a language, a goal that loses little when turned into a game. The author notes that the risk grows when the target is subtle, such as health or wisdom. | ch. 9
idea: A heuristic needs checking against what it stands for | Thirty minutes of exercise before breakfast is a rule of thumb for health. The author says a person steps back from time to time to ask whether the rule still serves that aim. | ch. 9
idea: Aesthetic judgement does not follow from rules | The author cites writers who hold that no description of a work lets anyone predict whether they will value it, and that every proposed principle, such as symmetry being beautiful, has been broken by a work people admire. This sets aesthetic reflection against the clear scoring of a game. | ch. 10
idea: Winning is only a road to the value in striving play | An achievement player counts more wins as more value. A striving player asks afterwards whether the struggle was worth it, and answers in terms that the game does not supply. | ch. 10
