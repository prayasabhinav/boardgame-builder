term: Board game experience profile | The set of scores a game receives on the paper's traits, shown as a bar graph so two games can be compared at a glance. Each trait is scored low, medium or high, and a trait that is absent is scored nil, which still counts as a score. | p. 21
term: Rule complexity | The length, difficulty and intricacy of a game's rules, which sets how much time and effort a player puts in before they can play. Mage Knight, with two 20-page rulebooks, is high, and Dixit, with a two-page rulebook, is low. | p. 12
term: Player reliance | How far a game needs input from the players that the game itself does not supply, such as conversation, a hint, a story or earned trust. A game where players choose from a set of options has low player reliance. | p. 13
term: Decisive randomness | Randomness that makes the decision for the player. Rolling a die to move in Snakes and Ladders is the example, since the die settles where the piece goes. | p. 14
term: Productive randomness | Randomness that hands the player a set of options to choose from. The paper's example is rolling three dice and choosing which one to use for the move. | p. 15
term: Theme synergy | How closely a game's theme, aesthetics and mechanics fit together. When it is high, taking the theme away leaves the mechanics without meaning, and when it is low the game would play much the same without it. | p. 16
term: Pasted-on theme | A theme added late in development that has almost no effect on how the game plays. | p. 16
term: Multiplayer solitaire | Games where players can do very little to each other through the rules, so each plays almost alone, and the scores are compared at the end. | p. 18
term: Petty diplomacy | In a competitive game for three players with interaction, the two trailing players always pull down the leader, which can produce a never-ending stalemate. Race games, where players can do little to each other, do not have this. | p. 18
term: Competitive and collaborative interaction | The paper scores interaction on two separate scales, one for competition and one for collaboration, instead of one scale running from one to the other. A game can be high on both, as in Tichu, where partners cooperate in order to compete against the other team. | p. 19
term: Semi-cooperative game | A game in which players work together for nearly the whole game, usually to stop a condition that makes everyone lose, but only one player wins at the end. Archipelago and CO2 are examples, and they tend to give each player a secret winning objective. | p. 20
term: Fully cooperative game | A game with no traitor mechanic where everybody wins or everybody loses, so every interaction is aimed at the shared result. Pandemic and ...and then we held hands are the examples. | p. 20
term: Specialist and maximalist profiles | A specialist game concentrates on a few traits, as The Resistance does on player reliance and interaction. A maximalist game pushes all its traits high, as Mage Knight Board Game does. | p. 24
idea: Four traits, each on a scale | The tool rests on four traits of a board game: rules, randomness, representational backdrop (theme) and interaction. Each runs along a continuum, scored low, medium or high, with finer marks such as medium-low or 0 to 10 allowed. | p. 12
idea: Seven scores in use | To classify a game, the tool has the user score it on seven differentiators: rule complexity, player reliance, decisive randomness, productive randomness, theme synergy, competitive interaction and collaborative interaction. | p. 20
idea: Choice is what makes luck feel like agency | The paper follows Knizia in saying that games of luck differ mainly in how much control the player has over the result. Choosing which bet to place is one thing that gives the player agency even when luck decides the outcome. | p. 14
idea: Luck and skill are not opposites | Following Garfield, the paper treats randomness as separate from skill, so a game can need a lot of skill and also depend a lot on luck. Skill comes from several things, such as rule complexity, player reliance, or the need to draw or sing. | p. 15
idea: Decisive randomness is usually softened | In modern board games decisive randomness rarely appears without some softening. When it is not softened it tends to be a global effect that hits all players equally, such as which resources Catan produces each turn. | p. 16
idea: A theme can be tested by taking it away | A game has high theme synergy if the experience is altered a lot when the theme is removed, so that fighting becomes a matter of who rolls higher. Miniatures, maps and rules written to evoke the theme often point to high synergy. | p. 17
idea: Eurogames play down theme | Games with low theme synergy are more common among Eurogames, which tend to use symbolic wooden pieces such as pawns and meeples so as to bring forward the mechanical side. | p. 18
idea: Competition and collaboration are not opposites | The paper calls the split between competitive and collaborative games a false dichotomy, as luck and skill are. In Diplomacy a collaborating ally can turn into a rival, and in Tichu partners cooperate to beat the other team. | p. 19
idea: Interaction in a two-player conflict | A two-player war simulation such as the Commands and Colors series is high on competitive interaction and has none on collaborative, because the two players are always competing and each move is planned to help one side or hurt the other. | p. 19
idea: Trading adds cooperation | A classic Euro-style game has medium competitive interaction, with few chances for direct attack. The paper says that adding a trading mechanic immediately raises the level of cooperative interaction, since fair trades almost always benefit both sides. | p. 20
idea: Multiplayer solitaire divides opinion | Two reviewers cited by the paper call Agricola multiplayer solitaire, and one calls that a design failure. The paper notes Agricola has held a top-ten place on BoardGameGeek for over half a decade, and that five of the top ten have strong multiplayer solitaire elements. | p. 19
idea: Eurogame and Ameritrash profiles differ | Agricola scores mostly medium, with no high extremes, while Descent: Journeys in the Dark is high on more than half its traits, with very high interaction on both scales and more theme synergy. The paper reads Ameritrash games as skewed to the extremes, especially in theme and interaction. | p. 22
idea: Similar games can be told apart | The tool can also separate two games of the same type. The Castles of Burgundy has the same moderated profile as Agricola but less theme synergy and more randomness, which the paper traces to its dice and less representative pieces. | p. 23
idea: Mass-market games pair a simple move with a loose overlay | Monopoly and Clue combine a simple roll-and-move mechanic with a loosely player-reliant one, trading in Monopoly and deduction in Clue. The paper says Exploding Kittens has a very similar profile to Monopoly. | p. 23
idea: A profile can show intended and actual experience | The author suggests designers can use the profile to sketch the experience they want for a prototype and, afterwards, to compare it with how players plot their own experience, so differences show up. | p. 25
idea: Scores are the author's judgement | The profiles in the paper come from the author's own analysis and are described as subjective, a first step towards a typology specific to board games. | p. 25
