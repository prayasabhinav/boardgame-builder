idea: Prizes separate players | Boyd held that greed and dishonesty are not part of competitive games themselves. They come in through prizes, which set able players apart from the rest and discourage the less able. In her playground programmes she used games for the play itself, with no reward at the end. | p. 9
idea: A game is a pretend situation | Boyd wrote in 1934 that play is always an artificial situation, while work is a real one. A game is set up in the imagination and defined by rules the players accept. A child who plays moves out of the real situation into the pretend one and acts as consistently in it as if it were real. | p. 25
idea: A game is a problem and its solution | For Boyd every game is set up as a problem, and playing the game is the same act as solving it. The pattern of the game gives the starting conditions for the problem. Acting steadily within the frame of the game is the same discipline as any careful problem solving. | p. 25
idea: A game copies social life in a small space | Boyd called a game a social pattern, a copy of how people are organised, held inside limits of time and space. Players take roles and deal with each other under rules, as people do outside it, and then the game comes to an end. | p. 25
idea: Rules and strategies come first | In Boyd's view, and in the mathematical theory of games, rules and strategies matter most. Each side picks a plan of behaviour beforehand, and what it meets depends on the plan the other side picked. This differs from everyday talk, where a game means a run of tricks and dishonest moves. | p. 25
idea: Outside rewards bend the rules | Boyd accepted competition as part of a game. She said outside prizes turn players towards getting round the rules, widen the gap between winner and loser, and push out the enjoyment and spontaneity of play. A game where players avoid the rules by dishonest moves is, to her, a struggle to dominate the others and not a game. | p. 26
idea: Rules decide what counts in the play world | Simon quotes Huizinga: every game has rules, and they decide what holds inside the world the game makes. The rules bind the players completely and leave no room for doubt. Play happens inside fixed limits of time and place, and the players feel it as different from ordinary life. | p. 24
idea: Undirected play stays thin | Boyd differed from writers who wanted children's play left alone. She thought play with no adult guidance stays meagre and limited. A leader who joins in, in mind as well as body, can steer play so that it teaches social skills. Her example was Edith, a girl of fourteen who spoke little, and who began to form sentences and join classroom exercises after directed play. | p. 23
idea: Groups grow by working on a shared problem | For Boyd, people form a group by acting together on something that matters to all of them. Organisation follows from that joint work. A problem that concerns the whole group is solved by the whole group, because deciding it only at the top leaves everyone else with no part in it. | p. 27
idea: Kinds of group relation run from loose to tight | Boyd listed kinds of group experience. At one end are people doing separate things side by side, who need almost no co-operation. At the other end are activities that need close co-operation, dependence on each other and joining of effort. Each kind came with its own sorts of social relation. | p. 27
idea: Leadership belongs to the situation | Boyd said leadership is a function and not a trait. Each member leads when they add something needed to the matter in hand, and the adult leader guides the process and stops an aggressive member from ruling the group. | p. 29
term: Strategy | A plan of behaviour chosen in advance for a game, which says what a player will do in each circumstance that may arise. What arises depends on the plans the other side has chosen. | p. 25
term: Pay-off | The advantage or loss that comes to a player out of a situation and its results. In Boyd's account it is part of the situation itself and does not need a separate prize. | p. 26
term: Integrated group | A group with a sense of belonging, shared effort, and joint and personal responsibility. Boyd held that not every group reaches this, and that a group needs shared work to get there. | p. 27
term: Federation of groups | Several groups working on joint programmes while each keeps its own identity and its right to decide for itself. Boyd saw this as the highest stage of group growth. | p. 27
term: Play leader | An adult who joins a group's play in mind and body in order to guide it. Boyd's leader steers the process and does not stop members from contributing. | p. 29
idea: Work is real and play is made up | Boyd said the difference between work and play is not enjoyment, because work can be enjoyed and play can turn into work. The difference is that a work situation is genuine and a play situation is artificial. A child who plays a game accepts its rules and its roles, and also helps to create and hold up the pretend situation. | p. 47
idea: Players take a game seriously because of the challenge | Boyd asked why a child works hard at something known to be only a game. She gave two causes: the nature of the situation and the child's attitude. The attitude is willingness to accept the problem the game sets, whether it asks for a skill, for beating an opponent or for a shared task. This willingness does away with merit points and badges as a spur. | p. 48
idea: A game squeezes a long life event into a short one | Boyd compared a game to a well-made drama. It removes what does not matter and puts events close together, so a typical long experience is packed into little time and space. Because of this, a child gets more and different experience from games than from ordinary days. | p. 48
idea: Folk games grow out of people, not out of adults' plans | Boyd said traditional games were not made by adults to amuse or teach children. They match body development and cultural patterns, and are looser than many real-life situations in how they are organised. She called them social patterns with a stamp of universality. | p. 48
idea: Taking turns teaches the move from wanting to rule-following | Boyd described growth from self-gratification to self-determinism. In the first, a baby gets what it wants when it wants it. In the second, a child acts by the rules the group made and helps enforce them on others and on self. A game packs this change into one unit, since a player who joins has to follow the rules. | p. 49
idea: A penalty is accepted when the cause is visible | In Two Deep, a tag game, a frail girl let herself be tagged again and again and had to keep running as the one chasing. After about twenty minutes she began to watch for the runner and got away in time. Boyd said that she took the penalty without complaint because the link from her failure to the result was close, and because the rules were applied to everyone the same way. | p. 49
idea: Behaviour barred outside is allowed in the game | Boyd noted that play lets a child act in ways that would be offensive in a genuine situation. A game cast list can hold thieves, robbers and kidnappers, yet the robbers only tag, the thieves only steal sticks and the witch carries off Monday while Sunday turns her back. The rules keep the danger small. | p. 50
idea: A game role is a part and not a character | Boyd separated the role in a game from the role in a play. In baseball the pitcher and catcher are roles, and in a fox and chickens game the fox and hen are roles, but players do not act in character. In Little Sally Waters, Sally stands, turns with eyes shut and points her finger, with none of the weeping the song describes. | p. 51
idea: Immediate results teach more than advice | Boyd wrote that a game is life in miniature, and that the play group is society in small. When a child takes more than a fair share of turns, the other children protest at once in terms the child follows. She argued that fairness is built through action with quick results and not by being told what is right. | p. 43
idea: Aggressive players learn to give up extra turns | Boyd saw active children who at first wanted every turn come, under steady leadership, to refuse more than their share and to ask for fairness for a younger or timid child. She held that this came out of playing in the group and could not be handed over as a rule to memorise. | p. 42
idea: Games without contact steady an unsettled group | A leader worked with youths aged fourteen to sixteen who found it hard to keep to a game. Contact games made them over-excited, so she chose target games between two teams and bowling, where each player takes a turn and adds points to the team score. With no contact and no interplay, they stayed in the game and forgot themselves. | p. 39
idea: Traditional games are an art form | Boyd put games and folk dances beside painting and music as patterns that express balance, proportion and harmony. She said time has kept the ones that drew a response from each new generation. She added that when music or ballet turned artificial, composers and dancers went back to folk forms. | p. 41
idea: The game and the leader's part both matter | Boyd held that the spirit of play matters and so does the content. For play to serve social education, the leader needs to join the group and stay one of its members, with the same share in the play as the children, and needs to know the games thoroughly. | p. 44
idea: Board games grow from daily events | Boyd noted that events such as the capture of slaves, a trip to market or a tribal fight may be the basis of board games like backgammon and chess. The story fades as the game passes between people, but the pattern of the social process stays, like city streets that look clearer from the air once the details are gone. | p. 63
idea: A close chain of cause and effect makes games teach | Because a game's events come in quick order inside small limits of time and space, Boyd said, cause and effect are plain to the player. The player picks up more than in daily life, much of it without being able to put it into words. A fielder who catches a fly ball behaves as if he had done the physics. | p. 64
idea: Teach a game as a whole | At the Lincoln school, games and dances were shown as wholes, with details left until the basic pattern was learned and enjoyed. Seventy boys learned basketball to official rules this way, while others failed when it was taught bit by bit. | p. 59
idea: Group size and age spread were found by trial | Bertha Schlotter found that groups whose mental and chronological ages differed by no more than about two years gave the closest social relations and play, and that twenty children was the largest group she could work with. | p. 59
idea: Games sort by what they ask of players | Boyd said games may be arranged on a scale by their main factors, such as special body skills, social organisation, co-operative interaction, team play and intellectual content. | p. 66
idea: A group makes its own rules when given the work | At the Geneva school, girls and a group worker planned a two-week recreation programme with no prizes, no cottage set against another and no special treatment for the well behaved. The groups became clubs, and they began to settle conduct problems among their own members. | p. 57
term: Artificial situation | In Boyd's account, the made-up setting of a game, set up by the players' acceptance of its rules and roles. She set it against the genuine situation of work. | p. 47
term: Genuine situation | An everyday, real-life setting where results are not pretend. Boyd used the term for work. | p. 47
term: Self-gratification | Getting what one wants when one wants it, as a baby does. Boyd saw games as the place where a child moves from this to following shared rules. | p. 49
term: Self-determinism | Acting by rules the group made, including rules one helped to make and applies to oneself. Boyd used the word for what a game builds in a child. | p. 49
term: Folk game | A traditional group game passed from one generation to the next, like other folkways. Boyd noted that many are taken up by other cultures. | p. 47
term: Role | A part in a game such as pitcher, catcher, fox or hen. Boyd said players take the part and act as themselves, which differs from acting a character in a play. | p. 51
term: Target game | A game where players aim at a mark and add their points to a team score, taking turns with no contact. Boyd's leader used them to settle an unsteady group. | p. 39
term: Social education | Boyd's name for what directed play and group life give a child: sharing in a group, taking on values, and learning to meet problems with others. | p. 44
term: Unverbalizable level | Boyd's phrase, taken from Korzybski, for learning that happens by doing and cannot be put into words. She said games give unusual experience at this level. | p. 64
idea: One game, play at different depths | Boyd compared a mechanic and an engineer working the same problem at different levels. In dominoes, a child may count only the result of the next move, while an older player works from all exposed pieces and weighs near and far results. The rules stay the same, and players of unlike ability can share one game. | p. 66
idea: A stable pattern holds varied players | When the basic pattern of a game stays fixed, Boyd noted, players of very different ability are held to a way of behaving that generations of players made. She held that this steadies the nervous system and helps social adjustment. | p. 66
idea: The game ends and its world dissolves | Boyd described playing as lifting oneself out of a genuine situation into an imagined one, keeping to the rules, playing the assigned role and working with the other players. When the game is over, the whole structure comes apart. She likened this to a cast on a stage, though in a game the players are the ones inside it. | p. 67
idea: Competition belongs to some games and not others | Boyd accepted competition because many games contain it. In her account a game is a problem, and winning is the last step in solving it and not the chief pleasure. She added that swimming, skiing and skating are not competitive and lose value when made so. | p. 69
idea: Each replay is a new problem | Even with the same opponents, each play of a game forms a new arrangement, Boyd observed. That makes it a new problem and calls for a new series of moves. | p. 69
idea: Stakes and publicity pull interest away from the problem | Boyd said prizes, cheering and publicity turn players towards the extras and away from solving the game. She used gambling as the extreme case, since a gambler wants the opponent's money and not the game. Replacing stakes with points and publicity, she asked, may kill interest in the game in the same way. | p. 69
idea: Games have a design that can be badly made | Boyd called traditional games and dances structural art designs. She watched a teacher join sound traditional dance figures into a new folk game, and the dancers could not pass easily from one figure to the next. Parts that worked alone did not add up to a whole that worked, and she found out the game had been invented by a teacher. | p. 69
idea: Play with others becomes more than rudimentary | Boyd pointed to children raised among animals, whose play stayed crude and copied the animals, such as running on all fours. She took this to mean that play grows through association in groups. | p. 80
idea: A well-formed game is not play unless spirit is present | Boyd held that children can be kept busy by a game of sound form and still not be playing. For her, play needs spontaneity and exhilaration shown within the rules and in solving the game's problem. Excitement is not the same as exhilaration, and activity is not the same as spontaneity. | p. 83
idea: Striving for excellence costs spontaneity | Boyd said learning often means trying to do what one cannot yet do, and putting technique before the activity. Aiming at excellence in play shifts interest to the self and raises competition. She also granted that players may not be joyous while learning a complex game. | p. 84
idea: Play has its own rules for rough behaviour | Boyd wrote that in play a person may be rough, noisy and unconventional, but that play has its own social controls, code and rules. These are taken on freely, can be enforced, and keep a balance between personal freedom and what the culture demands. | p. 85
idea: Play patterns keep the customs of a culture | Boyd observed children playing family life, shop-keeping, funerals and school in line with the customs around them. In the same play they show courage, bear pain, respect others' rights and keep to the rules. | p. 85
idea: The player helps make the role and the activity | Boyd described the player as creating a role while helping to create the activity. This gives the player control over themselves as a player, so what they do or fail to do is their own responsibility. | p. 87
idea: What a game does for a player depends on several things | Boyd listed what shapes the value of a play activity: the age, earlier play, health and handicaps of the player; the choice of activity and the leadership; the personality of the teacher; and how fully the teacher joins in. | p. 87
idea: Cheating spoils the game for everyone | Boyd described girls who worked out a group feeling in hide and seek. Cheating started only after they had enjoyed keeping to the rules, and when it did, the game was spoiled so suddenly that the girls protested against the spoiling and not the cheating itself. They then asked for equal, impartial treatment for all. | p. 90
idea: Players object to turns going to favourites | Boyd gave the case of a child with standing among the others who is chosen again and again for the most wanted roles, when players pick their own successor. Children outside the favoured group objected that it is not fair to always choose only friends. She read this as children forming rules from play. | p. 91
idea: Rules that block every mistake block learning | Boyd argued that organisations which make rules to prevent errors stop children learning what not to do as well as what to do. A child needs to act, take the result and judge it to build judgement. | p. 92
idea: A folk dance is played for the dancers | Boyd separated folk dances from ceremonial dances, done to influence fate, and from stage dances, done to influence an audience. Folk dances are done for the pleasure they give to the dancers themselves. | p. 67
term: Competition | A contest between players, which Boyd treated as part of many games. In her account it has no jealousy or injustice, and winning is the final step in solving the game's problem. | p. 68
term: Gambling | Playing a game to win the opponent's money. Boyd said the gambler cares about the stakes and not about the game itself. | p. 69
term: Play behaviour | Boyd's name for conduct that has nothing to do with routine living and is done for its own immediate satisfaction. Games, dances, drama and sports are patterns produced from it. | p. 81
term: Spontaneity | In Boyd's use, free action by the whole person in response to a situation, where meaning and impulse come together. She set it apart from the rush of a mob, which gives no room to think. | p. 83
term: Exhilaration | A lift of feeling that Boyd said play needs in order to be play. It differs from plain excitement. | p. 83
term: Intrinsic satisfaction | The pleasure a player takes in playing itself, with no reward from outside. Boyd's definition of play rests on it. | p. 79
term: Transformation | Boyd's word for how values and habits from play carry into wider life. She described a two-way flow, where children turn life into play and turn play back into life, with no direct copying of one behaviour. | p. 75
term: Folk dance | A dance made by common people and danced for the dancers' own pleasure. Boyd called it close kin to the game. | p. 67
idea: Winning stressed too hard ends the play | Boyd gave the case of school baseball teams that won and were then beaten up by the losers. She said that when winning is stressed too much, the play is killed, and adults then try to bring it back by authority or by preaching about sportsmanship. | p. 106
idea: Praise for one player spoils the play for others | Boyd held that singling out a child with a high level of skill can raise jealousy in the others where left alone it might have raised emulation. The praised child may start to show off, and everyone is pulled away from the purpose of the activity. | p. 106
idea: A play that pretends to be enjoyable is not play | Boyd wrote that when the show of enjoyment is played up, the natural life of play dies. She said players who force a game, which she called farced play, are failing to get into the game in their minds. She advised choosing activities that hold interest and stretch the players without overstretching them. | p. 107
idea: Games with a catch lose interest once the catch is known | Boyd ranked traditional games, whose content keeps drawing players back, above games that make one player the butt of a joke, and above games with a catch that ends interest once found. | p. 107
idea: Play lasts as long as the challenge holds | Boyd said play depends on interest that comes from seeing the problem and accepting the challenge. Once the thing to be made, the act of skill, the opponent or the group task no longer holds the whole challenge, the attitude of play ends. A player who withdraws in mind while keeping to the letter of the rules has stopped playing. | p. 108
idea: A game shows a player's own faults at once | In play, Boyd noted, cause and effect sit so close in time and space, and other players answer so quickly, that a player's shortcomings show up straight away. The same closeness gives a reason to overcome them. | p. 109
idea: Dance, game and drama each hold a player in a role | Boyd wrote that in a dance the player follows the design to the close, in a game the player obeys the rules and plays a role in relation to the other roles, and in a drama the player acts within the frame of the play. When it is over, the player steps out of the role. | p. 109
idea: Familiar structure frees the player | A player new to a form of play, Boyd observed, is mostly busy working out its structure. Once the structure is established, less attention goes to it and the player is set free to be creative. This happens only when a leader does not press structure and technique too hard. | p. 110
idea: Aimless excitement is not play | Boyd separated play from purposeless impulse, where incidents have no meaningful sequence. A game, with its definite frame, needs closer adherence to events, more continuity and more self-discipline than free capricious play. She added that within the frame each player can still play a role in their own way. | p. 111
idea: Telling a player what to do defeats purpose | Boyd said a child's purpose in play can be defeated by interruption, by being told what to do and how, or by help given when persistence would have got the child through. She held that the child wants to be steered and does not want to be handed the answer. | p. 111
idea: Solving the problem is what matters to the solver | Boyd held that if problem solving were only getting the answer, it would be of little value. The process has value, and effort counts even when the aim is not reached. Having made one's full effort is satisfying even without success. | p. 112
idea: Prizes redirect effort from the problem | Boyd asked whether awards, emblems and honour rolls turn children's effort to solving the problem or to winning the award. She said those with little chance of success respond badly, and those with ability lose interest in the process itself. | p. 113
idea: Thinking about the play kills the play | Boyd said that in true play the player does not look inward at personal gains. A child who claps and cries out at a successful move is expressing the end of a successful play and is not stepping back to judge. In her view, making players aware of what play does for them destroys the spirit of play. | p. 113
idea: Repeating a game builds skill and adaptability | A child delights in playing something over and over, Boyd noted, and the playing is never twice alike though the general pattern is the same. Repetition makes the child more skilful and not more regimented. She held that skill matters most when it grows through use. | p. 114
idea: Regimentation and spontaneity differ | Boyd defined regimentation as telling players how to play and how to solve the problem. Spontaneity is leaving them to find their own way inside the frame. She warned that yelling and over-excitement under regimentation are taken by adults as a sign of enjoyment and are not. | p. 115
idea: Self-made play can drift to a side matter | Boyd saw children playing house or a similar game build up a main purpose and then be carried off by a smaller incident that pleased them, carrying on as if it were the main purpose. She called this a creative adventure that is not logical. | p. 117
idea: Co-operation sits inside competition | Boyd held that in dances, drama and games each participant takes the role needed for the whole activity to go on, so that stronger dancers help weaker ones and actors give fellow actors openings. Players come to see co-operation as necessary even where competition is one of the factors. | p. 117
idea: Emulation needs a playful signal | Boyd said emulation starts from a stimulus pattern that is itself playful. In her example, a teacher ended a demonstration with French nursery children by saying 'you can't catch me' and running round the court, and the whole group followed laughing. A plain spoken suggestion to run would not have done the same. | p. 118
idea: Emulating differs from copying | Boyd compared two play leaders. One taught rope-jumping patterns step by step to girls in a line, who copied them. The other sang a song while playing with a ball in time, handed out balls and let the children make up their own plays. The second gave room for original play and the first gave only imitation. | p. 120
idea: Competition needs nothing at stake | Boyd noted that competition runs through every non-musical game, even among small children, as in hide the thimble. She defined it as seeking to gain what another is trying to gain at the same time, with no ill will, and with nothing material at stake. | p. 121
idea: A frame of reference leaves room inside it | Boyd saw children dramatising stories or playing house hold to the general pattern while each plays a role in their own way, as long as the requirements of the pattern are met. | p. 111
term: Farced play | Boyd's term for play pushed or forced, such as a game acted out without the players getting into it. She read it as a sign of limited ability to enter the game in mind. | p. 107
term: Regimentation | Telling players how to play, how to solve the problem and how to act as they play. Boyd set it against spontaneity. | p. 115
term: Emulation | Doing an act in response to seeing another do it, with the aim of equalling or beating it. Boyd said it works when the model is playful and within the learner's reach, and differs from copying. | p. 116
term: Frame of reference | The general pattern and requirements that a play activity sets, such as a game's rules and purpose. Boyd said players can act in their own way inside it. | p. 111
term: Build-up | Boyd's word for correcting behaviour by feeding the whole person with developing experience, in place of fixing the fault or punishing it. | p. 102
term: Introspection | Looking inward to judge one's own play while it goes on. Boyd said it breaks the spirit of play. | p. 114
term: Stimulus pattern | An example of play, such as a rope-jumping rhyme or a challenge to chase, that sets off a matching response in other players. | p. 118
idea: Competition and emulation differ | Boyd said both challenge a player by another's shown ability. In a game, competition is move by move, each player or team trying to gain an edge on the other. Emulation is a striving to equal another's skill. Competition used as the only motive is one thing, and competition beside co-operation, empathy and emulation is another. | p. 122
idea: Competition and winning are two different things | Boyd wrote that competition is the process of one player outwitting another, while winning is centred on the finish. Someone has to win, but a team or person who has enjoyed play without stress on winning is unlikely to be ruled by winning. | p. 124
idea: Competition added to non-competitive activities | Boyd held that putting contests into skating, swimming, drama or music tends to commercialise them. She also held that a programme with no competition at all removes emulation, strategy and the chance to make judgements and act on them. | p. 124
idea: Prizes broke up a card group | A small group of men and women met at a public recreation centre to play cards, and word of their enjoyment grew it to over fifty. The contest inside the games had only added to the pleasure until prizes came in. Then people criticised the differences in prize value, winning replaced playing together, and the group broke up, including friendships between neighbours. | p. 125
idea: Choosing players for roles by contest has costs | Boyd gave the case of two girls who competed for the lead in a school play. The role went to the daughter of a prominent family, and many thought this was unjust. The girl who lost lost faith in honest effort and drifted, and Boyd said she might have done well in another activity had she not met the competitive selection. | p. 125
idea: Knocking down what a child builds teaches nothing | Boyd said a child who builds a house and has it knocked down does not learn to build sturdier houses. A child can redo a building that fails to satisfy, but destroying it is not the same. | p. 126
idea: Building blocks teach cause and effect | A child who stacks blocks, finds they fall, and tries again until they stand is dealing with mechanical stress. Boyd said this is intelligent mental behaviour, whether or not it leads to planning a bridge on paper. She added that play materials ought not to set too many problems at once. | p. 126
idea: Things cannot answer a child's treatment | Toys and objects cannot react to how the child treats them, Boyd noted. Play with things alone reveals a hidden problem but does not solve it, and equipment play stays closer to reflex action unless a dramatic form such as a jump-rope rhyme is joined to it. | p. 126
idea: Made-up play needs a defined frame to teach discipline | Boyd said imaginative play lacks one thing that organised games and dramatised stories have, which is the discipline of solving problems inside a defined frame. In playing house the frame is only loosely given, and each player acts with general reference to the task. | p. 127
idea: A leader organised a room into a small town | About fifteen girls began playing house on chalked squares on the floor. The leader gave them paper and scissors for dishes and cut paper babies for the mothers. When the children wanted something to do, she added a school, then a factory with a time schedule struck on the piano, then a grocery and a coal peddler for boys. She insisted that orders be remembered exactly, wrong orders be refused and delivery mistakes be put right, which gave the trade a dignity the children liked. | p. 128
idea: Each part of a pretend town needs the others | In the same play, dishes from the factory had to be needed by the mothers, the school had to teach the youngest and set work for older ones, and the store had to take orders that the mothers had to place and remember. The leader kept every group tied to the others and to the whole. | p. 129
idea: A big group of children is like a heap of building material | Boyd wrote that a large group of children has to be organised into a house or be wasted. Where the Polish children's resources were thin, the units could not have kept going without the worker's organising. At around fifty children the worker could go from group to group as a visitor, and above sixty the overflow was given separate games in the corridor. | p. 129
idea: Play changes experience and does not copy it | Boyd said a child who plays at a train journey is transforming it by selecting and shortening events from a long span of time into a close sequence. Fifteen-month-old Paul built a train from chairs after a trip. Boyd said it is a misconception to call this imitation. A copied game like Patty Cake, she said, is not transformation. | p. 132
idea: A prompt helps while the child supplies the content | Boyd's example had Paul laying out playing cards in a broken line, and a neighbour running her fingers along them saying 'here comes a train'. Paul then made trains with the cards in several rooms. She held that a prompt, given when imagination is ready, can start play while the child puts in his own content. | p. 132
idea: Play can run away from its players | Girls of eight and nine playing house made their dolls sick, shared news of worsening illness and drew the shades, until they enjoyed the worry no longer and could not leave the illusion. The play leader announced herself as a doctor, visited each home, and the babies recovered and the shades went up. | p. 135
idea: A leader plays two parts | Boyd wrote that the worker often plays both player and teacher. The worker keeps the frame of reference intact by objecting when a player acts against it, and does so only when no other player does, since players learn from enforcing the rules on each other as well as obeying them. | p. 138
idea: Dictated play kills empathy | Boyd held that a coach who tells players exactly what to do, in place of letting them act from their own sense of the situation, defeats their own intelligence. She said this makes a player dependent and not adventurous or experimental. | p. 139
idea: The same party made two ways builds two groups | Boyd compared two girls' clubs planning a valentine party. In the first, all talk over the programme, ideas clash and adjust, and all make the decorations together. In the second, motions are put, committees are appointed and each does a part. The first draws members together and the second separates them, though the parties look alike. | p. 147
idea: Groups run from separate activity to shared interest in each other | Boyd described a scale. At one end each member acts mostly alone with little interest in the others, and at the other end members care about one another as persons. Groups held together mainly by a programme break up when the activities stop interesting them. Groups whose interest is in each other last longer and make new programmes. | p. 146
idea: Conflict inside shared work builds the group | Boyd wrote that when members work on a project for shared satisfaction, differences and conflicts come out and tend to resolve. Free expression of felt difference is the only way to reach consensus. She added that some incompatibilities show up as well, and the group steadies as some members draw closer and others drop out. | p. 145
idea: Play patterns turn out different from their first use | A group formed for one purpose often grows closeness for another, Boyd observed, as with a clothing workers' union that built a recreation programme, and a sewing class whose members refused any new member. | p. 144
term: Mutual-participant group | Boyd's name for a group where members take part together in feeling, thinking and acting on interests they share. | p. 143
term: Psychological intimacy | The close sharing among members of a small group that Boyd saw as the core of group experience. She said arts and play create it as well as express it. | p. 143
term: Individuation | In Boyd's scale of groups, the end where each member acts largely alone and has little interest in the others. | p. 146
term: Collectivism | In Boyd's scale of groups, the end where members are interested mainly in one another as persons and not only in the activity. | p. 146
term: Consensus | Agreement reached by members freely stating their differences. Boyd held this is the only way to it. | p. 145
term: Synthesizing medium | A game, story or similar form that pulls scattered impulses and experiences into one organised whole for the player. | p. 132
term: Ideational organisation | A way of organising pretend play by adding ideas that fit within a concept, such as a school or factory in a town. Boyd set it against organising by a leader's authority. | p. 127
idea: Rules start still and become active when played | Boyd defined a game as a problem situation set up for a time in the imagination, with playing as the way the problem is solved. In checkers, two players accept the fixed rules, which become active as soon as they are acted on. They move pieces in turn, always in relation to the rules, to each other's changing positions and to the final aim. | p. 162
idea: A game and a physics problem use the same working method | Boyd said the way a player behaves in a game is identical to thinking inside a frame of reference in physics or philosophy, though the game's behaviour is of a different order. She held that highly organised games are problems whose solution keeps behaviour in a cause-and-effect order. | p. 162
idea: The game, not the leader, steers behaviour | A play leader showed sixty restless boys and girls aged eight to sixteen how a game controls behaviour. She gathered a few in a circle for a tiptoeing game in which one player stands with eyes shut in the centre and another tries to touch him unheard. The others joined, and the group kept the silence rule for twenty-two minutes in a row. | p. 162
idea: Players still differ inside a fixed frame | In the same silence game, nothing in the rules stopped one boy crawling to the centre on hands and knees or another taking off his shoes. Boyd said that behaviour in a game is always relative to the frame of reference, and that this leaves room for different ways of solving the problem. | p. 163
idea: A game's frame is given and a club's frame is made | Boyd contrasted a game, whose pattern is already fixed, with a club, whose frame its members have to create together and keep creating. A club both makes its own social frame and acts in relation to it, which she called self-control in the highest sense. | p. 164
idea: A ready-made answer removes the challenge | A principal told a club of boys, who had asked whether to give a member who seldom came another chance, that people always get another chance in her school. Boyd said that with the answer supplied, the boys had no reason to go back and work it out together, and no real thinking was needed. | p. 150
idea: Badges and special privileges work against the group | Boyd argued that a place trying to build integrated groups cannot give privileges or insignia to members rated well behaved, or punish those rated badly behaved. She said both prevent the development of the kind of group where they could grow. | p. 153
idea: Games make problems that have set limits | Boyd contrasted mathematical problems, which can be stated exactly and solved by formal logic to one correct answer, with social problems, which deal with values in continuous change and call for continuous experimentation. A game sets a problem inside a stated frame, so it can be solved and finished. | p. 179
term: Problem situation | Boyd's description of a game: a situation set up for a time in the imagination, which players solve by playing. | p. 161
idea: Players agree to a contract before the game starts | Boyd said every game has a pattern (Cat and Rat, baseball), fixed rules for that game, and cultural rules that are taken for granted, such as not hurting each other and not giving the game to the opponent. Accepting these is something like a gentleman's agreement. Inside it, players create and solve the game's problem as it changes move by move. | p. 202
idea: An error in play does not stay on a record | Boyd noted that when a player fails to keep the agreement, the others react at once and everyone, including the one at fault, understands why. The mistake is not written down on a report card, the game goes on like a merry-go-round, and the momentum of the group carries the player along. | p. 202
idea: Activities run on a scale from watching to joint clubs | Boyd set out six steps of social relation with examples. First is the auditor-spectator, as in theatre or watching sport. Second is individual taking part within a group activity, as in crafts or skating. Third is casual social contact, as in quoits, card and board games and tennis. Fourth is intensive interaction, as in athletic and social group games, country dancing and drama. Fifth is long-term association in an integrated club, and sixth is a federation of clubs. | p. 203
idea: Card and board games sit low on the scale of co-operation | In Boyd's scale, equipment games such as cards, board games and tennis give only casual social association. Activities in the third and fourth steps need co-operation only while the activity lasts, and young people can stay at the lower steps for years with little concern for others or for building the programme. | p. 204
idea: Games differ in what they exercise | The type of game situation shapes what a player uses, Boyd noted. Pitching horseshoes is mostly physical, chess is mostly mental, and square dancing is mostly social. In each, the whole person is to some degree active. | p. 195
idea: Start from what the players already do | At Manteno State Hospital, a leader working with severely withdrawn women reasoned that since they could walk they could move to music, and since they threw chairs about the ward they might throw and catch a volleyball. Many did not respond for months, and one held out nearly two years, but taking part rose from five or six out of fifty to between half and nearly all. | p. 193
idea: Stable groups help each other | At Manteno, the same patients stayed in the same group with the same workers. In an evening class, one couple could not follow a slightly complex dance figure, and the other couples in turn, without any word from the worker, took them through it. | p. 195
idea: Simple activities were chosen for a reason | For patients who functioned at a very low level, the Manteno workers kept the problems in the activities easy by choosing activities that are easy to carry out. Boyd said the method still differed from giving exercise or entertainment, since the players were solving problems inside a frame. | p. 195
idea: Competition in the wrong place keeps out those who need the group | Boyd held that competition on the gym floor between friendly groups is a legitimate part of the game, but that adding competition into other activities, usually by an agency, discourages and drives out the people who most need to take part. | p. 206
idea: The leader's impartiality sets the tone | Boyd wrote that a leader's partiality towards some members makes others unfriendly or jealous. Where the leader is impartial, the more capable members tend to set patterns that the less capable follow, with neither side aware of it. | p. 213
term: Play versus recreation | Boyd's distinction. Play needs self-started, spontaneous activity, even in its most passive forms, while recreation may need no more than passive reception of entertainment produced by others. | p. 194
term: Auditor-spectator participation | The first step on Boyd's scale of social relations, in which people listen to or watch an activity, as in theatre, radio or watching a sport. | p. 203
term: Individuated participation | The second step on Boyd's scale, in which a person takes part in a group activity somewhat alone, as in crafts, skating or playing an instrument. | p. 203
term: Casual social association | The third step on Boyd's scale, covering activities such as group singing and equipment games like quoits, cards, board games and tennis. | p. 203
term: Intensive interaction | The fourth step on Boyd's scale, covering group games, country dancing and dramatics, where co-operation is needed while the activity lasts. | p. 203
idea: A leader brings out a game without changing it | Boyd compared a play leader to an orchestra conductor, who gives a score a different reading without changing the composition. In the same way a play leader brings out values in a game without altering its structure. | p. 224
idea: A leader learns games by playing them | Boyd said a play leader needs a working knowledge of hundreds of games of many structures, learned by playing and not from a textbook. A leader picks a programme to challenge the players in relation to their developing abilities. | p. 230
idea: Some games need only one other player | Play that is a group activity stays thin without several players, Boyd noted, but in solitaire, checkers and other card and board games, one or another player is enough. | p. 229
idea: A slow game loses interest when the high point is far off | Boyd gave I Spy as an example of a game played at slow tempo, where nothing happens to hold attention and the climax is long delayed. The leader may change the game, or keep going and make finishing it something of a challenge, which she called jollying. | p. 233
idea: Players who do not try moves can find strategy by being beaten | Boyd considered a group of players who neither experiment nor plan their moves. Her answer was for the leader to join as an ordinary player, play strategically without explaining, and win. Players then tend to gain insight one by one, and she said teaching strategy takes away the satisfaction of trying one's own ability. | p. 233
idea: A simple game can start a player who is not ready | Players need not wait until they can enter a game's imaginative side in full, Boyd noted. They can start with simple games such as throwing at a mark, where success comes easily. Severely withdrawn patients who threw beanbags at random at a hole in a board, over several weeks, began to aim, to smile at a hit and to try again after a miss. | p. 234
idea: Start the game with little instruction | Boyd advised giving only a rough outline of a game and some demonstration, then starting at once and adding details as they matter. She held that stressing structure over play in the teaching of games kills the play for children and teacher. | p. 236
idea: Teaching Cat and Rat by gesture | Boyd's way into Cat and Rat with small children had the teacher place the Cat outside a circle and the Rat inside, tell the children to hold hands as a fence and keep the cat out, and then signal at once to let the rat out and shut the gate if the cat got in. She noted that the urge to watch a fleeing object brought even young children in quickly. | p. 236
idea: A few players can hold the rules for everyone | Only a bare outline of a game's frame of reference, Boyd said, needs to be carried in mind by enough players to maintain the structure. She held that a game keeps running, because at least some of the group take on keeping its structure, pattern and rules. | p. 236
idea: Complicated card and board games can be taught in stages | Boyd said many games can be taught only stage by stage, including some card and board games as well as active ones. Once players grasp the pattern, the leader gives rules, penalties and scoring when a need arises. | p. 237
idea: A fast game with changing roles gathers a scattered group | To draw unruly children into play, Boyd advised starting a popular game with no fuss about those who hold back. In her example a leader played catch with one girl, another joined, and a dodgeball game with no sides chosen began with nearly all taking part. The game ran with quick tempo, quick change of roles and no stops, and the two role players in the centre were the focus of interest. | p. 238
idea: A game has to match what the group can do | Boyd wrote that a group activity needing co-operation depends on a right estimate of what the group has in common. If a leader guesses wrong, a group may play politely, stay withdrawn if the game is beyond them, drift away or break up. | p. 222
idea: Choosing games by the behaviour they call for | Boyd advised matching the behaviour that is easily evoked at a given age, then choosing traditional games that use it and fit the group's maturity, the place and the occasion. She added that the choice gives players as large a variety of games as is consistent with their value to the group. | p. 235
idea: Roles can carry a timid player | Boyd held that a leader who plays within a game can assign roles at the right moment and encourage daring or concentration. A timid child caught up in concerted action may act with confidence, and repeated across games this can build a pattern of courage. | p. 226
idea: Real play depends on imagination being kept up | Boyd said players who cannot set up and keep imagination going are not playing, even if they follow the pattern of the game. If a player drops out in the middle of a swift game, a blunder or a missed chance can follow, as an actor out of character forgets lines. | p. 232
term: Common denominator | In Boyd's use, what a group can agree on and do together. Games, folk dances and clubs that need co-operation depend on a right estimate of it. | p. 222
term: Jollying | Boyd's word for cheering players on, in the spirit of play, to make finishing a slow game something of a challenge. | p. 233
term: Cat and Rat | A circle game used by Boyd as a teaching example. A Cat stands outside a ring of children holding hands, a Rat stands inside, and the children try to keep the Cat out. | p. 236
term: Slow tempo game | A game, such as I Spy, in which little happens to hold interest and the climax comes late. Boyd saw it as a risk to the spirit of play. | p. 233
term: Dodgeball | A ball game that Boyd described being played with no sides chosen, at quick tempo and with players changing roles often. | p. 238
idea: Prizes grew in value and so did the rivalry | Boyd described a club that added prizes to its meetings to liven them, against its leader's advice. As the prizes rose in value, rivalry rose with them and criticism of the members who bought the prizes began. The social events turned bitter enough to threaten the club. | p. 248
