pair: bidding+hidden-hands | Each player bids after seeing only their own cards, so a bid is a promise made partly in the dark. | A bid is a plain number of tricks, and no suit is named. | The high bidder names trumps only by leading a suit, so the choice stays unknown until the opening lead. | A bid built on hoped-for cards, not cards held, tends to fail. | A bidder who fails pays every other player. | under 'Napoleon' and 'Auction Pinochle'
pair: bidding+skill-with-luck-tempering | A bid counts the tricks a player can force, then leaves room for luck in a spare hand that others cannot see. | A bidder can reckon an outside ace, or a king and queen together, as one trick. | The bidder can count on the spare hand adding a little. | A bid that relies on one particular card in the spare hand tends to fail. | Each player has only one chance to bid, so bids have to be stretched to reach six or seven. | under 'Five Hundred' and 'Auction Pinochle'
pair: bidding+team-vs-team | When partners share one score, one player's bid puts the points of the whole side at stake. | Partners win points together when the bid is made. | Every player gets a say in the bidding before play. | One partner's misjudged bid costs both of them points. | After a bidding mistake, the rules can bar the partner from bidding. | under 'Partnership Auction Pinochle' and 'Auction Forty-Five'
pair: bluffing+trick-taking | A call that makes the other player doubt your hand can win the choice of trump without the cards to back it up. | A hand with few high cards can sometimes push a rival into giving up the deal. | A schmeiss can be tried on a hand not strong enough for a sound take. | The bluff can leave the caller making trump against a rival holding the top cards. | It works only when used rarely. | under 'Klaberjass'
pair: chance-with-mitigation+trick-taking | A player who dislikes the proposed trump suit can pass the choice on, which softens an unhelpful deal. | A player is not stuck with a first hand that does not suit. | The dealer gets a say in whether the cards are dealt again. | Refusing means more cards are dealt and there is a new hand to judge. | A refused suit may come back and be thrown out again. | under 'Seven-Up'

pair: deduction+trick-taking | Once the face-down stock is gone, a player who followed earlier plays can work out the opponent's hand. | The last tricks become a puzzle in which every card is accounted for. | Memory of earlier plays pays off in the final tricks. | Counting every card is a heavy task, so many players track only trumps and aces. | Almost always the hand with more trumps takes the last trick. | under 'Two-Hand Pinochle'
pair: direct-confrontation+trick-taking | When one player takes a contract and the other two play against that player, every trick becomes a contest between one and two. | The two other players join in a temporary partnership against the contractor. | A contractor who is set loses the value of his bid from his score. | The two opponents may not pass hints, so they work together only through the cards they play. | The contract holder is chosen by the bid, and the others cannot pick a different target. | under 'Five Hundred' and 'Rauber Skat'
